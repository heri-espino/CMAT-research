# Gneezy, Meier & Rey-Biel (2011) — *When and Why Incentives (Don't) Work to Modify Behavior*

## Citation

Gneezy, U., Meier, S., & Rey-Biel, P. (2011). When and why incentives (don't) work to modify behavior. *Journal of Economic Perspectives, 25*(4), 191–210. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.25.4.191

## 60-second summary

This is the main mechanism/review anchor for why an incentive can change behaviour while it is active yet fail to produce the same behaviour after removal. The paper distinguishes the standard direct price effect of incentives from indirect psychological effects such as information/signalling and crowd-out of other motivations. It explicitly treats the period **after incentives are removed** as scientifically distinct from the period in which they operate.

For Paper 1, this is useful because PPA can be framed as an incentive context that is present in MU and generally absent later. The relevant empirical question is therefore not whether incentives "work" in the abstract, but whether support use persists, decays, or changes after that context changes.

## Role in Paper 1

- **Priority:** core theory/mechanism anchor.
- **Used in:** Introduction, hypotheses/competing explanations, Discussion.
- **Main job:** motivate persistence versus decay/crowd-out as competing interpretations after incentive removal.
- **CMAT connection:** helps explain why later CMAT use after MU is informative, while not implying that PPA caused later behaviour.

## Design and method

- **Study type:** narrative/theoretical review of economic and psychological evidence.
- **Inferential statistics:** not applicable as a primary study.
- **Main conceptual distinction:** direct incentive/price effect versus indirect psychological effects.
- **Long-run definition used by the authors:** behaviour after the incentive is removed.
- **Domains reviewed:** education, public-good contribution, and lifestyle/health behaviour.

## Key evidence synthesized by the paper

- Incentives can raise the targeted behaviour in the short run while weakening other motivations.
- Small incentives can sometimes backfire because the incentive itself changes how the task is interpreted.
- Post-removal behaviour can remain changed because the incentive may alter beliefs, norms, or motivation.
- The review also stresses the opposite possibility: incentives may sometimes initiate behaviours that persist because the person experiences benefits or develops useful routines.
- For education specifically, the review concludes that incentives tend to work more consistently for concrete behaviours such as attendance/enrollment than for effort or achievement, and that effects differ across students.

### Quantitative examples reported from reviewed studies

These are **not pooled estimates from Gneezy et al.**; they are examples the review cites:

- PACES voucher winners in Colombia were reported as about **10% more likely to finish eighth grade** and about **0.2 SD higher** on achievement tests.
- PROGRESA is described as producing improvements in entry/progression/dropout-related outcomes under attendance-contingent transfers.

Do not treat those values as estimates from a single Gneezy et al. model.

## What this paper lets us say

- Post-incentive behaviour is a distinct outcome that should be measured rather than assumed.
- Incentives may produce persistence, decay, or crowd-out depending on mechanism and context.
- Effects on concrete participation behaviours can differ from effects on academic achievement.
- Heterogeneous response is expected rather than exceptional.

## What it does **not** let us say

- PPA crowded out intrinsic motivation among CMAT users.
- Later CMAT persistence proves habit formation.
- A three-visit threshold has the same behavioural meaning as a monetary reward.
- The CMAT study identifies the causal effect of introducing or removing PPA.
