# Leuven, Oosterbeek & van der Klaauw (2010) — *The Effect of Financial Rewards on Students' Achievement*

## Citation

Leuven, E., Oosterbeek, H., & van der Klaauw, B. (2010). The effect of financial rewards on students' achievement: Evidence from a randomized experiment. *Journal of the European Economic Association, 8*(6), 1243–1265. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1542-4774.2010.tb00554.x

## 60-second summary

This randomized field experiment offered first-year economics/business students different financial rewards for completing all first-year requirements within one year. Average passing rates and credit accumulation were essentially unchanged across reward arms, but the paper finds meaningful heterogeneity: higher-ability students and students from more advantaged backgrounds appear more responsive to higher rewards, while low-ability students do not show the same pattern.

For Paper 1, the main lesson is that a weak or null average effect can conceal sharply different responses across students, and that incentive design must be interpreted relative to feasibility of the target.

## Role in Paper 1

- **Priority:** core randomized incentive comparator.
- **Used in:** literature review and Discussion.
- **Main job:** demonstrate heterogeneous treatment response and the danger of interpreting an average null as universal non-response.
- **CMAT connection:** supports checking heterogeneity around support use/persistence without assuming a single response to the PPA context.

## Design and sample

- **Setting:** first-year economics/business students, University of Amsterdam.
- **Eligible students:** `254`.
- **Participants:** `249` (`98%` of eligible students).
- **Randomized groups:** high reward (`n=83`), low reward (`n=84`), control (`n=82`).
- **High reward:** `€681` for completing all first-year requirements within one year.
- **Low reward:** `€227` for the same target.
- **Primary outcomes:** pass all first-year requirements; number of credit points.
- **Additional outcomes:** reported study time, perceived effort, paid work, later achievement.
- **Design:** randomized field experiment.

## Key results

### Average outcomes

The paper reports no statistically significant average difference across the three groups in first-year passing rate or credit accumulation.

From Table 4:

| Group | Passing rate | Credit points |
|---|---:|---:|
| high reward | `.23` (`SE=.05`) | `23.3` (`1.7`) |
| low reward | `.20` (`.04`) | `22.7` (`1.7`) |
| control | `.20` (`.04`) | `23.1` (`1.7`) |

### Heterogeneity by math skill

For students classified as having high math skills:

| Group | Passing rate | Credit points |
|---|---:|---:|
| high reward | `.45` (`SE=.09`) | `32.8` (`2.2`) |
| low reward | `.39` (`.09`) | `29.5` (`2.3`) |
| control | `.33` (`.08`) | `28.1` (`2.1`) |

For low-math-skill students, passing rates were approximately `.08`, `.09`, `.09` and credit points `17.0`, `18.8`, `19.1` for high, low, and control respectively.

The article notes that these subgroup estimates are imprecise and often not individually statistically significant because of small subgroup sizes, but the monotonic pattern among high-math-skill students is substantively notable.

### Effort

Average weekly study time was similar across groups and differences were not statistically significant. However, `37%` of high-reward students versus `21%` of low-reward students reported that the reward increased their effort; the paper reports that these proportions differ significantly.

## Important interpretation

The authors argue that the reward target may have been too difficult for the average or low-ability student relative to the uncertain reward. This is important for Paper 1 because response to a threshold can depend on whether students perceive the threshold as attainable.

## What this paper lets us say

- Randomized financial incentives can have near-zero average effects while subgroup responses differ.
- Incentive magnitude and target attainability may matter.
- Reported time spent studying need not move even when some students perceive greater effort.
- Heterogeneity should be examined rather than treated as a post-hoc nuisance.

## What it does **not** let us say

- PPA effects should be largest for the same ability groups.
- A null average incentive effect means incentives are behaviorally irrelevant.
- The CMAT three-visit threshold is equivalent to a monetary completion reward.
- Observed subgroup patterns in CMAT would be causal treatment heterogeneity.
