# Paper 2.1 — short literature reading guide

This guide mirrors the rapid-reading layer used in Paper 1, but is intentionally shorter. It summarizes the papers currently stored in `paper/references.bib` for the visit-frequency manuscript and records the specific job each source performs in Paper 2.1.

**Bibliography audit (2026-09-20):** `references.bib` contains 14 entries. Twelve are cited in the current manuscript. `Austin (2011)` and `Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983)` remain in the BibTeX file as methodological references but are not currently cited in `paper/sections/01.tex`–`04.tex`.

The summaries below are deliberately claim-bounded. They are reading aids, not substitutes for checking the source before adding a new numerical or causal claim.

## Core field and evaluation reviews

### Mullen, Howard & Cronin (2024) — *A scoping literature review of the impact and evaluation of mathematics and statistics support in higher education*

**60-second summary.** This is the broadest recent map of mathematics and statistics support (MSS) evaluation. The review includes 148 publications from 12 countries and shows that reported impacts are often positive, but study designs, exposure definitions, outcomes and analysis methods are highly heterogeneous. A central message is that selection bias and weak or inconsistent evaluation designs limit causal interpretation of many user/non-user comparisons.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Principal field anchor for defining MSS, motivating evaluation of routine attendance records, and justifying cautious observational language.

**Boundary.** It does not provide a single pooled causal effect of mathematics support and should not be cited as evidence that CMAT attendance itself causes better outcomes.

### Lawson, Grove & Croft (2020) — *The evolution of mathematics support: a literature review*

**60-second summary.** This review describes how organised mathematics support evolved from a largely remedial response to the “Mathematics Problem” into an established part of higher-education provision. It synthesizes work on who uses support, why some students do not engage, the role and training of tutors, the institutional position of support centres, and the difficulty of evaluating effectiveness.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Provides institutional and historical context for treating CMAT as a mathematics-support centre rather than ordinary course teaching.

**Boundary.** The review is primarily narrative and does not imply that observed attendance frequency is a treatment dose.

### Matthews, Croft, Lawson & Waller (2013) — *Evaluation of mathematics support centres: a literature review*

**60-second summary.** This review focuses specifically on how mathematics support centres are evaluated. It organizes the literature around data collection, usage/activity measures and evidence of impact on students, staff and institutions, while emphasizing that quantitative and qualitative evaluation both face practical and inferential limitations.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Supports the manuscript’s argument that administrative attendance data are useful but incomplete measures of support engagement and that centre evaluation requires explicit attention to design limitations.

**Boundary.** Positive findings across centre evaluations are not equivalent to an average causal effect.

## Direct mathematics-support and tutoring evidence

### Rickard & Mills (2018) — *The effect of attending tutoring on course grades in Calculus I*

**60-second summary.** Using multiple regression for Calculus I, the authors relate tutoring-centre attendance to final course grades while controlling for prior academic ability. Their model associates roughly three tutoring visits with a one-percentage-point increase in course grade, with larger estimated associations among lower-achieving students.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Direct comparator for the idea that visit frequency may contain information beyond a binary user/non-user indicator.

**Boundary.** The result is observational and correlational; it does not establish that an additional three visits causally raise grades by one point.

### Mac an Bhaird, Morgan & O’Shea (2009) — *The impact of the mathematics support centre on the grades of first year students at the National University of Ireland Maynooth*

**60-second summary.** The study compares first-year students who used a mathematics support centre more than once with those who used it once or not at all, while also examining prior mathematical background through Leaving Certificate grades and diagnostic-test scores. Users generally had higher pass rates and marks, with especially encouraging patterns among students with weaker mathematical preparation, although the authors explicitly note the difficulty of separating centre use from effort and self-selection.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Core observational attendance/outcome comparator and an early example of separating more sustained centre use from minimal or no use.

**Boundary.** The design cannot rule out unmeasured differences between users and non-users.

### Mac an Bhaird, Fitzmaurice, Ní Fhloinn & O’Sullivan (2013) — *Student non-engagement with mathematics learning supports*

**60-second summary.** A survey of 1,633 first-year students across nine Irish higher-education institutions examines why students do not use mathematics learning support. Only 36.1% reported using support, and non-use reflected heterogeneous reasons including perceived lack of need, low motivation, time constraints, uncertainty about the service and practical or affective barriers.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Prevents the manuscript from equating zero visits with low motivation or lack of academic concern.

**Boundary.** The survey explains reported reasons for non-engagement; it does not identify the reasons for non-use in the CMAT cohort.

### Jacob & Ní Fhloinn (2019) — *A quantitative, longitudinal analysis of the impact of mathematics support in an Irish university*

**60-second summary.** Using 12 years of data and more than 10,000 first-year service-mathematics students, the study models passing as a function of prior mathematical background, module and mathematics-support attendance. Relative to never attending, even one visit is associated with higher odds of passing, and the association becomes much larger in the highest attendance category; the paper also argues that one-time users should not automatically be grouped with non-users.

**Paper 2.1 role.** The closest longitudinal precedent for separating initial engagement from progressively higher visit frequencies.

**Boundary.** The estimates remain based on observational attendance, so increasing odds across attendance groups should not be read as a causal dose-response.

### Büchele & Schürmann (2024) — *Causal evidence of the effect of a course specific mathematical learning and support center on economics students’ performance and affective variables*

**60-second summary.** Using six years of data, propensity-score matching and difference-in-differences, the authors evaluate a course-specific mathematical learning and support centre for economics students. In contrast with many simpler user/non-user studies, they find no direct effect on mathematics performance, while reporting favourable effects on test anxiety and other affective outcomes; broader engagement in semester learning activities is an important predictor of skill growth.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Identification-oriented counterweight showing why favourable raw attendance associations should not be automatically interpreted as treatment effects.

**Boundary.** Its design, setting and course-specific intervention differ from CMAT, so its estimates are methodological comparators rather than transportable effect sizes.

## Help-seeking and engagement

### Fong, Gonzales, Hill-Troglin Cox & Shinn (2023) — *Academic Help-Seeking and Achievement of Postsecondary Students: A Meta-Analytic Investigation*

**60-second summary.** This meta-analysis synthesizes 108 studies, 119 samples and 37,941 postsecondary students. Help-seeking is not a single homogeneous behaviour: formal and instrumental help-seeking are positively associated with achievement, whereas avoidant and executive forms are negatively associated; the pooled associations also show substantial heterogeneity.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Provides the broader theoretical context for interpreting support-centre use as formal help-seeking behaviour without equating attendance counts with motivation or help-seeking quality.

**Boundary.** A CMAT visit does not reveal whether the underlying help-seeking is instrumental, executive or driven by an institutional requirement, and the meta-analysis does not make CMAT attendance exogenous.

## Encouragement and stronger-identification comparators

### Pugatch & Wilson (2018) — *Nudging study habits: A field experiment on peer tutoring in higher education*

**60-second summary.** In a randomized field experiment at Reed College, brief postcard messages increased tutoring take-up by about 7 percentage points, or 23% of the control mean, and increased repeated attendance by almost as much. Despite the clear effect on tutoring use, the study finds little evidence that advertising-induced tutoring changed grades.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Demonstrates that an intervention can shift both first attendance and repeated attendance without producing a comparably clear academic-performance effect.

**Boundary.** The treatment is randomized encouragement, not tutoring attendance itself; its grade results concern students induced to attend in that setting.

### Paloyo, Rogan & Siminski (2016) — *The effect of supplemental instruction on academic performance: An encouragement design experiment*

**60-second summary.** A randomized encouragement design with 6,954 first-year course observations offered large incentives to attend Peer Assisted Study Sessions. The encouragement increased attendance by about 0.47 hours on average and more strongly among disadvantaged students; instrumental-variable estimates suggest a positive effect of attendance on grades, but the main estimate is imprecise and not statistically significant.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Strong-design comparator showing both the value and the limited statistical power of using randomized encouragement to identify attendance effects.

**Boundary.** The IV estimate is local to students whose attendance responded to the encouragement and should not be mapped directly onto CMAT users.

## Initial engagement versus frequency

### Gokhool & Lawson (2026) — *Demographic predictors of engagement with mathematics and statistics support*

**60-second summary.** The paper studies drop-in mathematics and statistics support at Coventry University across 12 disciplines and explicitly separates two processes: whether a student engages at least once and, conditional on engagement, how many visits the student makes. It models these stages with a hurdle specification: binomial logistic regression for initial engagement and a negative-binomial model for visit frequency among users.

**Paper 2.1 role.** The clearest conceptual and methodological precedent for separating the zero-versus-positive attendance margin from frequency among users.

**Boundary.** The project currently has only abstract-level access. Do not report which demographic predictors are significant, their coefficients, effect sizes or robustness results until the full text is reviewed. See `paper/LITERATURE_ACCESS_NOTES.md`.

## Methodological references retained in `references.bib`

### Austin (2011) — *An Introduction to Propensity Score Methods for Reducing the Effects of Confounding in Observational Studies*

**60-second summary.** Austin provides a practical introduction to propensity scores as balancing scores and reviews matching, stratification, inverse-probability weighting and covariate adjustment, together with balance diagnostics and distinctions between estimands such as the ATE and ATT. The central limitation is that propensity-score methods balance observed baseline covariates; they do not remove bias from unmeasured confounding.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Useful if a propensity-score sensitivity analysis is added later.

**Current status.** Present in `references.bib` but **not cited in the current manuscript**.

### Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983) — *The central role of the propensity score in observational studies for causal effects*

**60-second summary.** This is the foundational propensity-score paper. It defines the propensity score as the probability of treatment assignment conditional on observed covariates and establishes its balancing property, showing that under strong ignorability adjustment on the scalar score can remove bias attributable to observed covariates.

**Paper 2.1 role.** Foundational citation if the paper introduces propensity-score methodology or an associated sensitivity analysis.

**Current status.** Present in `references.bib` but **not cited in the current manuscript**.

## What to remember for Paper 2.1

The bibliography supports three distinct claims that should remain separate:

1. **Mathematics support is an established and widely evaluated institutional practice**, but much of its evaluation literature is observational and methodologically heterogeneous.
2. **Initial use and repeated use are not the same empirical object.** Prior work provides both observational frequency analyses and a recent explicit two-stage engagement/frequency formulation.
3. **A favourable user/non-user association is not automatically a causal tutoring effect.** Encouragement experiments and stronger observational designs often produce more qualified performance results.

For the current manuscript, the safest synthesis is therefore: CMAT attendance is an observed service-use behaviour; the zero-versus-any-attendance contrast and the frequency-among-users contrast should be analysed separately; and neither contrast should be described as a causal dose-response without stronger identification.
