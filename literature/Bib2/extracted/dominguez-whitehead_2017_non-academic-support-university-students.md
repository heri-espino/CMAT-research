##### Non-academic support services and university student experiences: adopting an organizational theory perspective

Yasmine Dominguez-Whitehead

Wits School of Education, University of the Witwatersrand, South Africa

## ABSTRACT

Recent and continuous transformation efforts at South African universities and the varied challenges facing higher education make it increasingly important to undertake an examination of student experiences that extend beyond the academic realm. This paper presents findings on nonacademic support services, as experienced by students. The study was conducted at two South African universities and is grounded within an organizational theory perspective. The data indicate that students ' interactions with those charged with providing non-academic services are characterized by substandard services. Participants ' reports are analyzed and the findings indicate that participants critically construct explanations for the substandard services received. Three overarching themes are considered in accounting for substandard non-academic support services: (1) personal, (2) institutional, and (3) interpersonal. The findings are discussed with respect to implications and recommendations for university management and administration.

## Introduction

Student support in the context of higher education has implications for how students experience the university (Ruben 1995; Kuh et al. 2010) and has been a topic of much concern in the literature on student affairs and student development (see, for example, Komives and Woodard 2003; Reynolds, Mueller, and Clark 2008). Although academic support services (such as library, writing, and tutoring services) have received considerable attention (see, for example, Hamrick, Evans, and Schuh 2002; Archer 2011; Bell and Frost 2012) less is known about support services that are primarily concerned with non-academic aspects such as, financial aid, parking, security, and residence hall services. Nonacademic services are essential for successfully operating a university and for helping students succeed. However, non-academic services have traditionally been viewed as concomitant functions of the university and are undervalued due to their non-academic nature.

This paper examines non-academic student support services by adopting an organizational theory perspective. First, a rationale is provided, which makes a case for why it is relevant to examine nonacademic support services and a brief overview of higher education in South Africa is presented. Second, the study ' s theoretical framework is discussed and a review of the literature on student support and a customer service oriented context is provided. Third, the methodology employed is described. Thereafter, a discussion is presented which engages with the findings, which indicate that participants experience substandard non-academic services. Lastly, implications and recommendations are considered.

## Rationale

Despite non-academic services possibly being undervalued due to their non-academic nature, these services can influence academic success (Furr and Elling 2002). For example, the services provided by residence halls, financial aid offices and campus security are important for promoting or creating a space where students can succeed academically (Park, Denson, and Johnson 2014; Patton and Gregory 2014; Blimling 2015). More specifically, students who live in the residence halls and benefit from the services provided by them have been known to gain positive academic outcomes (Pascarella and Terenzini 2005) and to integrate socially and academically into the campus community (Graunke and Woosley 2005). Additionally, financial aid, particularly for low-income students can become relevant not only for degree attainment, but also for learning and achievement (GoldrickRab, Harris, and Trostel 2009).

Providing support and validation to students is particularly significant, with invalidating experiences being detrimental for students. Students are invalidated when they are undermined or disrespected by staff members. Validating experiences, on the other hand, refer to instances in which staff members display respect for a student and do something that affirms the student as belonging at the university and being capable of doing academic work. Validating experiences within the university are beneficial for students and can manifest themselves in a classroom setting or outside the classroom (Rendon 1994). Indeed, non-academic staff members can make significant contributions. When they display an interest in, and concern for, students, they create an environment that promotes student success (Kuh et al. 2010). This suggests that how students are treated by non-academic staff can impact both student outcomes and experiences. Within the South African context we know that students who are mistreated by administrative staff can feel discouraged and unwelcomed, and this in turn can contribute to them regretting enrolling at a specific institution of higher education (Imenda, Kongolo, and Grewal 2004). Accordingly, a focus has been placed on providing quality service and the need to provide skills and training to staff members who serve students (Akoojee and Nkomo 2007). Nonetheless, non-academic services and the ways in which such services are provided continue to be an overlooked area of study in South Africa.

## The South African higher education context

Higher education in South Africa has recently experienced substantial developments. Systemic changes have transformed higher education management and leadership practices since the advent of democracy in 1994. Key governing documents have placed specific demands on the leadership and management of universities (see, for example, Department of Education 1997, 2001, 2008). Policy developments and implementation challenges have been pervasive and have contributed to complexities with respect to how to best manage higher education institutions within the context of transformation.

In addition to the management and leadership challenges experienced, the higher education system faces challenges relating to a dearth of financial aid for students, student housing shortages, racial tensions and a lack of racial integration, and concerns surrounding academic quality and standards (Koen, Cele, and Libhaber 2006; Pattman 2007; Department of Higher Education and Training 2013). In particular, the recent 2015/2016 #FeesMustFall student protests have brought to light the significance of financial aid challenges and the high cost of higher education (Naicker 2016). Another major concern is the unacceptably high rate of attrition, which is estimated at approximately 50% (Badat 2016). This is an indicator that students face difficulties when it comes to successfully completing a university degree. Investigating the academic challenges faced by students and the academic support services offered to students is necessary, but this does not necessarily provide us with a complete picture of the challenges that students face.

## Theoretical framework

The study draws on organizational theory by assuming that as members of a university, individual students and staff members are part of, and embedded in, an organization. This framework has been specifically selected based on its applicability to the data. While organizations have the potential to be promising places, they unfortunately can also be confusing and frustrating spaces (Bolman and Deal 2003) that are characterized by different actors and highly structured and complex functions (Scott and Meyer 1994). The study is thus grounded in an organizational perspective which rejects the view that rationality, efficiency, and rules characterize organizations (Scott and Davis 2007). Instead, an organizational theory perspective is adopted which is concerned with individual participants within organizations, the ways in which individuals make sense of the organizational environment and the overarching ways in which organizations operate. In making sense of the environment, individuals within organizations organize information to process it, reduce uncertainty, and cope with equivocality (Weick 1995). In other words, individuals within an organization make sense of how they experience the organization. One way in which individuals make sense of their experiences in organizations is by providing explanations and reasons that account for their experiences.

The importance of individuals and the ways in which they make sense of organizations, as conveyed by organizational theory, makes it relevant to consider personal, institutional, and interpersonal dimensions. With respect to the personal dimension, individuals arrive at organizations with their own personal characteristics, orientations and life experiences. The importance of personal characteristics in organizations has been discussed as it pertains to congruence between an individual and an organization, and in terms of an individual ' s performance outcomes (Ostroff 1993). While people enter organizations with their own personal characteristics, organizations, particularly long-standing organizations such as well-established universities have developed system-wide practices, patterns and cultures that can be explicit or implicit. The institutional dimension is thus concerned with these system-wide practices, patterns, and cultures. Organizations develop not only rules, policies and formal ways of operating, but they also embody pervasive beliefs and values, and a distinct culture (Bolman and Deal 2003). These system-wide and pervasive ways in which organizations function are concerned with that which is institutionalized. In other words, what is institutional transcends an individual person within an organization, and is concerned with the organization ' s character and explicit and implicit rules.

In order to better understand how organizations operate it is necessary to take into consideration the personal characteristics of individuals within organizations and the institutional character of organizations, but it is also necessary to be concerned with the ways in which people interact with one another within organizations. The interpersonal dimension is concerned with interactions between organizational members (McAllister 1995). The interactions that organizational members have with each other are based on the ways in which organizational members perceive each other (Heider 1982). In other words, interactions between organizational members, which are based on perceptions of others, are relevant for understanding organizations. An organizational theory perspective which is concerned with how individuals within organizations make sense of their environment and which draws on personal, institutional, and interpersonal dimensions provides a framework for this study.

## Addressing quality and student support in a customer service oriented context

Students increasingly view service provision in higher education in similar ways to the type of service provided by commercial entities (Wright and O ' Neill 2002). Within the specific South African context, students have demanded improvements that are specifically concerned with financial aid services, inadequate housing, and unsupportive staff (Koen, Cele, and Libhaber 2006). Indeed, the recent 2015/2016 #FeesMustFall protests have been concerned not only with making higher education

more affordable, but also with addressing a lack of resources to support high quality academic development programs, adequate student housing, and curriculum transformation (Badat 2016). Thus, students are demanding that universities offer affordable and high quality education that is responsive to their needs. Accordingly, numerous universities now view themselves as educational businesses, and this has had implications for adopting a business-like approach to their management and operations (Johnson 2001). This marketization of higher education, which increasingly views students as consumers, has meant that there are heightened concerns with respect to satisfying the needs of students and providing quality service (Maringe 2010). On the one hand, viewing students as consumers has placed students as central stakeholders who should be able to raise concerns with respect to the quality of their educational experience. On the other hand, concerns with respect to the commodification of the university have been raised (Maringe 2010). It has been argued that adopting a business model that views students as customers and clients, commoditizes knowledge and de-professionalizes those that work at the university, without necessarily improving the quality of service (Ntshoe et al. 2010).

An examination of non-academic support services requires acknowledging the marketization of higher education and the increasingly widespread perspective that students are consumers who pay for goods and services. Universities are thus challenged to seek a balance between being sensitive to the needs of students, while simultaneously maintaining the integrity of the university as a place that prioritizes learning and knowledge production. While the student-as-consumer discourse is a prominent one, universities are also called to consider providing high quality non-academic support service, not necessarily because students are paying customers, but because non-academic support services have the potential to promote student success (cf. Komives and Woodard 2003).

## Methodology

The study employed qualitative methodology in order to examine the type of experiences, related to non-academic support services, recurrently reported by students. The data for this study were collected at two South African research universities. For the purposes of this paper, the first university is referred to as G University and the second university is referred to as K University. Both universities offer numerous degrees and house a medical school, law school, and a school of education, in addition to the many disciplines found within the sciences, social sciences, and humanities. As comprehensive research universities they offer non-academic support services such as housing, financial aid, medical, counseling, and security services. G University has been in existence since the late nineteenth century and K University has been in existence since the early twentieth century. As such, the universities are some of the oldest and largest universities in the country and are located in major South African cities. Both universities attract students from all corners of the country and from many different parts of the world. The purpose for selecting two universities was to identify recurrently reported experiences that indicate transferability of findings across two universities, instead of identifying potentially idiosyncratic reported experiences that are only relevant to one particular university.

### Participants

Participants were recruited in a variety of lectures and participated on a voluntary basis. Students interested in participating were asked to invite friends who attended the same university to take part in focus group interviews. The student sample was not intended to be representative of university students in South Africa. However, the study sought to obtain a range of student participants, and thus a purposive sampling procedure was utilized to ensure that student participants included first year, second year, third year, and fourth-year students, those studying in the disciplines of humanities, social sciences, science and engineering, and health sciences, and students from various racial backgrounds. A total of 15 focus groups were conducted (10 at G University and 5 at

### Data collection and analysis

Focus group interviews were selected as a research tool since they allow participants to engage in discussions with similar others by using their own terminology and pursuing their own concerns (Kitzinger 1995). The participation of friends in focus groups served the purpose of intentionally bringing students together who were well acquainted with each other and would likely feel comfortable discussing their experiences with familiar peers. The use of focus groups is thus aligned with the study ' s aim to investigate non-academic support services, as experienced by students themselves, since it allows students to collaboratively engage with each other and specifically discuss non-academic support services. The interactive nature of focus groups enables multiple participants to agree or disagree with each other, creating opportunities to confirm or reject reports of fellow participants, thus enhancing the study ' s trustworthiness. The researcher ' s role in the focus group interviews was to facilitate discussion by introducing questions for discussion, asking students to elaborate when necessary and posing follow-up questions to elicit more detail and depth (Rubin and Rubin 1995).

During the focus group interviews, participants were asked a total of 12 open-ended, non-invasive questions. The questions were concerned with eliciting information related to their favorite aspects of being university students, the strengths of the university, the challenges and difficulties they experience, what could be done to improve their lives as students, and what kinds of events/incidents had been particularly important for them during their time as university students. Participants were specifically probed with respect to non-academic experiences, events, and incidents. Four of the 12 questions are included below to illustrate the types of questions posed:

- (1) What are some of your favorite things about being a student at your university?
- (2) What would you say are the strengths of your university?
- (3) What are some of the challenges or difficulties that you face as a student?
- (4) Can you discuss specific issues/problems that you have to deal with that are not directly related to academic matters?

The study yielded a considerable amount of data. The paper at hand focuses on a subset of these data, with 43 students represented in the data analyzed for this study, which is specifically concerned with non-academic student support services. Participating students included a wide variety of students, with 21 identifying as White, 17 identifying as Black and 5 identifying as Asian. Fifteen identified as first-year students, 11 identified as second-year students, 9 identified as third-year students and 8 identified as fourth-year students.

With respect to the criteria utilized to determine what constitutes non-academic support, only data that were explicitly concerned with financial aid services, campus security, housing and residential services and parking services were analyzed. While it is acknowledged that overlaps between academic and non-academic matters exist, data that were explicitly concerned with library, writing and tutoring services were not included in the analysis, nor were data concerned with academic courses, assignments or interactions with academic staff members.

The study was grounded on the ontological premise of the constructivist paradigm, which suggests that multiple forms of realities are constructed by individuals in localized and specific settings (Guba and Lincoln 1994). This paper is thus concerned with examining the subjective experiences and perspectives of student participants, as they relate to how they make sense of the nonacademic support services they receive. The epistemological assumptions of this approach are underpinned by an interactive link between the researcher and the participants (Lincoln and Guba 2000), as well as between the research participants.


<!-- p:7 -->


With respect to data analysis, first, the video- and audio-taped focus group interviews were transcribed. All identifiable information (names of participants, staff, universities, and campuses) were replaced with pseudonyms. Second, the constant comparative method of data analysis was utilized to code the data and generate analytic categories that represent the experiences recurrently reported by students (Glaser 1965). This inductive analysis yielded the two following categories: (1) lack of service and assistance and (2) verbal abuse. Subsequently, three themes (personal, institutional, and interpersonal) were drawn from the organizational theory framework adopted, and were applied in light of their suitability for analyzing students ' reports when it came to providing explanations for substandard service. The data analysis process thus began by utilizing an inductive approach, and subsequently utilized a more deductive approach, which incorporated the three themes drawn from the theoretical framework. Approaches that combine inductive and deductive analysis, such as the one employed, are useful when it comes to connecting data with theory (Barnes 2012).

## Findings and analysis

The findings indicate that participants experience substandard non-academic support services and construct explanations for the substandard services they receive. Substandard services are characterized by: (1) lack of service and assistance and (2) verbal abuse. Three overarching themes are considered which present explanations for the substandard non-academic support services received by students: (1) personal, 2) institutional, and (3) interpersonal.

### Substandard services

The excerpts below describe examples of some of the substandard services experienced, from the point of view of student participants. In reporting substandard service, participants generally identified a single individual staff member. Thus, substandard service typically consisted of one-on-one interactions between a student and a staff member. Substandard services refer specifically to participants ' reports of experiencing a lack of service and assistance, or enduring verbal abuse.

#### Lack of service and assistance

A lack of service and assistance refers to instances when students perceive that they are denied a service or form of assistance by a non-academic staff member. Excerpt 1 below provides an example of a lack of service and assistance that is related to parking. It is noteworthy that K University has had long-standing problems relating to scarcity of parking for both staff members and students. This has led to some remote open grass fields being used for parking when paved parking is no longer available. While limited designated parking spaces are available for staff members, designated parking spaces are not demarked for students. This results in students and visitors sharing parking lots, and on occasion parking spaces (that may usually be available to students) are reserved for specific prominent visitors. While visitors are not required to pay for parking, staff and students are charged an annual parking fee for their parking permit.

### Explaining substandard service

The aforementioned discussion has served to illustrate that substandard services are characterized by: (1) a lack of service and assistance and (2) verbal abuse. However, students also provide explanations for the substandard services they receive. This demonstrates that participants are not passive recipients; they instead critically construct explanations and reasons to make sense of the substandard service received. Three overarching themes are considered in accounting for substandard services: personal , institutional , and interpersonal . Selected excerpts are presented and discussed to illustrate these three themes.

Excerpt 4 demonstrates that participants collectively engage in generating several institutional explanations for the lack of adequate service and assistance experienced. First, the reference made to ' everyone ' points to a system-wide ethos of poor service, which deflects attention from the individual staff member. Second, a lack of ' clearly defined roles ' points not to one staff member not knowing what their job is, but instead to staff members generally within the institution not having a good understanding of their responsibilities and this is seen to contribute to ' disorganization ' within the institution. Third, the lack of institutional funding to hire more people is viewed as contributing to overworked staff members, thus implying that overworked staff members do not provide adequate service to students. Participants in Excerpt 4 do not necessarily agree on the exact reason why they experience poor service, however, the explanations they provided are all concerned with institutional factors that highlight system-wide beliefs, practices, ways of operating, and organizational resources.

The participants in Excerpt 4 perceive that staff members are stressed and overworked, and there is indeed evidence of high levels of work overload among support staff at South African universities. High levels of work overload can impact stress levels, and higher levels of physical and psychological stress are reported by South African support staff when compared to support staff in other countries (Coetzee and Rothmann 2005). Additionally, support staff at South African universities report feeling that their university is not committed to them as employees (Coetzee and Rothmann 2005) and this in turn can mean less productivity and a lack of assuming responsibility (Chow 1990). This is further complicated by the incremental reduction in government funds for institutions of higher education in South Africa since 1994, and the resulting cost cutting strategies that have been implemented (such as outsourcing and austerity measures) (Badat 2016). An institution ' s resources and organizational culture thus matter when it comes to operating optimally and providing services.

In conceptualizing organizational culture, it has been noted that organizational members hold common assumptions that result in ' patterns of perceiving, thinking, feeling and behaving [that] provide meaning, and comfort ' (Schein 1990, 111). Based on this definition, an organization ' s culture has potential consequences for how members ' think, feel, and behave. It would then follow that the organizational culture that non-academic staff members subscribe to, has potential implications for how staff interact with students and the service that students receive (or fail to receive). With respect to role ambiguity, it has been noted that within service providing institutions, friendliness decreases with increasing role ambiguity (Brown and Wade 1987) and as role clarity decreases, job tension and role conflict increase (Rogers, Clow, and Kash 1994). In other words, when organizational members do not have clearly defined roles, this impacts other factors, such as friendliness and job tension. In providing explanations related to system-wide operations and organizational culture, participants in Excerpt 4 draw on institutional factors to account for, and make sense of, the substandard service they receive.

Evidence for this is provided by her stating ' first thing they see, is a first year student. ' In other words, the way she is viewed and perceived matters for how she is treated. Excerpt 5 depicts first-year students as undesirable and illustrates that participants perceive the relationships between staff members and first-year students to be strained. Moreover, Anne ' s disdain for first-year students (despite her being a first-year student herself), provides not only an explanation for the substandard service she receives, but also possibly provides an excuse for this type of service.

The reports of students in Excerpt 5 should be understood within the backdrop of first-year students in South Africa experiencing anxiety due to them not receiving explicit information pertaining to what is expected of them with respect to registration and registration-related processes (Bojuwoye 2002). That first-year students in South Africa often report feeling lost during the registration period and complain of standing in long lines for extended amounts of time (Modipane 2011) suggests that the orientation they receive could be improved. Staff members may thus view firstyear students as not having adequate information to manage registration and administrative requirements.

Interpersonal factors and social context matter for student success (Allen 1992) as well as for quality in higher education (Morley 2003). The ways in which university staff members perceive, relate to, and interact with, students has been identified as key to good student affairs practice. For instance, Carl Rogers ' notion of unconditional positive regard, which advocates sustaining a nonjudgmental attitude, has been applied to student affairs practice (Moore and Upcraft 1990). Rogers (1958) unconditional positive regard is concerned with the characteristics of a helping relationship and has implications for the interpersonal. Staff members who hold positive views and nonjudgmental attitudes toward students, as endorsed by the work of Rogers (1958), may be better placed to engage in interactions with students that promote respect and a helpful disposition. Holding a nonjudgmental attitude toward students has been viewed by many student affairs practitioners as a central tenet of good interpersonal relations. Indeed, interpersonal skills and working cooperatively with others is a prominent theme in the area of student services (see, for example, Lovell and Kosten 2000; Herdlein 2004). By drawing on interpersonal factors, which are concerned with how financial aid staff members feel about and interact with first-year students, participants in Excerpt 5 are able to provide an explanation for the substandard service they receive.

## Discussion

Given the marketization of higher education and its implications for viewing students as consumers and universities as businesses (Maringe 2010), it is apposite to examine non-academic support services from the perspective of students themselves, while simultaneously being cautious of the student-as-consumer model. The findings indicate that participants across two South African universities experienced substandard services. The substandard services were characterized by a lack of service and assistance, and verbal abuse. More specifically, students reported experiencing substandard service when they interact with parking attendants, security guards and staff at both the financial aid and housing offices. While parking and security services are important, a lack of access to funds and housing have major implications for student success in higher education (Broton and Goldrick-Rab 2016) and efforts to assist students in gaining access to both financial aid and housing are imperative. The substandard services that students report confronting in housing offices are consistent with reports which indicate that housing allocation processes at several South African universities are impacted by maladministration, bribery, corruption, and nepotism (Department of Higher Education and Training 2013). It is difficult to conceive that offices in which maladministration, bribery, corruption, and nepotism take place can offer adequate services and support to students. Additionally, the substandard services that students report receiving from financial aid offices are consistent with reports that some financial aid offices only disburse National Student Financial Aid Scheme funds to students toward the end of a term, instead of during the beginning of the term, which is when the funds are supposed to be made available to students (Jones et al. 2008). There is thus evidence that some financial aid offices are inefficient and that these inefficiencies may lead to students experiencing undue hardship. Adequate financial aid service should help students address their financial aid problems and should not contribute to them experiencing financial difficulties. The role of housing and financial aid offices is critical and the service offered by such offices should be scrutinized and monitored.

Participants ' collective reports serve to provide multidimensional explanations for substandard non-academic support services. Three specific dimensions provide explanations for the service received: the personal, the institutional , and the interpersonal . Participants ' accounts are consistent with the view that organizations can be confusing and frustrating spaces and that organizational members attempt to make sense of their organizational environment (Bolman and Deal 2003). By drawing on an organizational theory perspective that takes into account the experiences of students, an examination of the university as an organization is possible. Students are thus seen to engage with the roles and functions of offices that provide non-academic services. They also display that they have insights into the challenges experienced by non-academic staff members. Taken together, the data indicate that participants have an awareness of personal, institutional and interpersonal factors that are perceived to have implications for their university experience.

The limitations of the study deserve some attention. The data were collected at two large urban universities situated in South Africa, as such the findings may not be pertinent to small or medium-sized universities located in rural settings or small towns. While the findings are not generalizable, the study nonetheless reveals some of the ways in which students experience and explain substandard services. The findings may present useful insights for universities that experience challenges providing adequate non-academic services or are committed to improving non-academic services. It is also noteworthy that the study was concerned with the experiences of students and therefore deliberately offers a student perspective. This means that the perspectives of non-academic staff or management were not examined. Future research, which examines the views and perspectives of non-academic staff members with respect to the services they offer students, could further our understanding of non-academic services within higher education.

## Implications and recommendations for management and administration

The participants ' reports of substandard services are characterized by (1) lack of service and assistance and (2) verbal abuse. This signals a need for university management to improve the caliber of service and administration in a manner that meets students ' needs and validates their presence at the university. Although students are already represented in university councils and have their own student leadership and governing bodies (Koen, Cele, and Libhaber 2006), there might be additional ways in which they can provide more direct and consistent feedback to university offices that provide nonacademic services. This could be done by electing a few students to sit on the committees that provide leadership and direction to non-academic support service offices, particularly those that provide vital services such as financial aid and housing offices. However, no amount of student feedback will bring about improved services, if university officials and staff members at all levels do not have the skills, resources, and the willingness to meet the needs of students.

The recommendations for management and administration discussed below draw heavily on the three themes: (1) personal, (2) institutional , and (3) interpersonal . The recommendations are based on the perspectives of students, and while a student perspective is valuable, a note of caution is warranted, which acknowledges that this is only one perspective. Recommendations based on the views and perspectives of non-academic staff and university management are also valuable, and future research which examines additional perspectives would be particularly useful for gaining insights on the challenges experienced by staff members at a personal level.

With respect to institutional factors, courtesy and a healthy work ethic should be fostered and promoted as part of a university ' s ethos. Additionally, providing non-academic service to students should be viewed as part of assisting students to succeed academically. Other system-wide factors such as a dearth of monetary resources should be taken into consideration. Universities must not only be aware that a lack of financial resources could negatively impact the provision of non-academic student support services, but must also be prepared to mitigate some of the problems associated with this. Additionally, university leadership should provide all staff members with clear direction, expectations, and unambiguous information about the roles that they must assume and the tasks they must perform. University structures and processes should be clearly outlined for staff as well as for students, to prevent confusion, ambiguity, and frustration. There appears to be a need for the implementation of clear processes and functions at offices and units that are in charge of fees and financial aid matters. These types of services are vital for under-resourced students and a lack of service in this domain can result in a ' domino effect of problems later ' (Godwin and Markham 1996, 667). In other words, if financial aid problems are left unresolved this may later lead to registration, housing, or academic problems.

Although individuals ' perceptions of others and the ways they interact with other organizational members can be shaped by their own values and ideas or by the institutional culture, individuals can develop work orientations that are congruent with providing optimal service (cf. Alvesson and Willmott 2002). More specifically, interpersonal factors do not necessarily have to be viewed as static. The ways in which people interact with each other can be changed or altered. It is reasonable for universities to expect certain behavior from staff members. Universities can shape, drive, and enforce how individual staff members interact with students. Ethical practices and appropriate behavior, as well as effective listening and displays of empathy are key when it comes to assisting students (Herdlein 2004). As such, appropriate training, guidance, and professional development workshops and programs can provide clear expectations and guidelines with respect to how non-academic staff should interact with students and the types of helpful orientations that they should adopt.

## Concluding remarks

While participants report experiencing substandard services, their reports indicate that they are not passive recipients. They actively consider explanations for the type of service they experience. The findings indicate that the substandard services they receive can be explained by engaging with personal, institutional , and interpersonal factors. Investigating the ways in which students experience and make sense of non-academic support services can provide useful direction for rectifying problems and concerns related to non-academic service provision, and this may lead to promoting and supporting student academic success and positive student experiences.

Further research that examines non-academic support service (particularly from the perspectives of non-academic staff members and management) is necessary to further inform practice and address substandard service. Additionally, research that examines exemplary non-academic support service at universities can provide examples of models, practices, and organizational frameworks that could be emulated. By adopting an organizational theory framework, support services can be examined by taking into consideration the various role players within organizations and the complexities of large institutions, such as universities.
