#### Empowering global voices: enhancing language component of self-efficacy through digital co-national peer support

Marianna Rolbina a ,  Siobhan Caughey a and  Mina Rezaeian b

a Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, United Kingdom; b University of Edinburgh Business School, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, United Kingdom

## ABSTRACT

Navigating unfamiliar academic requirements and different expectations in host  country  universities,  international  students  often  experience  reduced self-efficacy -  or  confidence  in  their  academic  ability.  Yet,  across  the existing  variety  of  support  mechanisms,  few  studies  explore  co-national peer support. Our exploratory experimental study investigates the potential  of  asynchronous  digital  peer  support  from  second   -  and  thirdyear international students, who have successfully transitioned from home to host  academic  system,  to improve  first-year students' self-efficacy during  the  early  months  of  their  university  degree.  We  demonstrate  that co-national peers provide more effective vicarious improvement of international  students'  language  confidence  (an  important  component  of self-efficacy)  than  generalised  peer  support.  The  study  contributes  to higher education literature by highlighting the positive role of conationals  in  helping  peers  transitioning  to  a  new  academic  system.  It  also advances  emerging  research  on  asynchronous  and  digital  interventions, confirming  it  as  a  promising  avenue  for  providing  peer  support  in  the early  stages  of  a  new  degree  and  managing  the  initial  hesitation  many international students feel when asking for help.

## Introduction

International students face heightened challenges compared to their local peers when starting a new academic degree: profound lifestyle changes (Denovan and Macaskill 2013), loss or weakening of preexisting social ties and increased pressure for autonomy (Guay, Ratelle, and Chanal 2008; Macakova and  Wood  2022),  stereotypes  about  their  learning  styles  (Heng  2016),  language  barriers  (Sherry, Thomas, and Chui 2009), and unfamiliar requirements within the host country educational  systems (Yip  2021).  In  this  situation,  peer  support  and  acceptance  is  pivotal  for  restoring  their  self-efficacy, or confidence in effectively handling new challenges (Arthur 2017; Bandura 1977; Yu, Mak, and Body­ cott 2021). Self-efficacy is a central element of academic adaptation; high levels are linked to improved academic  and  social  performance,  while  low  self-efficacy  increases  dropout  risk  (cf.,  Holden  2016; Honicke and Broadbent 2016; Sotardi 2022). Yet, the international transition that erodes self-efficacy also  fosters  isolation  and  reluctance  to  engage  and  seek  help  (Mikal,  Yang,  and  Lewis  2015;  Wu, Garza,  and  Guzman  2015),  creating  a  paradox:  students  most  needing  peer  support  are  least  likely to pursue it. Finding new ways to improve international students' self-efficacy through peer support is thus not just a theoretical conundrum, but an important practical concern.

####### KEYWORDS

Peer support; international students; self-efficacy; asynchronous delivery; social cognitive theory Social Cognitive Theory outlines four self-efficacy cultivation approaches (Bandura 1977): enactive (gradually mastering tasks), vicarious (observing others' experiences), verbal persuasion (encourage­ ment),  and  emotional  arousal  (e.g.  desensitising  to  challenges  through  repeated  exposure).  The vicarious  approach  is  particularly  promising  for  its  low  engagement  barrier  and  rapid  impact, while requiring no direct interaction with the task or with others. It occurs when students observe someone  tackling  a  problem,  empathise  and  compare  themselves  with  the  observed,  learn,  and gain  confidence  in  addressing  similar  challenges  without  directly  encountering  them  (Bandura et  al.  2003).  The  effect  is  amplified  when  peers  share  some  characteristics  (Rosenberg-Kima  et  al. 2010). Despite its potential, the vicarious approach to cultivating self-efficacy is rarely studied inde­ pendently, as in classroom setting observations frequently co-occur with enactive interactions (Bor­ gonovi  and  Pokropek  2019).  Moreover,  no  research  has  specifically  explored  its  impact  on international students.


<!-- p:3 -->

While the COVID 19 pandemic-driven shift to online learning raised mental health concerns (cf., Aristovnik et al. 2023), it also revealed new opportunities for vicarious interventions. For instance, Walton and colleagues (2023) found that reading peers' experiences improved the sense of belong­ ing,  particularly  for  underrepresented  students.  Furthermore,  Raaper,  Hardey,  and  Aad  (2024) showed online videos by other international students help their co-national peers navigate new aca­ demic systems. These findings align with Social Cognitive Theory's emphasis on peer similarity and prior evidence that international students connect more easily with co-nationals (Arthur 2017; Mikal, Yang, and Lewis 2015). However, the impact of such interventions for international students' selfefficacy remains underexplored.

We follow up on these disjointed findings, responding to the call for deeper insights into the role of co-nationals (Wilczewski and Alon 2022), with the research question:

Is  vicarious  asynchronous digital peer support from co-nationals associated with higher self-efficacy and aca­ demic performance among international university students?

This  paper  contributes  to  academic  literature  in  three  ways.  First,  it  advances  peer  support research (Bandura 1977; Bandura et al. 2003) by establishing vicarious asynchronous digital inter­ vention as a  gateway mechanism to broader engagement. Second, it enriches the literature on international  students  (Jääskelä  et  al.  2017;  Wilczewski  and  Alon  2022)  by  highlighting  conationals' unique role in peer support and self-efficacy cultivation, suggesting that national aca­ demic context matters when talking about international students. Finally, it opens new research avenues  by  following  up  on  emerging  studies  of  new  media  and  mechanisms  of  student support (Han and Geng 2023; Huang 2023; Walton et al. 2023), integrating them with international student research and validating vicarious digital peer support as an effective tool for boosting conationals' self-efficacy.

## Theoretical background

### Peer support mechanisms

While personal elements, such as choosing better learning strategies, foster self-efficacy (e.g. Gutz­ weiler, Pfeiffer, and In-Albon 2022; Sotardi and Brogt 2019), the social element is equally critical. Stu­ dents  have  higher  self-efficacy  and  thrive  in  inclusive  environments  where  they  feel  a  sense  of community,  free  from  exclusion  or  alienation  (Brouwer  et  al.  2016;  Yu,  Mak,  and  Bodycott  2021). Creating  such  spaces  requires  a  combined  effort  of  institution,  teaching  staff,  families  and  peers (Wilcox, Winn, and Fyvie-Gauld 2005).

Peer  support  mechanisms   - student clubs and societies,  study groups,  or peer mentorship   - enhance students' sense of belonging, self-efficacy (Sotardi 2022), academic performance (Dancer, Morrison,  and  Tarr  2014),  and  overall  learning  experience  (Wang  et  al.  2023).  These  mechanisms include  both  enactive  interactions -  asking  peers  for  support -  and  vicarious  experiences hearing peers ask questions in class and observing their interactions  - and are most effective when strong relationships and shared characteristics exist (Laranjeira and Teixeira 2025; Sawtelle, Brewe, and  Kramer  2012).  However,  isolating  the  impact  of  peer  support  from  other  support  systems remains challenging (cf., Bowden, Tickle, and Naumann 2019; Yu, Mak, and Bodycott 2021), limiting comprehensive understanding of its effects.


<!-- p:4 -->

The absence of a unifying consensus on peer support's role stems from two dominant perspec­ tives in the literature. Some view peer support as a bridge to formal academic assistance from teach­ ing staff (e.g. Brouwer et al. 2016; Hommes et al. 2012; Wilcox, Winn, and Fyvie-Gauld 2005; Zeng and Watkins 2010), while others see it as a dual-purpose mechanism that boosts emotional well-being and academic performance independently from staff support (Cao, Zhu, and Meng 2021; Laranjeira and Teixeira 2025). For instance, a study on doctoral students demonstrates that peers provide prac­ tical,  applied  advice  while  supervisors  offer  broader  directional  guidance  (Wang  et  al.  2023).  It remains  unclear  whether  these  perspectives  reflect  distinct  roles  of  peer  support  or  variations  in the sufficiency of staff guidance, raising questions about its efficacy as an isolated mechanism.

Additionally,  while  peer  support  is  theoretically  accessible  to  all  students  starting  university (Sotardi  2022),  many  feel  isolated  and  hesitate  to  seek  help,  perpetuating  a  vicious  cycle  of reduced engagement and support (Guay, Ratelle, and Chanal 2008; Mikal, Yang, and Lewis 2015). Students find it easier to empathise with similar experiences (Bandura et al. 2003), yet diverse back­ grounds lead to varied experience of academic and social challenges (Chelberg and Bosman 2020). Despite acknowledging the importance of peer similarity, no research has explored whether tailored peer  support,  as  opposed  to  one-size-fits-all,  could  improve  self-efficacy  of  students  with  shared feature.  However,  studies  on  international  students  provide  some  insights  into  the  potential  of such targeted approaches.

### Challenges faced by international students

International  students  are  not  a  homogenous  group.  Individual  differences  in  self-efficacy  among students  may  override  international  differences  (Bartimote-Aufflick  et  al.  2015),  yet,  the  latter persist  and  are  felt  more  acutely  by  international  students  (OECD  2023;  Zhao  2020).  Thus,  conationals share some experiences, making their support valuable.

International students frequently struggle with the new linguistic and cultural environment (Wilc­ zewski and Alon 2022; Zhang and Goodson 2011). However, even when language and culture are familiar,  students  are  faced  with  unfamiliar  educational  systems  (cf.,  Yip  2021).  For  example,  Yu, Mak, and Bodycott (2021) found that while English and local Cantonese proficiency aided mainland Chinese students in Hong Kong, academic self-efficacy had a greater impact on adaptation. Unfami­ liar academic system affects international students' adaptation as it requires them to reassess their understanding  of  degree  requirements,  participation  expectation,  course  structure,  and  learning style (Zeng 2016, 2021).

While national differences do not imply that one educational system is superior or more challen­ ging than another, the transition poses challenges as students' prior educational assumptions clash with host country expectations (Holden 2016) in elements such as willingness to ask questions (Zeng and Watkins 2010) or different interpretation of terms, e.g. what the word 'idea' means in academic context (Zeng 2016, 433). Mismatches between expectations and students' actual efforts can foster harmful  stereotypes,  portraying  international  students  as  less  capable  or  engaged  (Arthur  2017; Heng 2016). Support must therefore address both the new environment and these differences.

Co-nationals are well-positioned to offer unique peer support, fostering confidence and belong­ ing  through culturally specific  empathy (Cao, Zhu, and Meng 2021; Mikal, Yang, and Lewis 2015), with peer advice benefiting international students more than domestic ones in boosting their confi­ dence (Dancer, Morrison, and Tarr 2014; Zou and Yu 2019). However, exclusive co-national groups were  argued  to  cause  isolationism  and  clique  formation,  limiting  engagement  with  the  broader international  community  (Taha  and  Cox  2014).  For  example,  non-European  students  in  the  US

often prefer co-national friendships over local ones (Glass, Gómez, and Urzua 2014). The role of conationals  in  peer  support  thus  remains  debated,  warranting  additional  evidence  (Wilczewski  and Alon 2022).

This inconclusive evidence may stem from international students' shyness in using formal peer support, often turning to online resources instead. Studies show they manage acculturative stress and depression  through  informal  internet  sources  (Nguyen  et  al.  2019)  and  maintain  their  preexisting social ties via social media, aiding acculturation (Yu, Foroudi, and Gupta 2019). With the rise  of  online  and  digital  tools  following  COVID  19  pandemic,  digital  peer  support  requires further exploration.

### Digital interventions

Historically, digital interventions in higher education were limited by the difficulty of implementing rapidly  evolving  technologies  in  this  sensitive  field  (Delahunty,  Jones,  and  Verenikina  2014). However, the forced shift to digital tools during COVID-19 offered new insights.

Digital  technologies  promoted  inclusion,  enabling  students  to  engage  confidently  from  the comfort of home (Tai et al. 2022) and fostering a sense of belonging for those previously alienated (Gravett and Ajjawi 2022). Digital peer support interventions enhanced self-efficacy (Han and Geng 2023) and reduced peer pressure anxiety (Huang 2023).

Despite these benefits, long term reliance on online tools like digital classrooms can harm selfefficacy and mental health, particularly when students lack alternatives (Aristovnik et al. 2020; Bar­ tolic  et  al.  2021;  Yaghi  2022).  Short-term  vicarious  interventions,  in  contrast,  show  promise.  For instance,  Walton  and  colleagues  (2023)  used  brief  (under  30  minutes),  asynchronous  (delivered online prior to the studies) interventions, featuring older students' university experiences, boosting the sense of belonging and retention. Similarly, social media videos made by student influencers fos­ tered belonging among peers (Raaper, Hardey, and Aad 2024).

This emerging research, though mostly focused on general student population, underscores the role of peer similarity. Short interventions' particularly benefit underrepresented and international students  (Walton  et  al.  2023),  with  co-national  guidance  aiding  comprehension  of  a  foreign  aca­ demic  system  (Raaper,  Hardey,  and  Aad  2024).  These  findings  suggest  vicarious  digital  peer support from co-nationals is  an  underexplored  avenue  for  enhancing  international  students'  selfefficacy, and a logical next step in this nascent stream of academic research. The next section inte­ grates these insights into a two-stage study.

## Empirical setting and methodology

### Context

This  exploratory  study  investigates  digitally  delivered  peer  support's  impact  on  academic  selfefficacy and grades among first-year international students in a UK university. We compare tailored digital  support,  based  on  students'  national  academic  backgrounds,  to  generalised  information about the international student experience to identify optimal peer support strategies.

Conducted at the University of Manchester, the study was open to students from any program, with  the  only  restriction  being  that  those  receiving  peer  support  held  no  prior  UK  degree.  Our study consisted of two stages: qualitative interviews in semester 1 and the quantitative experiment spanning semesters 1 and 2 of the 2023/2024 academic year.

In  the  first  stage,  second-  and  third-year  international  students  were  interviewed  about  their experiences,  challenges,  and  surprises  during  their  first  year  to  develop  digital  support  for  firstyear  students.  In  the  second  stage,  first-year  students  were  divided  into  two  groups.  Those  who could be matched with a current second- or third-year student received peer support tailored to their  national  academic  background.  Meanwhile,  students  who  could  not  be  matched  with  a current second- or third-year student received generalised support. The process and findings from both stages are presented in the following sections.

### Transparency and openness

Interview transcripts and data for both stages of the experiment have been made publicly available via the Open Science Framework. Access is available at https://osf.io/s4mdn/.

### First stage - interviews

The digital support videos were produced from structured interviews with current second- and thirdyear international students, edited to remove the interviewer's voice and display the questions on the  screen.  To  ensure  relevance  to  the  study's  theoretical  framework,  we  conducted  a  thematic analysis of the interviews before the experiment, establishing a connection between the stimulus and the experiment and increasing the internal validity of our study. Interviews were collected as outlined below and coded using MaxQDA software (Verbi GmbH 2025).

#### Interview participants

Twenty-four current international students from the University of Manchester (17 female, 7 male) participated  in  the  interview  stage.  They  represented  diverse  regions  (see  Table  1),  including countries where English is widely learned (e.g. India), or is the first language (e.g. Australia), ensuring language barrier was not the only determining factor. The sample included both male and female students from multiple programmes with several countries represented by multiple students for tri­ angulation and higher reliability (Dey 2005). Therefore, we do not expect further patterns to emerge from  additional  sampling  and  have  achieved  high  theoretical  saturation  (Glaser  2001),  with  the single downside of having no African countries' representatives.

The opportunity to participate in the study was advertised in second- and third-year classes, email lists, and noticeboards. Interested students completed an expression of interest form, providing their country of prior study and university email. Informed consent was secured before the experiment began, with the protocol reviewed and approved by the Proportionate University Research Ethics Committee at the University of Manchester.

#### First  stage results

While the recordings were made specifically for the experiment, analysis of the transcripts was con­ ducted to establish the connection between the stimulus and the experiment.

Two  independent  coders  used  MaxQDA  software  to  apply  axial  coding  to  the  interview  tran­ scripts,  guided  by  Telbis's  (2013)  International  Student  Self-efficacy  Scale, 1 designed  specifically for international students' self-efficacy (as opposed to general academic self-efficacy). This scale con­ sists  of  four  subscales:  Community  Acceptance,  Language  Ability,  Academic  Ability,  and  Financial Stability. It is important to note that despite its name, 'Language Ability' subscale measures students' confidence in general communication rather than pure linguistics as can be seen in Table 4, thus we refer to it as language and communicative component of self-efficacy. These concepts were used to identify key themes in the interviews, aligning with the experimental study's use of the question­ naire. We did not expect Financial Stability to be a prominent theme as we explicitly asked our inter­ viewees to focus on the academic side of their experiences to maintain the focus of our research. However,  that  and  other  categories  were  allowed  to  emerge  to  ensure  validity  of  the  analysis (Dey 2005).

To explore peer advice, we coded the difficulties and unexpected requirements faced by secondand  third-year  students  during  their  first  year  in  the  UK,  along  with  their  proposed  solutions. Responses  were  grouped  into  themes,  and  the  first  order  codes  were  derived  from  the  data. These  codes  were  then  compared  to  the  theory,  and  second-order  codes  were  identified.  Across the 24 interviews, 39 codes were identified   - 13 for difficulties and 16 for solutions (see Appendix B). Over the course of two conversations (pre- and post- initial coding), both coders agreed that no other codes arose from the data, therefore reaching thematic saturation, where 'no new properties of the pattern emerge' (Glaser 2001, 191). The intercoder agreement was high (80.43%) and a Kappa coefficient of 0.94, calculated using both assigned and unassigned codes to eliminate the possibility of agreement by chance, confirmed near-perfect, non-chance agreement.

Second-order codes identified were Language and Communication , New Academic Requirements , and the Pressure for Autonomy for difficulties, and the Use of Academic Resources , Engagement with Academic Community, Active Communication , Autonomy Development , and Psychological Adaptation for solutions. These align with Telbis's (2013) subscales of Language Confidence ( Language and Com­ munication and Active  Communication ),  Academic  Ability  ( New  Academic  Requirements and Use  of Academic Resources ), and Community Acceptance ( Engagement with Academic Community ). Pressure for Autonomy and Autonomy Development did not correspond to this scale, but reflected a prominent theme of autonomy and independence in higher education literature (e.g. Guay, Ratelle, and Chanal 2008), while Psychological Adaptation reflected the broader literature on self-efficacy (cf., Wilczewski and Alon  2022;  Yu,  Mak,  and  Bodycott  2021).  Financial  Stability  subscale  themes  were  absent,  as expected.  Of  eight  second-level  codes,  five  matched  the  topics  on  the  scale  expressing  students' lack of confidence in those areas and their gradual improvement, indicating strong thematic align­ ment between the stimulus and the outcome as we did not prompt specific topics to be covered in the interview other than limiting focus to academic elements.

We  grouped  second-level  codes  by  co-occurrence  in  the  same  interviews  to  understand  how second   -  and  third-year  students  overcame  new  difficulties  or  requirements.  Figure  1 illustrates key findings. Each interviewee discussed multiple topics, which is reflected by all codes having at least  one  co-occurrence.  The  most  frequent  combination  (with  15  co-occurrences)  was  students mentioning new academic requirements as a challenge, then describing solutions through engage­ ment with academic community, active communication, and autonomy development. For example, a Malaysian student mentioned that they were surprised that in the UK, 'we need to have a lot of critical analysis, especially in our assignment and our essays and even during classes' ( New Academic Requirements ) and then recounted how they addressed this by asking their academic advisor, being referred to and attending critical thinking workshops ( Engagement with Academic Community ). The reliance on social resources over purely academic ones (such as books or feedback) for solving chal­ lenges is notable, its implications discussed in the Conclusion.

### Second stage - experiment

The second stage of the research explores the benefits of digital peer support through a two-stage experiment.  First-year  international  students  were  invited  to  attend  two  lab  sessions.  In  the  first session,  during  the  first  semester,  participants  were  asked  to  fill  in  a  questionnaire  and  watch  a peer  support  video.  In  the  second  session,  during  the  second  semester,  students  were  asked  to fill  in  a  retrospective questionnaire.

#### Experiment participants

Sixty-six first-year  international students from the University of Manchester (40 female, 25 male, 1 non-binary/third gender, Mage = 21.95, SD = 4.92) took part in the research. Participants came from twenty-one  countries  with  twenty-three  countries  of  previous  education  (see  Tables  2 and  3). Four participants (all  female)  were  excluded  as  they  detailed  previous  experience  studying  in  the United  Kingdom.  After  exclusion,  there  were  thirty-three  participants  in  the  experimental  group and twenty-nine participants in the control group during session one, and thirty-three participants in the experimental condition and twenty-two in the control condition during session two. Informed consent was obtained from participants prior to the commencement of the experiment, and the pro­ tocol was reviewed and approved by the Proportionate University Research Ethics Committee at the University of Manchester.

#### Stimulus materials and procedure

Eligible participants were invited to the Behavioural Research Laboratory during the first semester. After being informed of what was required within the two sessions and being given the opportunity to  ask  questions,  participants  completed  a  two-stage  questionnaire.  Stage  one  contained  demo­ graphic  information -  age,  gender  identity,  country  of  origin,  and  previous  country  of  study. Stage two contained a five-point Likert scale questionnaire in which participants rated statements relating to four self-efficacy constructs: community acceptance rates (C1), language ability (C2), aca­ demic ability (C3), and confidence 2 (C4). C1 (internal consistency of the scale α = 0.88), C2 ( α = 0.9), C3 ( α = 0.5) and C4 ( α = 0.6) were taken from International Students' Self-Efficacy Scale (Telbis 2013, 64).

Upon completion of the questionnaire, participants were instructed to watch a pre-loaded inter­ view with a current international student. The experimental condition watched their assigned inter­ view (i.e. an interview with a current student who shared their country of previous study) whereas the  control  condition  watched  generalised  student  experience  interviews  addressing  the  same themes. 3 Students  could  watch  the  video  repeatedly  and  were  provided  a  notebook  and  pen  to take  notes.  Once  participants  were  finished,  they  were  reminded  of  what  was  required  in  the second session and booked a time slot to attend.

The  second  session  took  place  during  the  first  week  of  the  second  semester,  and  participants returned to the Behavioural Research Laboratory. After being reminded of what the session required and being given the opportunity to ask questions, participants completed another two-stage ques­ tionnaire. Stage one required participants to provide their summative and formative grades for the semester. This required participants to provide the name of all the course units taken within the first semester and the numeric scoring of their grade provided on Blackboard. They were also required to provide the maximum scoring possible for the unit to allow us to standardise the grades to account for  differences between course unit grading. Stage two required participants to answer the Likert scale questionnaires again, this time retrospectively about their experience thus far. On completion of the task, participants were debriefed, thanked, and reimbursed with a £10 Amazon voucher.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our research revealed that asynchronous vicarious exposure to co-national peers' experiences sig­ nificantly improved the communicational component of self-efficacy (language confidence) in inter­ national students, offering key implications for existing theories.

First,  we  expand  peer  support  literature  by  showing  that  asynchronous  vicarious  peer  support from co-nationals improves the language and communication component of international students' self-efficacy. This illuminates the under-explored role of vicarious self-efficacy cultivation (Bandura 1977;  Schunk  and  Mullen  2012).  By  isolating  the  vicarious  component  from  enactive  one,  we observe its stand-alone effectiveness, with peer similarity being pivotal. While we started with the assumption  of  vicarious  peer  support  being  an  independent  mechanism,  the  significant  increase in  language  confidence  element of  self-efficacy  combined  with  lack  of  impact  on  other  elements of self-efficacy and grades, positions peer support as a 'stepping stone' to broader support and out­ comes  (Hommes  et  al.  2012;  Wilcox,  Winn,  and  Fyvie-Gauld  2005).  The  limited  scope  of  results, though  unexpected,  aligns  with  the  gradual  nature  of  self-efficacy  development  often  spanning years (cf.,  Brouwer et al.  2016;  Gutzweiler,  Pfeiffer,  and In-Albon  2022).  However, our study  estab­ lishes  that  brief  vicarious  interventions  can  be  an  important  first  step  in  academic  self-efficacy cultivation.

Second, we address calls to clarify co-nationals' role in international students' studies (Wilczewski and Alon 2022). While prior studies highlight the positive impact of local student interaction (Wu, Garza, and Guzman 2015; Zhang and Goodson 2011), our findings reveal co-nationals' critical role for international students beyond informal friendships (Glass, Gómez, and Urzua 2014). Their experi­ ences of transitioning from one educational system to another are a unique, untapped source of selfefficacy cultivation. This is vital for understanding the role of co-nationals and international students' experiences:  even  those  from  same-language  countries  need  to  adjust  to  new  academic  require­ ments (Zeng 2016; 2021), and co-nationals prove essential in addressing these.

Third,  we  contribute  to  emerging  research  on  asynchronous  and  digital  delivery  of  academic support (Han and Geng 2023; Huang 2023; Walton et al. 2023) by demonstrating its effectiveness for co-nationals' self-efficacy. The first part of our study revealed that students tackled new academic challenges like understanding grades and managing workload through active communication and engagement with the academic community (see Figure 1). The experimental part confirmed com­ munication  as  the  self-efficacy  dimension  that  was  improved  post-intervention,  opening  new avenues  for  digital  peer  support  research.  Future  studies  can  investigate  how  and  if  the  content of digital peer support affects its effectiveness, leveraging flexibility and accessibility of online deliv­ ery  (Raaper,  Hardey,  and  Aad  2024).  Such  studies  can  further  manipulate  the  treatment  by  using more  semi-structured  interviews,  following  up  on  the  interviewees'  experiences  of  activities identified  in  this  study  (Appendix  B).  Alternatively,  this  can  occur  through  interview  questions (Appendix A) relating to self-efficacy dimensions for which we did not find significant results (e.g. community  acceptance).  Furthermore,  changes  can  be  made  to  the  medium:  for  example,  to written stories, courses, or social media posts (cf. Walton et al. 2023). The creation of different treat­ ments while applying a similar questionnaire tool to measure impact will help establish the role of vicarious intervention content in its effectiveness.


<!-- p:13 -->


This study has limitations. First, without observations, we cannot confirm whether first-year stu­ dents actively followed their peers' example of active engagement (see Figure 1) or only felt more confident in doing so. Existing literature does not provide a sequential view of self-efficacy cultiva­ tion mechanisms, often considering them simultaneously (e.g. Borgonovi and Pokropek 2019; Jääs­ kelä  et  al.  2017).  Thus,  future  studies  could  clarify  vicarious  peer  support's  role  among  other approaches of self-efficacy improvement.

Second, this  exploratory  study  analysed  the  effect  of  digital  asynchronous  peer  support  on  all first-year international students, with balanced control and experiment groups by gender, country of origin, and previous education (see Tables 2 and 3). However, differences may arise from study subject,  language  proficiency,  culturally  different  regions,  level  of  studies,  or  combinations  of these  factors  (cf.,  Rosenberg-Kima  et  al.  2010;  Sawtelle,  Brewe,  and  Kramer  2012;  Wang  et  al. 2023) that future studies can shed light on through multivariate analysis. While aiming to suggest a  more  accessible  peer  support  mechanism,  self-selection  affected  our  sample,  notably  through the absence of African international students among interviewees and a relatively small sample of experiment participants, limiting generalisability of our study (Dey 2005). While we could not fully resolve  the  reduced  engagement  paradox,  there  are  two  promising  approaches  we  can  identify based on our results and existing literature.

The first approach would require a targeted engagement of existing international students. Our sample  included  countries  from  across  the  cultural  dimensions'  spectrum  (cf.  Hofstede,  Yan,  and Michael  2010),  and  the  interviewees  were  very  enthusiastic  to  participate.  Thus,  our  study  does not  suggest  cultural  differences  were  the  deciding  factor  behind  the  lack  of  engagement,  but likely had an indirect effect similar to what was described by Preuß, Zimmermann, and Jonkmann (2025). Their study found female STEM students from North Africa and Middle East showed lower levels  of  belonging  in  a  German  university  than  males  from  the  same  regions,  but  the  regional effect was only significant in conjunction with gender. Therefore, future research can address the reduced engagement paradox by adopting a more fine-grained approach, designing interventions targeting specific subgroups of co-nationals or specific students from those groups to act as 'influen­ cers' (e.g. Raaper, Hardey, and Aad 2024).

Another  approach  to  resolve  the  reduced  engagement  paradox  is  through  understanding  the role  of  vicarious  improvement  of  self-efficacy  in  the  'big  picture'  of  peer  support.  Our  findings provide a short-term perspective, showing that students felt more confident communicating after hearing about their peers' experiences (see C2 items in Table 4). However, conversations and infor­ mal requests for help are only a small part of the university support system (cf. Nguyen et al. 2019), which was outside the remit of our study. While we suggest that vicarious intervention can be the first step in seeking other support as it gives students confidence to communicate with peers, longi­ tudinal studies are required. Such studies will allow for exploration of whether international students are more willing to seek other support after a vicarious intervention, thus overcoming the reduced engagement paradox.

Finally, our study showed no significant results for self-efficacy elements not addressed by intervie­ wees,  suggesting  the  content  of  the  intervention  might  have  impacted  the  domain  of  self-efficacy affected. The lack of significant results in elements outside communication is consistent with the litera­ ture viewing peer support as a 'stepping stone' to other kinds of support (Brouwer et al. 2016; Wilcox, Winn, and Fyvie-Gauld 2005). As an exploratory study, we did not limit our interviewees in the topics they've  addressed.  However,  future  research  can  offer  insights  into  the  connections  between  the themes addressed by vicarious interventions and the domains of self-efficacy affected.


<!-- p:14 -->

These limitations emphasise the need for additional research into the effects of digital synchronous peer support on students' self-efficacy. As this was an exploratory study, it is especially important due to the possibility of smaller effect sizes going undetected. As the potential exists that subtle changes may not have been detected within this study, future research should draw upon these findings to guide additional  directions  of  investigation.  This  can  be  done  through  larger  samples  to  explore  the  null findings, through a more diverse sample to allow for deeper analysis of differences, and through multi­ stage studies to explore the process of cultivating self-efficacy throughout the academic lifecycle.

Beyond theory, our study yields practical implications. It highlights asynchronous peer support as a promising  avenue  for  overcoming  barriers  of  traditional  buddy  or  mentor  programs  like  shyness, incompatible  schedules,  and  time  commitments.  This  study  shows  that  co-nationals'  peer  support enhances  students'  well-being  and  language  confidence,  fostering  further  engagement.  We  also provide a potential route to a better integration of international students into the academic commu­ nity  by  helping  them  understand  new  requirements  and  avoid  feeling  othered  (Guay,  Ratelle,  and Chanal 2008; Macakova and Wood 2022; Wu, Garza, and Guzman 2015). By empowering international students  to  support  their  co-nationals,  educational  providers  can  foster  resilience,  confidence,  and integration, paving the way for transformative outcomes and deeper mutual understanding.

#### Appendix C - List of rules authors applied when standardising grades

The following rules were applied:

- (1) grade needed to be in percentage format,
- (2) if  the grade was not provided in percentage format, it was converted into that format,
- (3) an average was calculated when more than one grade was provided,
- (4) formative and summative grades were treated equally,
- (5) non-numeric expressions of a grade were converted according to the University of Manchester's grading system,
- (6) expressions of 'over N' were treated as one point over the expressed grade type.
