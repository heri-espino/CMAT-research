## ABSTRACT

## ARTICLE HISTORY

This article explores how academic relationships shape first-year university students' engagement with and perceived access to acquiring disciplinary content knowledge. Drawing on a reflexive thematic analysis of interviews with 25 students across 10 programmes and employing Self-Determination Theory as an analytical lens, the study examineshow relationships influencestudents'motivation, engagement, and sense of academic belonging. The findings reveal that relationships with lecturers, teaching assistants, and peers both facilitate and constrain the perceived access to disciplinary content. Lecturers often appear as distant stars - highly knowledgeable yet sometimes inaccessible – while teaching assistants act as guiding compasses, helping students navigate academic complexity. Peers function as anchors, providing support and belonging but occasionally reinforcing uncertainty and pressure. The analysis further exposes how the quality of these relationships shapes students' perceived access to disciplinary knowledge. ### Motivation and self-determination theory

While integration theories emphasise the interplay between individuals and institutions, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) adds a complementary psychological lens for understanding the significance of social and professional relationships.

SDT distinguishes between two types of motivation. Intrinsic motivation refers to engaging in an activity for its inherent enjoyment or interest. In contrast, extrinsic motivation involves engaging in an activity to achieve external rewards, such as recognition or praise. The theory examines how individual, social, and cultural contexts influence motivation (Ryan and Deci 2018), highlighting that humans are naturally inclined toward growth, learning, and forming relationships. However, this growth is contingent upon supportive environments (Ryan and Deci 2020).


<!-- p:4 -->


SDT identifies three fundamental psychological needs that must be met for optimal motivation and well-being:

Autonomy: The experience of self-determination and control over one's actions.

Competence: The feeling of mastery achieved through well-structured environments that offer appropriate challenges and constructive feedback.

Relatedness: The sense of belonging and connection nurtured through respect and care.

When these needs are satisfied, individuals are more likely to experience enhanced motivation and well-being. Conversely, when these needs are thwarted, development and learning can be hindered (Ryan and Deci 2018, 2020).

Numerous empirical studies across various countries have employed SDT as a framework to explore the role of relationships in HE.

For instance, a quantitative study by Beachboard et al. (2011) examined the importance of relatedness within learning communities among 2,000 American students. The study emphasised that relationships among students and with lecturers are essential to professional development and career readiness, highlighting that the dynamics within the formal learning community can positively or negatively influence academic growth. Similarly, Yusof et al. (2020) carried out a quantitative study involving 1,974 Malaysian students, focusing on their experiences of relatedness. Their findings showed that relationships with lecturers, friendships, and collaboration with peers – both academic and non-academic – are crucial for fostering a sense of belonging. In another quantitative study, Wild, Rahn, and Meyer (2023) analysed data from 2,662 first-year students in Germany to examine the correlations between SDT's three fundamental psychological needs and dropout risk. The results indicated that both relatedness and academic interest directly affect dropout rates, while autonomy and competence influence dropout rates indirectly through their impact on academic interest. This study further emphasises the importance of feeling connected, highlighting its role both for motivation and student retention.

Through SDT, these studies highlight the crucial role of relatedness in HE and reveal that strong relationships enhance students' social and academic well-being, their sustained engagement with their studies and drop-out rates across various institutional and cultural contexts.

Research using SDT, and specifically relatedness, aligns with research on belonging, which highlights the process by which students adapt into a new academic community. Studies indicate that when students feel a sense of belonging, their motivation and enjoyment of their studies increase; conversely, a diminished sense of belonging negatively affects their experience (Pedler, Willis, and Nieuwoudt 2022). The relationships formed during HE thus play a key role in developing an academic identity (Gilani and Thomas 2025), where the concepts of 'being, becoming, and belonging' are interconnected (Meehan and Howells 2019).

The study presents student engagement as a complex, multidimensional construct essential for understanding student success in HE (Bowden, Tickle, and Naumann 2021; Kahu 2013). Engaging students involves behavioural, cognitive, and emotional connections shaped by individual, structural, and institutional factors (Kahu and Nelson 2018). When students' interests align with supportive environments and relevant curricula, their engagement deepens, enhancing academic success and personal growth (Bowden, Tickle, and Naumann 2021).

Research indicates that SDT's dimensions enhance engagement (Rahimi 2024) and emphasise the importance of relationships and a sense of belonging (Kahu 2013). ## Method and analysis strategy

This article presents data from a research project at a Danish university that explored how first-year students encounter disciplinary content. The university consists of five faculties: humanities, business and social sciences, health sciences, natural sciences, and technical sciences. Twenty-five first-year students from ten study programmes across all five faculties participated. Ideally, each faculty contributed with two programmes, typically including both an 'applied' and a 'pure' discipline (Muller 2009). Most programmes provided two students for two rounds of group interviews; however, individual interviews were conducted when only one student per programme signed up for participation or when scheduling prevented group formation.

For this study, all 25 first-year students were treated as a single cohort, as no significant disciplinary differences emerged related to the project's analytical focus. Among the 25 participants, there were 13 female and 12 male participants. Most participants had taken at least one sabbatical year between upper secondary school and university, and their ages ranged from 20 to 25. Four had previously discontinued another HE-programme. The authors do not have information on students' grade point averages (GPAs) at entry, but they know that entry requirements varied across the included programmes: three required a GPA above 9.0, five required a GPA between 6.0 and 8.9, and two admitted all applicants. However, GPA was not actively used as part of the study's analytical framework.

The students were recruited during teaching activities and participated voluntarily. #### Interpersonal dimensions

In peer relationships, the academic and social dimensions are often intricately linked. While some students may not see the need for friendly relationships with their peers, for many, these connections are very important. A strong social network can foster a sense of security and belonging, motivating students to engage more actively in academic activities. However, friendships can sometimes impede individual learning. This is particularly true when the closeness of the relationship makes it challenging to engage in critically constructive academic discussions, such as providing honest feedback to one another:

'You do not want to raise your hand (...) and say, 'I did not understand what you meant, would you like to repeat that?'You do not want to put your friends in that position'(Stud15, 2nd semester).

Students frequently compare themselves to their peers, often viewing them as 'academic mirrors.' This mirroring can be validating, especially when students realise that others share their insecurities or struggles with the 'student experience.' However, this comparison can also be discouraging. When students perceive themselves as falling short of their peers' academic standards, ambitions, or ideas of what it means to be a 'true' student, it may lead to feelings of insecurity and pressure. ## Discussion

### Navigating changing role expectations

The analysis and metaphorical reanalysis reveal that students' relationships with lecturers, TAs, and peers may serve ambiguous functions depending on how students experience these relationships. This is consistent with previous research (cf. Theoretical Framework). These relationships can simultaneously foster and challenge students' sense of belonging to their chosen profession and field of study, while also influencing their perception of the acqusition of the disciplinary content. The reanalysis further emphasises that students must navigate a complex relational landscape, in which various individuals can impact them socially, academically, and emotionally, as they seek to establish their own positions within the communities relevant to their studies. This navigation occurs within institutional frameworks that present both opportunities and limitations regarding acceptable behaviours.

The specific study in which the students are enrolled reflects a distinct cultural and organisational context that they must learn to understand and adapt to. Consequently, the educational environment is not an open space with limitless possibilities; rather, it is defined by norms, expectations, and role models. The influence of relationships on academic development is not purely supportive; it is contingent on the context and the institutional expectations associated with these relationships.

### The ambiguous functions of relationships

Our findings build on and support existing literature (Franz and Paetsch 2023; Schaeper 2020; Tinto 2017), emphasising the crucial role that relationships with lecturers and peers play in the transition to university. However, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of how these relationships can either facilitate or hinder the acquisition of academic material, depending on specific conditions.

While relationships can both enhance and hinder the students' perceived access to acquire disciplinary content, students across various relationships tend to have similar expectations regarding how the learning community can support their academic growth. This is particularly evident in formal learning environments – such as lectures, class teachings, laboratories, and study groups – where students thrive in a culture that is friendly and critically constructive. In contrast, a negative relational culture can diminish motivation and impede the academic journey, leading some students to withdraw – either mentally or physically.

The findings demonstrate that social and academic dimensions do not merely interact but are deeply intertwined. The sense of security found in relationships, along with clarity regarding expectations, cultivates a sense that disciplinary content is accessible. Ultimately, relationships with lecturers, TAs, and peers serve a dual purpose: they promote both social and academic integration, but only under specific conditions. It is within the tension between support and challenge that students navigate their roles and develop academic and professional competence.

### SDT and academic anchoring

In line with SDT as articulated by Ryan and Deci (2000, 2018), our findings indicate that students are fundamentally motivated by learning, relationships, and a sense of belonging in their engagement with disciplinary content. Their motivation, whether intrinsic or extrinsic, is continuously shaped by their interactions across individual, social, and cultural contexts. SDT identifies three fundamental psychological needs that foster intrinsic motivation: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. This study primarily focuses on relatedness – the sense of belonging and connectedness – while recognising that experiences of autonomy and competence are also critical to enhancing students'motivation and engagement. These concepts are discussed through the perspective of relatedness, emphasising how belonging enhances the development of autonomy and competence.

### Autonomy in relation to relationships

Several students express that their relationships with lecturers and TAs significantly impact their ability to master disciplinary content and take ownership of their learning – experiences that resonate with SDT's need for autonomy. Particularly during the early stages of their studies, some students express feelings of uncertainty, feeling that they 'do not own the whole world yet' (Stud21, 1st semester). Supportive relationships help them gradually build academic self-confidence and mastery. For example, when lecturers clarify academic expectations and encourage open dialogue, students feel a strengthened sense of autonomy. Conversely, unclear roles or overly controlling communication can undermine this sense of ownership.

### Competence in relation to relationships

The development of competence is closely tied to relationships with lecturers and TAs. These individuals act as 'academic translators' and guides, providing appropriate challenges and constructive feedback. They help students navigate the curriculum and cultivate professional skills. When relationships with TAs are characterised by accessibility and recognition, students' sense of academic competence is enhanced. In contrast, distance and perceived inadequacies in professional communication can lead to demotivation and uncertainty about one's abilities.

### Relatedness

The sense of belonging and the ability to engage with lecturers, TAs, and peers are central to students' experiences as valid participants in the academic community. Consistent with previous studies, the study highlights the importance of relationships with both lecturers and peers for academic growth. However, we expand on this perspective by emphasising the unique role of TAs as mediators between lecturers and students. TAs not only teach disciplinary content but also create environments where students can test their understanding and develop more confident participation.

Findings reveal that students do not rely solely on relationships within their immediate academic setting. ## Conclusion

This study highlights the vital and multifaceted role that relationships with lecturers, TAs, and peers play in shaping first-year university students' engagement with and perceived access to acquiring disciplinary content. Through a thematic and metaphorical analysis, it has become evident that these relationships not only promote this perceived access but also influence its very nature, co-constructing the accessibility and importance of academic knowledge.

However, the findings also illustrate that while these relationships can facilitate students perceived access, they can also present challenges. Lecturers have the capacity to inspire and motivate; but overly complex communication or personal biases can create barriers. TAs emerge as essential figures, acting as both academic translators and relatable role models. However, their dual role may be perceived as a barrier, if their knowledge or demeanour is questioned. Similarly, peers contribute to reflection and a sense of belonging, but they may also introduce insecurities and performance pressures.

Utilising SDT as a framework enabled a deeper understanding of how relationships shape students' experiences of autonomy, competence, and, particularly, relatedness – the sense of connection and belonging – which is fundamental to motivation and academic engagement. In addition, the data indicate that relationships beyond the HE context have the potential to enhance the development of academic skills and a sense of disciplinary identity.

Ultimately, this study emphasises the critical importance of acknowledging the complex role that relationships play in the academic journey of first-year students. These connections should not be perceived solely as support systems; rather, they are fundamental components that facilitate academic accessibility and potential acquisition. It is essential for academic institutions to actively engage with the relational aspects of teaching and the transitional phases of university life.
