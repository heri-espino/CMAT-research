## Evaluation of mathematics support centres: a literature review

§ JANETTE MATTHEWS*+,TONYCROFT†, DUNCAN LAWSON* AND DAGMAR WALLER

†Loughborough University, ↓Newman University and §Institute of Mathematics and its Applications (IMA)

*Email: J.Matthews@lboro.ac.uk

[Submitted January 2013; accepted July 2013]

Mathematics Support Centres (MSCs) have been established at universities in the UK and a number of other countries, of which colleagues from Australia and Ireland have been the most prolific in publishing about their work. Their main functions are to address issues surrounding the transition to university mathematics and to support students' learning of mathematics and statistics across the wide variety of undergraduate courses. There is a growing body of research examining the operation and impact of MSCs. This article will review and synthesize available published research evidence so that informed decisions can be made about the value of mathematics support activity and the targeting of future funding. Evidence will be shown of the evaluation of MSCs in each of the following areas:

- the collection of data and the challenges that are presented in both quantitative and qualitative studies;
- analysis demonstrating MSC usage and activity;
- analysis showing the impact of MSCs on students, staff and the institution.

The article will conclude by identifying areas where further research would be helpful.

## I. Introduction

In the epilogue to the 2010 report Responding to the Mathematics Problem: The Implementation of Institutional Support Mechanisms, Kyle wrote:

... we see that the concept of mathematics support has not only become firmly embedded in UK Higher Education, but colleagues have moved on to gather data on the way students use such resources and look for optimal strategies for the delivery of this support, and this is perhaps the most convincing evidence of acceptance. Mathematics support came of age in the first decade of the 21st century. What might once have been described as a cottage industry now plays a respected and widely adopted role in Higher Education (Kyle, 2010, 104).

This observation reflected not only the growth in the number of mathematics support centres in higher education institutions both in the UK and elsewhere, but also that the community had begun to look into ways of measuring the effectiveness of mathematics support initiatives.


<!-- p:2 -->


Although there may have been some limited activity in the UK prior to 1990, it was during the period 1990–2010 when institutions publicly acknowledged that many students found the mathematical elements of their courses very demanding. The seminal report Measuring the Mathematics Problem (2000) recommended that prompt and effective support should be available to students whose mathematical background is found wanting' (Hawkes &amp; Savage, 2000, iv). Many universities responded by providing such support through mathematics support centres.

Whilst the term 'mathematics support centre' encompasses a wide range of provision, it should be interpreted in this article to mean a facility offered to students (not necessarily of mathematics) which is in addition to their regular programme of teaching through lectures, tutorials, seminars, problems classes, personal tutorials, etc. (Lawson et al., 2003, 9).

From 2005 to 2012, mathematics support in the UK was given significant additional momentum through national funding for sigma: centre for excellence in university-wide mathematics and statistics support, based at Loughborough and Coventry Universities. The sigma Network (http://www.sigmanetwork.ac.uk) acted to share practice, disseminate findings, and act as a stimulus to support activity in other institutions.

During the first decade of the 21st century there has been reform of the Advanced Level (A Level) and Advanced Subsidiary Level (AS Level) Mathematics curriculum, an increase in the number of students taking A Level Mathematics and a huge increase in the numbers taking AS and A Level Further Mathematics. However, despite these changes the mathematics problem' has not been solved as recent reports such as Mathematical Needs - Mathematics in the workplace and in Higher Education (ACME, 2011) have highlighted. The report states:

We estimate that of those entering higher education in any year, some 330,000 would benefit from recent experience of studying some mathematics (including statistics) at a level beyond GCSE, but fewer than 125,000 have done so. This places those responsible for many university courses in an impossible position. They cannot require an appropriate level of mathematics of their applicants and hope to fill their places, and in many cases they are unable to design courses with the level of quantitative demand that would be appropriate for their disciplines. (ACME, 2011, 1).

ACME's report addresses the full breadth of subjects studied in higher education. Focusing on STEM subjects, a House of Lords report Higher Education in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) Subjects states that there is still evidence of a serious skills gap between the mathematical skills of students when they enter higher education and those needed for STEM first degrees (House of Lords Select Committee on Science and Technology, 2012, 15).

Consequently, the need for mathematics support in higher education in the UK is likely to remain for the foreseeable future. However, the current financial climate may put the existence of some centres under threat. MacGillivray &amp; Croft (2011) note that a recurring theme in audits of provision is that centres often exist precariously and lack security. Although the audit of current provision in the UK (Perkin et al., 2012) found that the number of institutions offering mathematics support had increased substantially, it also found a few instances of a reduction in services mainly due to a lack of funding.

MacGillivray &amp; Croft (op cit) emphasize the importance of collecting and analysing information to provide evidence of the contribution of support centres to improving student performance and reducing attrition. Only a few centres have the resources to gather and analyse large quantities of data. These centres are making their findings known through discussion at annual conferences such as CETLMSOR held in the UK and the Irish Workshop on Mathematics Learning and Support Centres organized by the Irish Mathematics Learning Support Network and in journal publications. Furthermore, research into the effectiveness of mathematics support centres can be found in postgraduate theses such as Whitehead (1992), Perkin (2007), Liston (2008), Symonds (2009) and Patel (2012).


<!-- p:3 -->


It is therefore very timely to take stock of the research evidence that critically examines provision and tries to measure its impact on students, staff and the institution. The purpose of this article is therefore to review and synthesize published research into the effectiveness of mathematics support activities to enable informed decisions to be made regarding the development of such services and the targeting of future funding. Areas where further research would be helpful will be identified.

The method used was to conduct an online search of academic journals and conference proceedings during July 2011–July 2012. Doctoral theses were also consulted. University websites were searched for evidence of mathematics support centres and associated publications. Although there was evidence of mathematics support in countries other than the UK, Australia and Ireland such as South Africa, the USA and Germany, no evidence was found of evaluation of these services. In this review, literature has been omitted where the focus of the article is the establishment of a mathematics support centre and evaluation is not addressed. No attempt has been made to categorize particular studies in terms of rigor, academic excellence or good practice. Many papers referenced do not specifically evaluate mathematics support but have elements that are of interest. A method of referencing has been adopted which points the reader to the relevant page of a paper where appropriate.

### 2. Evaluation of mathematics support centres

#### 2.1 Principal sources

Although this article makes reference to a wide range of sources, the following studies, given in chronological order, are the principal sources for methods of evaluation of the effectiveness of MS:

- Action Research into Effective Student Support in Mathematics (Challis et al., 2004). This report from a Conference Working Group, co-authored by academics from several UK universities, considered how MS may be evaluated and evolved a methodology incorporating all current forms of evaluation of MS. The article does not report evaluation of any specific MSC or MS activity.
- Measuring the effectiveness of a maths learning support centre—The Dublin City University experience (Dowling &amp; Nowlan, 2006). This study addressed the effectiveness of the MLC by studying usage statistics, student feedback, and pass rates of at-risk students. The study found the Centre had made a positive contribution to student retention.
- Investigation of completion rates of Engineering students (Cuthbert &amp; MacGillivray, 2007). This Australian study found that students who used optional support for first year engineering mathematics available through the Queensland University of Technology (QUT) Maths Access Centre were twice as likely to complete their course.
- Justifying the Existence of Mathematics Learning Support: Measuring the Effectiveness of a Mathematics Learning Centre (Gill &amp; O'Donoghue, 2007). This study at the University of Limerick examines ways of measuring effectiveness suggesting metrics such as student numbers/ uptake, department/ college participation, independent reviews, retention/grades, research output, development and expansion, associated projects, and links with other centres.
- Mathematics Support—support for all? (Pell &amp; Croft, 2008). This study analyses data from first year engineering cohorts at Loughborough University and seeks to differentiate between students who would have failed had they not accessed MS, those who fail but do not access support and those competent frequent users who wish to do better. The authors propose a methodology for evaluation.


<!-- p:4 -->


- Towards a culture of data collection and analysis in mathematics support centres (Croft, 2009). Croft, using his experience at Loughborough University, highlights the challenges in evaluating MS. A classification is made of two types of evaluation—soft' measures such as usage data or student feedback, and 'hard' measures that are tangible measures of improvement in performance. The need to balance time and resources between student support and evaluation of services is emphasized.
- The impact of the mathematics support centre on the grades of first year students at the National University of Ireland Maynooth (Mac an Bhaird et al., 2009). This study considers the influence of the MSC on the grades of first year students who attended the centre. Students were identified as atrisk from prior mathematics qualifications or a diagnostic test. The centre was found to have a positive influence on the most at-risk students. However, a high percentage of this group chose not to attend the centre.
- Learning support and students studying mathematics and statistics (MacGillivray, 2009). This article reports an overview of mathematics and statistics support in Australia including evaluation, demonstrating the impact on grades and progression.
- The Origin, Development and Evaluation of Mathematics Support Services (Gill, et al., 2010). This study addresses the development of the provision of MS at third level (HE) institutes in Ireland and discusses how these services are evaluated.
- Evaluation of the University of Limerick Mathematics Learning Centre (Carroll, 2011) and reported in An innovative approach to evaluating the University of Limerick's Mathematics Learning Centre (Carroll &amp; Gill, 2012). This study sought to examine the impact of the MSC on students' mathematics education and their attitudes to mathematics. Qualitative and quantitative data are obtained through questionnaires administered to past or current users of the centre. Findings reveal positive indicators that demonstrate that the MSC improved students' confidence, attitudes, study habits and affected retention.
- Measuring the effectiveness of a mathematics support service: an email survey (Gillard et al., 2011). This study incorporates data from responses to an email survey from 21 UK institutions. Information is provided on the nature of MS provision with a focus on how universities measure effectiveness. It includes techniques for evaluation, and offers opinion on what makes an effective MSC.

#### 2.2 High level considerations

When considering evaluation it is important to be clear about precisely what is being evaluated and what a support centre is seeking to achieve. Whilst the aims and objectives of individual centres are broadly similar, they do differ in some details as the following examples show:

The primary objective of the Mathematics Learning Support Centre (MLSC) is to raise the level of student proficiency in the use of basic mathematical skills. Success in achieving this objective will help reduce student wastage rates and enable students to achieve better grades on their undergraduate programme. (Robert Gordon University) (Patel, 1998, 1)

To contribute to learning in mathematics by the provision of support, the creation of opportunities and the understanding of research to increase positive outcomes in student learning. (Central Queensland University) (Taylor, 1999, 3)


<!-- p:5 -->


To develop and provide academic programs and resources to enhance learner independence. (The University of Southern Queensland) (ibid)

To provide a pleasant environment where students can work, study and support each other. (unnamed institution cited in Lawson et al., 2001, 20)

To monitor the provision of mathematical support across UK institutions, endeavour to apply examples of good practice and develop innovative approaches to further enhance the service provided. (Cardiff University) (Wilson &amp; Gillard, 2008, 95).

Clearly, when examining the evaluation of MS, the individual aims and objectives of centres need to be considered. An example relating to usage statistics is useful. Many authors show high usage of their centres as evidence of success. In contrast, an aim of the MS at St Andrews is to seek to resolve students' difficulties in one appointment as far as possible.

For these [drop-in] models, a measure of success is the number of repeat visits by each student. By way of contrast, a measure of success for the appointment-based model is how quickly the tutor is able to "clear''the problem encountered by the student: this corresponds to a low average number of visits per student, at least in a given time period ... (Marr, 2010, 24–25).

In this case, a low number of repeat visits would possibly be an indication of success of the MSC. In order to show evidence of the effectiveness of MSCs, studies have considered their impact on different stakeholders. Gillard et al. (2011) suggest three specific perspectives, those of the students requiring support, the centre providing support and the host institution.

There are several problems associated with carrying out rigorous evaluation. Whitehead (1992, 163) highlights two issues—timing of evaluation and the voluntary nature of participation. The aims of MS may be long term and evaluation after a short space of time may fail to show effects that are significant at a later date. It is also difficult to quantify the support received as students choose whether to avail themselves of support and the extent to which they engage with the support offered. In seeking to determine the impact of support on module pass rates, voluntary participation may bias the data (Patel &amp; Little, 2006, 133). Even when studies are able to demonstrate that students who use MSCs outperform those who do not, it is often not possible to prove that the improvement is due to the activities of the centre (Lawson et al., 2003, 17).

#### 2.3 Usage data

Most MSCs are interested in knowing who uses their facilities and how often. The majority of published studies refer to usage data. Systems for collecting and recording data range from simple manual systems to those that incorporate automatic scanning technology, which has, over the years, reduced in cost and become more accessible.


<!-- p:6 -->


Data collected focus on details relating to the users, the support delivered and the activities conducted in the centre. Many institutions collect data on how users learnt about the MSC to inform their publicity strategies. There is little point in a MSC if potential users are unaware of its provision and, as many studies show, one of the on-going issues with MS provision is that there is a significant group of students who appear to need MS but do not engage with it.

Both paper and computerized records are kept. Data may initially be collected manually and then entered into a spread-sheet or database for analysis later. Depending on the details held (and this varies from institution to institution), it may be possible to determine:

- which individual students are using the MS service, and which departments/schools they are registered with;
- which modules and topics of mathematics they are requiring help with;
- how often they attend, for how long and when;
- what are the busiest times;
- which tutors are supporting which students, modules, departments and topics;
- what queries are being addressed;
- what equipment or resources are being used or are needed.

Lowndes (2003, 21) says, it is important to keep a log of student participation. Individual names are not required, but the numbers attending and from which programmes together with the specific topic/ area of advice are needed as this information can usefully enable further help sessions to be organized. Information may assist decisions for appropriate staffing and can provide important feedback to module leaders/teams with respect to appropriateness of module content or their expectations of prerequisite knowledge. Ní Fhloinn (2009, 95) points out the advantage of additional detail. If student details are retained, it will be possible to monitor student attendance patterns and will enable the performance of students who attend to be monitored.

Studies evidencing analysis from usage data will be discussed in Section 3.1.

#### 2.4 Feedback

As already mentioned, usage data may show who is using a MSC and what topics are being discussed, but it does not provide any indication of the quality or success of this support or insights into the student experience. A number of institutions collect feedback continuously and others at regular intervals. Studies employ various instruments such as questionnaires, face-to-face-interviews, observations of students and focus groups. Opinion is sought from several sources including regular users of MSCs, all students studying modules with mathematics content including non-users, MS tutors and module lecturers. In the sigma guide, Gathering student feedback on mathematics and statistics support provision - a guide for those running mathematics support centres, Green (2012) collated experiences from a working group of 15 UK universities. The guide gives advice and sample questionnaires.

Few studies have been conducted that examine the nature of MS across a range of universities. However, Mac an Bhaird et al. (2011, 51.12) report the development of a questionnaire by the Irish Maths Support Network piloted to students from six Irish Universities in 2009–2010. The paper-based questionnaire was sent to 22 higher education (third level) institutions during the second semester of 2010/11. Data from nine institutions who participated are being analysed and will be published.


<!-- p:7 -->


In summary, it is considered important to obtain feedback from MSC users and deliverers. Insights into the student experience of MSCs are obtained in the following ways:

- informal discussion with users and tutors—anecdotal evidence;
- comments collected when students access support;
- questionnaires ranging from simple to in-depth, distributed to both users of centres or all students in a year group, administered at various times, often at the end of academic years—on paper or by email;
- structured student interviews;
- observations by researchers of students using the centre;
- surveys and informal discussion with academic staff who may or may not work in the centre;
- reflective tutor logs.

In addition, data are sought from non-users of centres to gain further insights into MSCs, mathematical difficulties and reasons for non-use.

#### 2.5 Challenges to collecting data

Some studies report challenges that were encountered in both data collection and analysis. Usage statistics often under-estimate actual usage since student signing in is often voluntary (Croft, 2000, 440 and MacGillivray, 2009, 464). Ní Fhloinn (2009, 97) reports on the need to correlate qualitative data from student surveys with usage data as responses do not always tally—students reporting greater attendance than the centre records show. Computerized logging systems have been reported as compromising the intended informality of the centre (Woodhouse, 2004, 3).

Usage statistics do not capture the use of a centre's resources—either within the centre or those that are taken away (or downloaded) and used in private study (Croft, 1997, 16).

Data for comparative and longitudinal studies are often problematic. In some cases departments are reluctant to release information and in others it is simply not collected (Croft, 2009, 4). For example it is often impossible to correlate data with prior qualifications. The nature of higher education is such that there are so many factors that change on a year by year basis (for example, entry requirements, lecturing staff, student motivation, nature of assessment) and this causes severe problems for longitudinal studies (Hubbard, 1986, 250; Croft, 2009, 5).

Institutions often want to be reassured that MS is contributing to increased pass rates, however when a centre has been established for several years it is very difficult to prove that pass rates would be lower if it did not exist.' (Lawson et al., 2001, 23).

A final issue relates to time spent in respect of data collection and analysis.

With finite resources, there is always a balance to be met between the amount of effort expended on first-line support and effort expended on data collection and analysis. (Pell &amp; Croft, 2008, 169).

It will be seen however in Section 3 that despite these difficulties, the MS community has been able to assess the impact and effectiveness of their MS through rigorous evaluation.

#### 2.6 Evidence from internal and external reports

Several studies refer to the use of external reports as a source of data to demonstrate the impact of MSCs. An example given by Croft (2000, 442) is the External Subject Review (formerly Teaching Quality Assessment and now Institutional Audit carried out by the QAA) where reviewers have specific obligations to look at the quality of student support. He states that this evidence should be collected and summarized periodically. Croft (2009, 7) gives student comments on mathematics support in the National Student Survey (www.thestudentsurvey.com/) as another example of a source of data which could be trawled for comments to evidence the impact of MS.


<!-- p:8 -->


Parsons (2005, 4) at Harper Adams University College reports the use of engineering mathematics external examiner reports for this purpose.

Internal reports may also provide useful sources of data. Croft (2000, 443) gives the example of minutes from student representative boards, which may record students' experience of the MSC.

Although evaluation of this nature is not conducted by MSC staff, the literature shows that evidence of the impact of a MSC often refers to external reports, particularly as statements made are independent of the providers of the MSC service.

### 3. Analysis

The previous section showed ways in which data are collected and indicated some of the challenges. Discussed here are analyses to evidence the use and impact of MS.

#### 3.1 Analysis of mathematics support centre usage

MacGillivray &amp; Croft (2011) claim that as mathematics learning support is optional, usage data provide evidence that students and staff value the provision.

The fact that they come or seek advice is a measure of the extent of their needs and their perception that the learning support can help them. (MacGillivray &amp; Croft, 2011, 197)

This may understate the full extent of student need as it is known that many students who would benefit from support do not access it. MacGillivray &amp; Croft also maintain that continued use of mathematics learning support is a measure of student engagement.

Booth (2003) used attendance data to show the value of the MSC.

The success of the Centre can be measured by the amount it is used, which is significant. As time has progressed the Centre has grown from a small room used occasionally by a handful of students to the significant facility it is now with over 18000 student study visits and a further 12000 shop-visits each year. (Booth, 2003, 17)

Support centre usage data have been analysed in a variety of ways and for a number of purposes including:

- The total number of visits broken down by programme and faculty making it possible to attribute costs (Croft, 2000, 400–441) and to develop targeted initiatives (Marr, 2010, 26).
- Yearly totals to justify future funding (Staddon &amp; Newman, 2003, 22).
- Weekly number of visits demonstrating that visits were mainly assessment driven (Woodhouse, 2004, 3).
- Comparative student visit data to determine the impact on target students (Dowling &amp; Nolan, 2006, 52).
- Usage of different types of resource (Patel, 1998, 1).
- Determination of the busiest times of the week to allow more efficient use of staff resources (Ni Fhloinn, 2009, 95).


<!-- p:9 -->


In addition to analyses that show the usage of MS, many studies attempt to address the nature and impact of the support. These will be discussed in the next section.

#### 3.2 Analysis to evaluate mathematics support centre impact on student performance and retention

Whilst usage data may show that a centre is well-used and, by inference, valued by students, Pell and Croft (2008) point to a desire for more rigorous evidence.

What would be much more powerful would be quantitative data on how those who use the centre perform in their mathematics examinations, and whether failing students are those who do not make use of the centre. (Pell &amp; Croft, 2008, 168)

In this section studies are shown that use quantitative data to analyse the activities of the MSC highlighting impact on grades and retention.

Cuthbert &amp; MacGillivray (2007) report an Australian investigation at QUT of completion and retention rates of first year engineering students. They examined the effect of optional extra MS from the QUT Maths Access Centre. The study found that students who access support are nearly twice as likely to complete the course as the whole cohort and half as likely to discontinue engineering'(Cuthbert &amp; MacGillivray, 2007, 8).

In order to identify need and/ or to encourage the uptake of MS, diagnostic testing often takes place at the start of an academic year for first year students. Analysis of performance data with reference to the diagnostic test results and MSC attendance may show the impact of MS on progression and retention.

At Dublin City University (DCU) in 2004, a 15-question multiple-choice diagnostic test was developed and administered to target students. It identified at-risk students who were advised to visit the MLC. Dowling &amp; Nolan (2006) examined pass rates of students who were considered at risk of failing their first year in mathematics. The authors argue that comparison over two years of pass rates showed the MLC contributed directly to retention.

Patel &amp; Little (2006) provide evidence that diagnostic assessments with tailored mathematics study support can significantly increase module pass rates. At Robert Gordon University online diagnostic tesan    ong  ns ns s s sns    s sce Students are made aware of MS (one-to-one and group) in the Student Support Facility (SSF). Weak students receive an informal pre-support interview. The study shows a statistical review of pass and fail rates for students who received or did not receive study support. It finds that MS increases the mathematics related module pass rates to an above average level for students who lack confidence in their mathematics ability.

In a comparison of failure rates over four academic years for three mathematics modules, Gill &amp; O'Donoghue (2007) show a dramatic reduction in failure rates once the MLC opened. A diagnostic test is administered to identify students at-risk of failing and to prioritize the support services towards these students. A comparison of end of term grades for at-risk students was made for those who attended support and those who did not. It was clear that those who had attended performed better which Gill &amp; O'Donoghue considered a positive indicator for MS.

Robinson &amp; Croft (2003) describe an intervention that had the aim of improving retention rates among engineering students. All students took a diagnostic test on entry and were informed of support available through the MLSC. Students below a threshold were considered at-risk and were offered extra help. One group was offered group tuition and encouraged to visit the MLSC and a second offered support on an individual basis through the MLSC. Analysis found that students from both groups that accepted support performed much better than those who did not.


<!-- p:10 -->


Pell &amp; Croft (2008, 172) describe a study which sought to examine the impact of the MLSC at Loughborough University on the grades of first year engineering students over a five-year period. Analysis was made of attendance at the MLSC by module and by grade for each year. It was found that the MLSC was regularly attended by a significant number of engineering students (approximately 1 in 5). Making the assumption that MLSC attendance can only improve performance, they concluded that the small but noteworthy number who attended regularly but received a minimal pass grade would otherwise have failed, indicating a direct contribution of the MLSC to retention. In addition, the proportion of fail grade students seeking support was less than pass grade students indicating the need to develop strategies to target these vulnerable students. Pell &amp; Croft found also that the facility is used more by more-able students seeking excellence than the less-able avoiding failure.

In a statistical study to examine the influence of the MSC on the grades of first year students at the National University of Ireland, Maynooth, Mac an Bhaird et al. (2009) considered past examination and diagnostic test results when comparing grades of students who did/did not attend the MSC. They found significant evidence to suggest the MSC is making a difference to first year students who attend and has particular influence on the most at-risk students. They also found similar results in their study to Pell &amp; Croft in that there were a group of strong students who 'seemed not to be worried about failing, but were using the centre to improve their chances of achieving first-class marks' (Mac an Bhaird et al., 2009, 121).

Lee et al. (2008) describe linear regression models to predict first year performance on a UK engineering course. Data were collected from 133 students and included diagnostic test results, gender, prior qualifications, results and MLSC use. Some data were available from university records whilst other elements had to be collected via tests and questionnaires. Significant factors in the regression models were students' mathematics diagnostic test results, whether they had visited the MLSC during their first year of study and the number of statistics modules they had studied during Alevel mathematics. Apart from finding that a mathematics diagnostic test is a useful predictor of future performance, the models showed that 'students' marks could be improved by seeking help in the university's mathematics learning support centre' (Lee et al., 2008, 44).

Bamforth et al. (2007) describe a study where a pre-sessional mathematics course was offered to first year engineering students. It had dual aims—to reinforce mathematical competency and to encourage the on-going use of MS. Data are given for two academic year groups. The study sought to identify the impact of support though the examination of diagnostic test scores, final module marks and attendance at MS. Frequent attendance by those students who were less well-prepared mathematically on entry was found to have a positive impact. The study examined whether this positive impact is seen at the end of the second year of study and identified a drop in performance.

These studies indicate that diagnostic testing is used by many institutions to identify students who may need MS and to target the support. Engagement with MS has a positive effect on first year student grades leading to an increase in achievement and retention particularly for those students who are lacking in confidence. Two studies show that MS is also used by more able students seeking to improve their grades.

Lawson et al. (2002, 24) caution that evidence from studies that solicit views from MSC users may not be representative of the whole student body. They describe a study that interviewed 58 students from seven institutions who used the MSC. The research sought to determine students'views on the support centre location and opening times, on attitudes of support centre staff to problems students were encountering with mathematics and on good and bad features of the centre. They suggest that in particular, the views on the location and opening hours being suitable or not do not take into account the opinions of non-attenders.

Some institutions have initiatives that encourage MSC use. Patel &amp; Rossiter (2011) report on a strategy that demonstrates the impact of using the MSC. It sought to increase student engagement in mathematics learning. Administered through the mathematics and statistic help centre (MASH), it involved identifying individuals who needed extra support. Based on screening test results, an individualized learning programme with resources was generated. This early introduction to MASH showed an increase in the number of visits, positive student feedback and improved results. The initiative increased students' awareness and willingness to use centralized support.

Woodhouse (2004) reports analysis after one year of setting up a MSC at Nottingham Trent University. Analysis is shown from a feedback questionnaire that was sent to all students on their awareness of the MSC, reasons for non-use and other comments. About 159 students responded and nearly all written comments were positive. In addition, Woodhouse (2004, 4) also describes the nature of the students who used the centre, commenting that they are generally confident in themselves (although not necessarily with maths), well-motivated and well organised'.

Gillard et al. (2012) report a study at Cardiff University, which statistically analysed the relationship of self-reported student confidence and ability of students who attended the MSC. They show that students perceive the MSC to have significantly improved their confidence and benefited their ability. The study found gender differences and recommended further research into student expectations of tutors and what makes an effective tutor.

In a study conducted by Parsons et al. (2011), seven final year engineering students were interviewed to examine their experiences and confidence in learning mathematics. Using a framework to examine student responses, the study concluded that mathematics teaching and support staff should seek opportunities to improve students' self-confidence in mathematics as well as skills. Evidence for self-confidence arising from MS was shown.

In an attempt to take account of individual student differences when evaluating the role of MS, Patel &amp; Samuels (2009) reported on research in progress that was seeking to analyse the relationship between students' approaches to studying and the effectiveness of MS. A questionnaire was developed to determine preferred learning styles. No results were given in this article but may be found in Patel (2012).

Most studies examine the impact of mathematics support on first year students. Croft et al. (2008) report an action research study which resulted in extending the MSC provision to second and third year mathematics students. Evaluation showed peer support played a significant role in student motivation and confidence.

Solomon et al. (2010) report on data gathered from a qualitative study relating to second and third year mathematics students at two UK universities with developed MSCs. They report that initiatives aimed at addressing mathematical difficulties have evolved into collaborative working spaces which draw on student interaction and peer support.

Nzekwe-Excel (2010) describes a study at Aston University conducted via a questionnaire to determine the impact of a MSC. He concludes that 'it does not just help to develop students'


<!-- p:12 -->


mathematical skills but also re-models and contributes to addressing their negative experiences and perceptions about mathematics' (Nzekwe-Excel, 2010, 9)

Evaluation in Carroll's study (2011) at the University of Limerick established that

the mathematics learning centre improved students' confidence in their mathematical ability; improved their attitudes towards mathematics; helped to improve their study habits and played an influential role in preventing several students from withdrawing from college completely. (Carroll, 2011, 66)

In summary these studies indicate that MS has a significant role to play in the development of student confidence and attitude to learning mathematics which in turn impacts on mathematical performance.

#### 3.4 Non-users of mathematics support centres

As can be seen from the aforementioned studies, attendance at a MSC is associated with improved engagement, performance, retention and mathematical confidence. Highlighted in this section are studies that specifically seek to address why students who appear to need support do not attend.

The students who remain of concern are those who presumably knew that they were struggling and who were aware that help was available but who still chose not to come to the Centre and subsequently failed their maths module. (Woodhouse, 2004, 4)

In a study in 2001, Lawson et al. (2002, 24–26) attempted to address this group of students through interviews with students who did not use the MLSC (22 students from 2 different institutions). They were asked about their mathematics preparedness for their course, the attitude of teaching staff to problems with mathematics and about the location and the opening times of the support centre.

An in-depth study (Symonds et al., 2008; Symonds, 2009) was conducted at Loughborough University to identify why some students do not engage with MS. The first phase considered students who had failed a module in their first year and had never or rarely used the MLSC. Feedback was sought using individual interviews, focus groups and on the spot interviews across campus. The second phase was conducted in a similar way but the students targeted were regular users of the MLSC who had failed a first year module. Students were interviewed and presented with reasons given by nonusers for their lack of engagement with support to determine if these were barriers and how they had been overcome. Findings showed that successful students monitored and directed their learning availing themselves of MS when needed. Among those who failed and did not seek MS, there was a lack of awareness of need or they felt they had too many problems that needed addressing. A recommendation to encourage students to make the first step in seeking MS was made.

Research was carried out at the National University of Ireland, Maynooth by Grehan et al. (2010a) to determine why students do not engage with MS. It compared students who failed their first year with those of similar academic backgrounds who passed the first year and attended the MSC at least five times. Twelve students filled in a short questionnaire and seven were interviewed with open-ended questions. Analysis showed mathematical background was not the only factor in predicting future success in mathematics. Some students were unaware they had a problem or were unwilling to admit it until it was too late. They neither engaged with their programmes of study, nor with MS. They demonstrated different coping strategies to students who were motivated to engage to some extent.


<!-- p:13 -->


The successful students were willing to seek support, were more aware of their individual learning styles and developed strategies for overcoming embarrassment or fear through relying on and working with their peers. Failing students have more negative attitudes towards support.

In reporting further analysis, Grehan et al. (2010b) identified a fear category which inhibited engagement with MS—fear of failure, fear of the unknown, fear of being singled out and fear of showing a lack of knowledge or ability. This fear inhibited students in relation to peers or tutors and affected engagement and coping strategies.

Reporting on the Study Advice Service (SAS) at the University of Hull, Ireland (2006, 2) comments on methods that have been found to work to reach students who should be seeking help and are not. These include making students aware of who the support staff are through lecture attendance in semester one and making support as easy to access as possible including an email service, and departmental workshops.

In their study of the effectiveness of the MSC, Mac an Bhaird et al. (2009, 121) found that, in spite of evidence that attendance at the MSC had a positive impact on performance, the majority of students identified as at-risk from the diagnostic test and prior results did not attend the MSC and targeting these students was a priority.

### 4. Further research

This literature review has identified a significant number of evaluation studies that have taken place to evidence the use and impact of MS. As Gillard et al. (2011) say,

a wealth of information relating to mathematical support services is recorded but formal measurement of their effectiveness continues to be difficult (Gillard et al., 2011, 49).

Some areas have become evident where further research would be desirable, namely:

- reasons why students do not avail themselves of the support available and mechanisms that could be employed to increase engagement;
- identification of the barriers to recognizing that support is needed and accessing it before it is too late;
- for students who regularly visit the MSC, the contribution is making to their learning of mathematics. Would their studies be as successful if support were not available? Is support desirable and/or needed throughout their programme of study?
- investigation of what constitutes effective delivery of MS and the influence of gender differences as identified in Gillard et al. (2012);
- follow-up studies to determine whether the positive effects of MS continue beyond the first year as suggested by Challis et al. (2004, 64). These should examine progression and retention rates throughout all programmes of study and potentially examine the effect of increased mathematical confidence and success on non-mathematics modules;
- although evidence has been found through internet searches of MS activity in countries other than the UK, Ireland and Australia, little evidence has been found of evaluation of these centres. Direct contact has been made with some centres and it would appear that evaluation is limited and restricted to usage data or a justification in internal reports of the necessity for a MSC. International studies would provide valuable insights into the impact of MS on student learning and higher education institutions;
- a recommendation from Gillard et al's study (2011, 50) was to encourage the dissemination of good practice of the evaluation of MS. They sought the adoption of standardized measures for comparison


<!-- p:14 -->


across institutions. As the nature of MS varies so widely across institutions and local contexts are so different, this has proved difficult, but further investigation may be fruitful.

### 5. Conclusion

Ní Fhloinn says that

accurate evaluation of the operation of such centres is an important, but complex task given that centres should ideally be integrated into the overall learning experience of the student, complementing structures such as lectures and tutorials (Ní Fhloinn, 2009, 95).

She continues that it can be very difficult to establish that the MSC has been the key reason behind the retention of any particular student and that the aim of evaluation should be to ensure that the centre is operating as efficiently as possible, and having a positive effect on student learning, in particular for those students who are struggling with mathematics.

This article has reviewed the literature to date and found evidence of a number of studies that have attempted to evaluate the operation and effectiveness of MS. These vary in scope from quantitative analyses of MSC usage to in-depth studies into the impact on progression and retention rates, mathematical confidence and students' learning of mathematics.
