#### Performance assessment for undergraduate mathematics tutoring centres: an update to focus on diversity, equity and inclusion

Carolyn Johns a and Jessica Gehrtz b

a Mathematics Department, The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH, USA; b Mathematics Department, University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX, USA

####### ABSTRACT

Researchers have asserted that the success of an undergraduate mathematics programme includes the extent to which it attracts and maintains a diverse group of students enrolled in mathematics, serves minoritised students equitably and creates inclusive environments. Mathematics tutoring centres play an important role in the undergraduate mathematics programme; thus, they too should be held accountable to efforts related to diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI). A framework to assess the broader performance of individual mathematics tutoring centres has been developed, but this framework does not attend to indicators of DEI performance. The current work aims to fill this gap, building on the existing framework, to describe performance measures related to DEI efforts as part of a centre's overall performance assessment. We note that the existing framework includes the examination of policies and procedures used by the centre and thus is primed to explore structural barriers present in centre operations. We describe how mathematics tutoring centres can assess their DEI efforts and offer suggestions for indicators of success and need for change.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers have asserted that the success of an undergraduate mathematics programme includes the extent to which it attracts and maintains a diverse group of students enrolled in mathematics, serves minoritised students equitably and creates inclusive environments (Hagman, 2021). Mathematics tutoring centres (MTCs), also called mathematics learning centres or mathematics support centres, play an important role in the undergraduate mathematics programme; thus, they too should be held accountable to efforts related to diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI). The benefits of attending to DEI in the classroom are numerous, including increased sense of belonging and confidence as well as mathematics course grades and persistence in degree programme (Voigt et al., 2023). This work serves as a companion to Johns and colleagues' (2023) performance assessment framework for undergraduate MTCs, to include descriptions of measures and their interpretations which attend to the assessment of DEI. Performance assessments can take on many forms and serve many purposes. For example, a centre may want to conduct an assessment to justify funding or for internal improvement. Typically centres focus on the number of students served, student feedback, or impact on student grades (Mullen et al., 2024). However, Johns and colleagues argued for a much more robust assessment covering a variety of dimensions of performance, adapting Lee and Nowell's (2015) nonprofit performance assessment framework to MTCs. They argued Lee and Nowell's framework is suitable for MTCs by positioning them as similar to non-profits in that there are no income mechanisms, and they have broad and vague social missions (Akingbola, 2012; Wadongo &amp; Abdel-Kader, 2014). In examining the performance of an MTC with respect to DEI, Lee and Nowell's framework may be particularly useful as it looks beyond traditional measures to attend to measures which focus on the structural features of the organisation. Examination of a centre's policies, practices and norms enables the centre to analyse structural racism (McGee, 2020) present in the centre's operations and explore alternatives (Hagman, 2021).

We organise our work by each of the seven performance dimensions described by Lee and Nowell (2015) and subsequently by Johns and colleagues (2023) (Figure 1). The order of the dimensions can be seen as a fl owchart with each dimension impacted by those prior. For each of the seven dimensions, we describe its relationship to, and how it can be used to assess an MTC with respect to DEI. We also discuss how performance on each dimension point to potential changes for a more diverse, inclusive and equitable centre. This work focuses only on assessment and changes specifically related to DEI. For more on broader MTC assessment, see Johns et al. (2023).

In examining DEI, we align ourselves with Hagman (2021), who stated 'Diversity practices attend to the demographics of students in the [tutoring] programme (and the broader STEM programme), equity practices attend to the experience of those students, and inclusion practices attend to the full participation of those students' (p. 74). Regarding diversity, a centre might ask, do the students attending tutoring, and the tutors themselves, match the demographics of students enrolled in the courses being tutored? In terms of inclusion, Calabrese Barton and Tan (2020) argue inclusion must go beyond assimilation of minorities into dominant practices and power. Within an MTC, this entails tutors working with students in a manner that draws on the students' knowledge/experiences. With respect to equity, Gutierrez (2012) lays out four dimensions of equity, which we'll describe relating each to the tutoring context. (1) Access: Do minoritised students have the same access to tutoring as their peers? (2) Achievement: Do minoritised students see the same grade benefits from tutoring as their peers? Do they see the same change in confidence or belonging? (3) Power: Whose voice is privileged in the MTC? Whose thoughts are respected and valued? (4) Identity: How do minoritised students' identities impact their interactions in the tutoring space? Can students work with a tutor with a shared identity?

We emphasise that race/ethnicity is only one aspect of identity. Gutierrez (2013) notes that identity goes beyond ethnicity and a static moment in time. Rather, different aspects of one's identity may be more or less salient in different contexts and points in time. Students may face marginalisation with respect to gender, socioeconomic status, sexuality, first-generation status, disabilities, among other identities. In addition, students have intersectional identities; for example, Black females will experience marginalisation differently than White females or Black males (Crenshaw, 1991). Thus, within this work, we refer to DEI as it pertains to minoritised students from a myriad of identities.

## 2. Assessment framework

Johns and colleagues (2023) adapted Lee and Nowell's (2015) non-profit performance assessment framework to MTCs for the purpose of examining a single centre at a time. As described by Johns and colleagues, assessment is comparative to an ideal, similar centres, or an earlier point in time for the centre being assessed (Herman &amp; Renz, 2008). In this article, we aim to provide a DEI-focused companion to Johns and colleagues' work. We do not explore the application to specific existing centres but rather theorise on adapting the framework for DEI.


<!-- p:5 -->


The performance assessment framework is comprised of seven dimensions: (1) Inputs, (2) Organizational Capacity, (3) Outputs, (4) Behavioural and Environmental Change Outcomes, (5) Customer Service Outcomes, (6) Public Value Accomplishment and (7) Network and Institutional Legitimacy. As previously mentioned, these seven dimensions can be seen as a fl ow chart (Figure 1) with each dimension informed by those prior. In addition, Network and Institutional Legitimacy envelopes the other dimensions, impacting each. Similarly, a centre's mission statement influences all seven dimensions. It is important to note that organisational theory maintains that organisational structure is not ' one size fi ts all' and what works for one centre may not work for another (Laursen &amp; Austin, 2014; Lee &amp; Nowell, 2015).

We now outline the seven dimensions, delineating each dimension as it pertains to assessing centres for performance related to DEI. Using research and our own experience, we explore hypothetical performance on the measures and offer suggestions for change. As we move through the dimensions, we also describe impacts of previous dimensions on the current.

### 2.1. Mission statements

The guidepost informing performance assessment is the MTC's mission statement, which serves as the standard to which performance measures are aligned and held (Lee &amp; Nowell, 2015; Williams Jr et al., 2014). Leyva and colleagues (2022) argue that supportive-for-all instructional practices are necessary but not sufficient to create equitable opportunities and access. In addition, mission statements describing 'supportive-for-all' pursuits do not hold the centre accountable to DEI metrics. As an example, a centre mission reported by Johns and colleagues (2023, p. 16) states,

The mission of the center is to improve student learning and academic performances for all students in our calculus courses. We support instruction that carefully considers student thinking and engages students as active participants in their learning process.

This mission statement includes practices which generally support student learning but does not specifically address a desire for a diverse, equitable and inclusive centre. If centres find that their mission statement does not explicitly and sufficiently address DEI, crafting a new mission statement is a crucial fi rst step in upholding DEI as a priority. If it is not feasible to update the outward-facing mission statement (i.e. the mission statement posted on the website), we invite centres to write a mission statement for internal use that includes guiding principles focused on how they can strive to be a more diverse, equitable and inclusive centre.

The process of writing a new mission statement (or guiding principles) should include a range of stakeholders such as students, tutors, instructors and administrators (Meacham &amp; Gaff, 2006; Williams Jr et al., 2014) who are diverse across many identities. Involvement of administrators and instructors is key to ensure support for the centre's mission and activities (McDonald, 2007; Meacham &amp; Gaff, 2006). However, care should be taken to attend to who has power during the process. Gutierrez (2002) describes power as being held by those whose beliefs and perspectives are depicted in everyday society (generally white middleclass men). This power leads to the voices and opinions of white middle-class men being privileged over those from minoritized groups (Gutierrez, 2012). As such, centres may need to form multiple focus groups based on identity and/or university role, taking care not to simply defer to those with the most power.

### 2.2. Inputs

The performance dimension of inputs refers to how resources are acquired and used. These measures are generally reported as quantitative data or brief descriptions. It is important to note that resources are typically not controlled by centre leadership but rather upper administrators such as chairs, deans and provosts who hold power over the centre.

One of the most important inputs is the budget, including funding for tutors, which impacts all subsequent performance areas. Inadequate funding may lead to a focus on cost effectiveness, aiming to maximise the number of students helped. A mission describing only support for all would promote this practice. Given sufficient funding, a centre can attend to the specific needs of students with minoritized identities such as one-on-one weekly appointments where students can benefit from structure, individual attention, and get to know their tutor.

The physical space of the centre, and particularly the location, may also impact the inclusive nature of tutoring and equitable access to services. For example, Pawley (2019) described what is often viewed as the ideal student as 'White, male, between the ages of 18-22, lives on campus and lacks major obligations such as full-time employment or family care' (p. 24) and reports on structural barriers faced by minoritized students because of demands on their time. Students who live off campus, work full-time, or are responsible for family care may benefit from a location that is easily accessible from off-campus and between classes. In addition, a well-trafficked and well-lit area may help students feel safer and less avoidant (Rader &amp; Allison, 2009) of walking to the centre locations, especially after dark and on weekends. Further, Bjorkman (2019) noted the importance of a neutral space, such as a library or other course-neutral location, to create a welcoming environment. Students may feel more comfortable asking for help in a library location than a physical space embedded within the mathematics department offices. Bjorkman argues that a mathematized space may force minoritized students to assimilate into a space conceived for Pawley's ideal student, while a neutral space would be more inclusive to those who don't yet feel a sense of belonging in a mathematized space. For example, while transgender and nonbinary students see mathematics as useful and feel connected to peers, they have a lower sense of enjoyment in doing mathematics and belonging to the mathematical community (Brown et al., 2024).

Another input is the ability to recruit and hire a diverse tutoring staff. McGee et al. (2019) report poor retention of Black engineering students has been linked to feelings of 'being the only one' and that the demographics of faculty often fail to resemble minoritized students. In addition, Liera and Ching (2019) cite work demonstrating that minoritized students benefit from minoritized staff who ' curb stereotype threat . . . enhance sense of belonging ... and be someone with whom they can identify and more easily approach' (p. 112). However, at many institutions, instructors or graduate students hired by the mathematics department without input from the centre serve as tutors (Byerley et al., 2020;


<!-- p:7 -->


Byerley et al., 2024; Johns et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2022). While this reduces tutor salary costs, it may be problematic if this population lacks diversity. In addition, power dynamics and social interactions in the centre differ when tutors are instructors or graduate students versus undergraduate peer tutors (Bjorkman, 2019; Johns, 2019), and this may be particularly true especially for minoritized students.

### 2.3. Organisational capacity

Organisational capacity is the effectiveness of processes in using resources (inputs) along with an ability to innovate. Typically, the assessment of an MTC's organisational capacity will take the form of detailed descriptions of centre processes. Processes include the centre's hiring and training practices, procedures for student check-in, and marketing strategies. We highlight tutor hiring and training processes with descriptions of DEI best practices a centre might use for comparison. We also detail the importance of the ability to innovate.

Employee recruitment and hiring are one facet of organisational capacity. The salary, job description, application, work climate and tactics used to promote the position all impact an individual's decision to apply (Griffin et al., 2020; Raghunathan &amp; Murali, 2022; Saini et al., 2015). A competitive salary is important in attracting diverse applicants. Minoritized students are more likely to have fi nancial obligations (Pawley, 2019), pushing them to accept the most well-paying position rather than resume builders. In addition, when promoting the position, to mitigate feelings among minoritized students that the position isn't 'for them' , Hagman (2021) suggested intentional recruiting of these populations. This can entail advertising the position in campus cultural centres, using inclusive language and explicitly stating the centre's commitment to diversity in the posting, as well as ensuring images on the centre website and recruitment materials show diversity (Avery &amp; McKay, 2006; Cosgriff-Hernandez et al., 2022; Walker et al., 2012). The job description should include only the qualifications needed to perform the role (Cole &amp; Mross, 2022). For example, a centre may consider whether an applicant interested in tutoring college algebra, who received an A in the course, needs to have taken calculus 1 to be qualified.

Once students have applied for the position, centres must engage in equitable hiring practices. Liera &amp; Chang (2019) note the importance of implicit bias training for leadership involved in the hiring process. While this type of training does not eliminate implicit bias, it does ameliorate bias by bringing it to light. Before reviewing applications, a rubric must be agreed upon with clear criteria scored independently, and to be used at all stages of the hiring process (Carlson &amp; Zorn, 2021; Cole &amp; Mross, 2022; Cosgriff-Hernandez et al., 2022). These rubrics should allow for multiple forms of skills and talent beyond Pawley's (2019) ideal student, e.g. young single White male. For the interview, applicants should be provided with information on what to expect and should be asked a standardised list of questions (Griffin et al., 2020). In addition, the committee should consider offering online interviews to accommodate the needs of those for whom it would be a barrier to attend in-person, as well as ask the applicant if other accommodations are needed (Cole &amp; Mross, 2022). Finally, letters of recommendation or references should only be used after narrowing by all other qualifications to mitigate influence of implicit or explicit bias within the recommendations (Carlson &amp; Zorn, 2021).

To further understand aspects of the tutoring position or application process which either led to successful application or barriers to application for minoritized students, focus groups can be conducted with individuals who chose not to apply and left or moved forward at each stage of the hiring and employment process (Cosgriff-Hernandez et al., 2022; Hagman, 2021). Findings from focus groups may point to such aspects as the climate of the centre or the structural practices of the hiring process and employment which support or constrain a diverse applicant pool (Liera &amp; Chang, 2019).


<!-- p:8 -->


Tutor training must attend to practices, behaviours and attitudes which specifically support DEI within the centre and should be integrated throughout training. For example, in describing a research-based tutor training programme, Johns (2019; 2024), only briefly mentions an equity training session, which is set apart from the training curriculum. While days of training set aside to address DEI topics may be beneficial, tutors should also see DEI infused into all training topics. Important training topics include microaggressions (Crane et al., 2022; McGee, 2014; Seda &amp; Brown, 2021; Shah, 2019), implicit bias (McGee, 2014; Sue, 2017) and other forms of more overt racism (Solorzano et al., 2000) experienced by minoritized students within the mathematical and tutoring spaces. For example, Black students in the MTC may be presumed to be in lower-level courses, females may be asked fewer conceptual questions, and Asians may be presumed to be ' good at mathematics' . To support instructors in challenging stereotypes, Seda and Brown (2021) developed a 'have you heard' activity which starts with instructors adding statements they've heard (not necessarily believe) to posterboards labelled for different identities. Then, in groups, stereotype statements are rephrased. Activities like this can be adapted for tutors.

In addition, training needs to go beyond a focus on individual racism to address structural racism and barriers. For example, integrated into tutor training on active learning can be specific practices to engage minoritized students (Kress, 2024). For example, normalising not knowing, specifically with prior content (Kress, 2024), and demonstrating to students that their questions and contributions are valued (Leyva et al., 2022). In addition, training on student beliefs can include the role of perceived competence for minoritized students. Students with low confidence may rely on tutors to validate correctness, avoid challenging problems they think they can't accomplish, and be less willing to answer questions for fear of being dumb (Seda &amp; Brown, 2021). Without training on causes of these behaviours, tutors may assume these students are ' difficult' and want the tutor to do all the work. Tutors should engage in training which addresses the recognition of low confidence, reasons for the behaviour, and how to support students in gaining confidence. Growth mindset is one factor contributing to students' mathematical confidence (Yeager &amp; Dweck, 2012). Research suggests encouraging students that they can be successful, positioning mistakes as normal, praising effort and correct aspects of work and promoting sensemaking all increase growth mindset (Samuel &amp; Warner, 2021; Seals &amp; Valdiviejas, 2021; Siegle &amp; McCoach, 2007; Sun, 2018). Johns (2024) describes a professional development programme that trains tutors on these student beliefs and productive struggle through group discussions on (1) responding to students who exhibit unproductive beliefs, (2) the importance of and student barriers to student engagement in productive struggle and (3) the ways in which tutors promote/have room for improvement in encouraging productive struggle in tutoring excerpts. An example of a student exhibiting an unproductive belief could be ' A student says they don't understand a problem and pushes their paper toward you' . Tutors could identify that the reason behind this behaviour is low confidence or a belief that they should see how to do a problem right away. A tutor could respond to the student by reassuring them and either starting with a broad question about what they recall from class or helping them break down the problem into smaller pieces that they can solve. Encouraging students to engage in struggle, validating their questions, offering praise and encouraging the student to engage in the challenging aspects of mathematical work all send a message that their contributions are valid.


<!-- p:9 -->


Cliffe et al. (2020) also note the importance of providing resources and training for tutors regarding accessibility barriers for disabled students. Disabilities include mental health conditions, specific learning difficulties, social/autism spectrum, visual impairment, hearing impairment, physical impairments and chronic health conditions. Cliff and colleagues argue that the number of students reporting disabilities has been increasing, yet only 27% (17) of the 63 MTCs they surveyed offered special training and only 45.5% (22) of 62 respondents had special provisions for working with disabled students. They offer recommendations such as providing tutors with resources and training regarding signs of specific disabilities and the best ways to work with those students. Croft and Grove (2011) and Trott (2003) offer suggestions for training tutors on specific learning differences, including dyslexia and dyscalculia.

We note that the amount of time dedicated to training tutors is dependent on inputs of funding and leadership time. For example, Mills and colleagues (2022) found that most centres in their survey of 75 undergraduate MTCs in the USA would like to offer more training if they had the resources, and the centres valued pre-made materials. This points to constraints of limited resources available to centres to innovate. Centres may explore existing training materials aimed at STEM instructors for adaptation, such as the Inclusive STEM Teaching Project (2020).

Finally, capacity to innovate, constrained by inputs of centre leadership time and budget, dictates the scope of changes a centre can make to support DEI initiatives. Leadership given insufficient time to dedicate to the centre may spend their time focusing on the day-to-day management of the centre, while centres with higher leadership time may have greater ability to innovate and enact change. For example, Mills and colleagues (2022) found that MTCs with more dedicated management time tended to use more centre evaluation methods and spend more time on training. Innovation and change are impacted by the ability to develop, carry out and assess DEI change initiatives. Sufficient leadership time is also important for developing relationships with stakeholders (Lewis, 2007) as lack of broader academic/organisational support may hinder change efforts (McNeill et al., 2024).

### 2.4. Outputs

Outputs are the countable goods and services of a centre and are among the most common measures used to demonstrate success in MTCs (Mills et al., 2022), although they are explained by other performance dimensions. Most commonly, centres present counts of the number of students served to show usage (Croft, 2009; MacGillivray &amp; Croft, 2011), but these numbers are often reported in aggregate across all demographics (e.g. Byerley et al., 2024; Dowling &amp; Nolan, 2006; Gill &amp; O'Donoghue, 2007; Johns et al., 2023). Disaggregation of this data is important to show how the centre is or isn't successfully serving minoritized students. Like Hagman (2021), we assert that the students attending the centre should be as diverse as the students enrolled in the courses supported. Comparing the percentages of minoritized students in courses supported to the percentages attending tutoring provides a marker for diversity and inclusion efforts. To better understand quantitative output data, a centre needs to examine customer service feedback, discussed later, to aid in identifying which organisational capacity and input areas are influencing outputs. For example, a centre may fi nd the proportion of minoritized students in Course A who have attended tutoring is lower than the proportion enrolled. Student feedback could indicate changes are needed to marketing processes, an aspect of organisational capacity.


<!-- p:10 -->


In addition to counts of students, there are several input-to-output ratio measures that can be used to assess performance (Byerley et al., 2020; 2024; Johns et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2022). These include cost per visit (using tutor salaries), tutor hours worked per student visit and utilisation of facility space. Even in aggregate, these numbers can indicate potential concerns for a centre's inclusive pedagogical practices. For example, tutors may have excellent training (organisational capacity) in engaging students in active learning, a pedagogical strategy that supports undergraduate mathematics learning (Freeman et al., 2014). However, when an MTC is busy (low tutor to student ratio), tutors have been found to resort to lecturing (Johns, 2019; Johns et al., 2023). While using predictive modelling of centre attendance for staffing can reduce wait time (Howard &amp; Cronin, 2022), an input such as insufficient funding for tutors can negate good organisational capacity.

While ratio measures may be challenging to report disaggregated, if a centre is setup with different rooms by course, it may be possible to glean comparisons. For example, if students enrolled in Room A courses have a greater proportion of minoritized students than Room B, the ratio of tutor hours worked to eligible student in each room can be compared. Equitable access to tutoring would look for similar measures between the two rooms. In addition, if Room A has fewer tutor hours worked per student visit than Room B, this indicates students are able to spend less time with a tutor and/or have a longer wait in Room A than in Room B.

Other outputs include the number of hours open, services provided and courses served. To meet the needs of minoritized students, MTCs may need to restructure and expand in these areas. For example, Hagman (2021) suggested minoritized students who may work full-time could benefit from expanded weekend and night hours, as well as expanded services such as online tutoring. Minoritized students may also benefit from additional services to complement drop-in tutoring such as one-on-one appointments which offer students increased privacy, recurring one-on-one appointments which offer stability to students balancing multiple responsibilities, small group tutoring built around affinity groups, or tutoring offered in campus cultural centres. One-on-one appointments may be particularly important to minoritized students who may fear being labelled as 'not smart' because of others' perception of their effort (Dringenberg et al., 2022) and/or identity and avoid seeking help (McGee et al., 2019). This is experienced by Black and Brown students who are stereotyped as lacking smartness, as well as Asian students who are stereotyped as being smart (Lee et al., 2020; Shah, 2019). In addition, within classrooms (or in group tutoring scenarios), minoritized students may face inequitable participation based on power dynamics (Leyva et al., 2024; McPherson, 2017; Schenkel &amp; Calabrese Barton, 2020), microaggressions from peers and instructors (Lee et al., 2020; McPherson, 2017; Miller et al., 2021) and feelings of isolation experienced by being one of a few minoritized students (Lee et al., 2020; McGee et al., 2019). Small group tutoring offered by affinity group or tutoring in campus cultural centres may mitigate these experiences within tutoring.

### 2.5. Outcomes: behavioural and environmental changes

Behavioural and environmental change outcomes relate to the impact the centre intends to have on the students it serves and is generally reported as comparative quantitative data. For MTCs, the intended outcome is to generally improve course grades, which may come with residual intentions to increase graduation rates and retain STEM majors. Centres may examine the correlation between centre attendance and course grade and/or correlations between centre attendance and retention in STEM majors (Cooper, 2010; Cronin et al., 2021; Mills et al., 2022; Perez et al., 2021).

Other intended outcomes may include impact on feelings of belongingness and selfefficacy which can be examined as pre/post-test measures. Sense of belonging has been tied to degree completion for low-income, underrepresented STEM students (Hansen et al., 2024) and STEM degree retention for women and students of colour (Rainey et al., 2018). Self-efficacy, sometimes used interchangeably with confidence, is the set of beliefs about one's personal competence relevant to a specific context, such as mathematics (Maddux, 1995). Confidence has been associated with higher mathematics grades (Parsons et al., 2009), STEM persistence in African American and Hispanic college students with disabilities (Cardoso et al., 2013) and differences in self-efficacy have been found between gender and ethnicity (Wilson et al., 2015). There are several validated instruments available for measuring sense of belonging, self-efficacy and confidence in mathematics at the tertiary level (e.g. Good et al., 2012; Langenfeld &amp; Pajares, 1993; Sidney et al., 2024).

A mission statement (or guiding principles) attending to DEI would call for examining these outcomes in disaggregate. For example, Lord and colleagues (2011) note the importance of disaggregating data by both race and gender to understand STEM retention. By disaggregating data, centres can examine who is benefiting by examining questions such as, do students see the same increase in course grade per visit to the centre or the same increase in sense of belonging when comparing across identities? If the answer to these questions is no, then the centre should look back to its outcomes, organisational capacity and inputs to determine why these outcome measures are the case and where to make improvements. Customer service outcomes, discussed next, also shed light on the 'why' of these measured outcomes.

### 2.6. Outcomes: client satisfaction

Client satisfaction outcomes are the extent to which the services meet the needs of students and the student's satisfaction with their MTC experience. Client satisfaction can be measured with both quantitative and qualitative data. Common quantitative measures for client satisfaction include the percent of eligible students using the centre, the number of visits per student using the centre, and percent of students attending only once (Byerley et al., 2024; Johns et al., 2023). The fi rst two measures indicate whether a centre is getting students in the door by being effectively marketed (organisational capacity) and meeting student needs (output). The percent of students who only attend tutoring once indicates whether students are satisfied with the service they were provided, which is impacted by inputs, organisational capacity and outputs. For example, if tutors aren't sufficiently hired and trained based on working with a diverse population, students may experience microaggressions, which lead to a poor experience. These microaggressions may be identity or mathematics related. Mathematical microaggressions occur when an instructor, or tutor, uses phrases such as 'you just' , or 'it' s simple' (Su, 2015). Su reports that these phrases discourage students by sending the message that students should see how to do things right away.


<!-- p:12 -->


Additional qualitative data is key to understanding why students are/aren't attending or returning to tutoring. The most common way centres collect qualitative data is through student surveys with student interviews sometimes being used (Mills et al., 2022). For example, Perez et al. (2021) surveyed students attending their centre with questions such as 'tutor hours met my needs' , 'The tutoring session(s) helped me grasp the material' and 'the tutoring session(s) improved my confidence in math' . Student focus groups with minoritized students and disaggregated data are two means of better understanding the experience of minoritized students as they interact with the centre. Hagman (2021) called for the use of focus groups in many different contexts within departmental DEI efforts and urges for an anti-deficit view of students' experiences in conducting the focus groups. For example, it' s important to ask minoritized students who do use the centre, what has been most helpful and keeps them coming back? For students who don't use the centre, ask, what is preventing them from using it? What is preventing them from returning? These questions should focus on the systemic barriers faced, such as lack of evening or online tutoring, rather than on student deficits such as inability to attend in-person tutoring during the day. In what ways is tutoring not working for them. These focus groups should include a diverse group of minoritized students, attending to intersectionality among identities. These fi ndings can be used to explain behavioural and environmental change outcomes. For example, Seda and Brown (2021) assert children of colour are less likely to be asked challenging conceptual questions or be engaged in active learning than their white peers. If minoritized students describe this as their experience in tutoring, they may not see the same grade or affective gains as their counterparts. Here again, purposeful hiring and training can be used to promote engaging minoritized students in ways that position these students as just as capable and that are productive for their learning.

### 2.7. Public value accomplishment

Public value accomplishment is the value an MTC produces on the community/society which goes beyond customer service and behavioural/environmental outcomes for individuals and specific services (Lee &amp; Nowell, 2015). Moore (2007) argues measuring public value is important '(1) to meet demands for external accountability; (2) to establish a clear, significant mission and goal for the organization and (3) to foster a strong sense of internal accountability' (p. 97). Examples of public value include well-being/quality of life, social capital and equality (Hill &amp; Sullivan, 2006). We argue all three of these values apply to MTCs. Gholson and Wilkes (2017) state 'personhood in the U.S. context is granted-at least in part-through mathematical knowing and doing' (p. 231). Similarly, Moses and Cobb (2001) argue mathematics is a civil rights issue, necessary for ' economic access and full citizenship' (p. 5). Through their tutoring services, MTCs can aim not only to support minoritized students in their immediate coursework, but also their ongoing academic and career goals which may be impeded by performance in required mathematics coursework. Surveys and focus groups with individuals across the entire university community, including individuals such as parents and university donors, can indicate whether an MTC is perceived as providing this value. In particular, an MTC can interview students at various stages to examine how the MTC impacts minoritized students' STEM persistence, including their careers post-graduation. Quantitative data could also be used to similarly measure the retention of students in both a STEM major and the university based on their MTC attendance.


<!-- p:13 -->


In addition, public value includes the ways organisations change the behaviour of broader institutions through involvement in transforming institutional policies (Mendel &amp; Brudney, 2014). For MTCs, a public value produced may influence departmental policies and activities with equitable practices in mind. For example, Cronin and colleagues (2015; 2019; 2021) found MTCs can contribute to instructional change through providing feedback to instructors on student performance such as common topic difficulties. This can be extended beyond the instructional level to broader curricular change, such as alignment between sequential courses (Croft et al., 2016; Grove et al., 2018) or to university-wide policies such as university mathematics course placement practices. DiGregorio and colleagues (DiGregorio et al., 2023; DiGregorio &amp; Ellis Hagman, 2021) found alternative placement approaches can improve the experience for students with structurally disadvantaged identities. They suggest using videos of current students discussing the importance of practicing for the placement assessment as well as the provision of review materials. MTCs could help develop these materials and offer tutoring services for students practicing for the assessment. It' s important to note that while tutor centre leadership can contribute to departmental change efforts, the centre's strong connection to the mathematics department is key to their influence (Cronin et al., 2025). A centre's connections and network are the final performance dimension, described in the next section.

Additional attempts to measure public value have incorporated the other performance dimensions into various forms of public value scorecards (Faulkner &amp; Kaufman, 2017; Moore, 2003; Moulton &amp; Eckerd, 2012). For example, Moulton and Eckerd created a Nonprofit Sector Public Role Index to assess nonprofits' perceived performance on six different nonprofit roles that contribute to public value. However, the survey items align with a support for all aims, rather than attending to creating public value relevant to specific demographics. For example, the survey item 'promoting a sense of community among our users/clients' would refer to all users of the MTC rather than attending to specific populations. Instead, one might ask if the MTC promotes a sense of community among Black women. In addition, the survey item 'participating in government committees or commissions [department or university committees]' doesn't specify committees targeted at or with the potential to influence policies related to DEI. In addition, responses to the survey would need to be disaggregated by demographics to ensure the dominant voice doesn't drown out the voices of minoritized individuals.

Moore (2003) argues that public value is pursued through the organisation's strategic planning via their vision, mission and goals. As previously discussed, there's a difference in the targeted public value between a mission to 'improve STEM retention' and to 'improve STEM retention of minoritized students' or to 'foster a sense of belonging' and to 'foster a sense of belonging for minoritized students' . Minoritized students must be centred in mission statements (or guiding principles) in order to target public value specific to these students. A support-for-all mission statement would be misaligned with a public value to improve the quality of life for minoritized students via STEM retention. Further, the organisation's strategic planning influences their outputs and outcomes, which in turn creates public value (Moore, 2003). For example, a centre's type and quality of services provided will impact their ability to create public value as well as stakeholders' perception of their value. As we see, public value is impacted by all previous performance dimensions, as well as the fi nal dimension, network and institutional legitimacy.


<!-- p:14 -->


### 2.8. Network and institutional legitimacy

The seventh and fi nal performance dimension is a centre's network and legitimacy as an institution. Networks include stakeholders such as funders, university administration, faculty, other campus learning centres, parents and students. A centre's network extends beyond the university and includes relationships with other mathspecific and broader learning centres nationally and/or internationally, as well as the mathematics, mathematics education and other relevant research communities. Institutional Legitimacy is comprised of stakeholder/community trust in the MTC, perception of the MTC as being transparent and fair, and the adherence of the organisation to its mission (Faulkner &amp; Kaufman, 2017).

The strength and nature of relationships with funders and administrators is key for support on changes to inputs such as funding (LaTona-Tequida et al., 2024). As previously mentioned, a centre's funding and leadership time dedicated to the centre impacts the ability to enact DEI change. Strong relationships with stakeholders, who support the centre's DEI mission, assist with a desire to enact structural change in the centre. In addition, a centre's relationship with others doing equity work on campus and other tutoring centres allows the centre to draw on existing knowing and resources to efficiently make DEI changes (Cross et al., 2007; Mohrman et al., 2003; Tenkasi &amp; Chesmore, 2003). Finally, as previously discussed, strong departmental connections can enable centres to influence broader departmental and university changes (Cronin et al., 2019; Cronin &amp; Meehan, 2021).

A centre must also examine its institutional legitimacy by scrutinising its reputation and credibility. A centre's performance on institutional legitimacy will be determined, in part, by the public's belief that the centre upholds public values. For centres, the 'public' includes individuals such as students, faculty, staff, administrators and donors. Distinct from public value (singular) are public values (plural), which are 'normative personal judgements about social standards, principles, and ideas to be pursued and upheld' (Faulkner &amp; Kaufman, 2017, p. 70). For example, relevant public values might include valuing a centre treating students like adults with agency, who can make their own informed decisions regarding when they need help, what they need help with and how tutoring will be most effective for them. In contrast, the public might value controlling the parent-child relationship in centres with forced requirements such as attendance, working on specific problems in the centre and enforcing certain formats of tutoring. Some stakeholders may value independence while others value structure. Surveys and focus groups, particularly with minoritized groups, can be used to determine what stakeholder's value and their perception of the centre's alignment with these public values.

Trust is also key for institutional legitimacy. One method of building and maintaining trust is through audits. Audits should include a breakdown of spending, which attends to how resources are used. Specifically, how various programmes are supported and to what extent, as well as the demographics of the target users of the programmes and the actual users.

## 3. Discussion

We now provide four examples regarding important aspects of the overall performance assessment. The fi rst example illustrates the importance of connecting the mission statement to intended DEI objectives. The second elaborates on how targets for improvement must examine all performance dimensions and the third, how performance assessments can identify barriers to change. Finally, the fourth example elucidates on centre performance as measured against an ideal, comparison with other centres, or comparison within the centre over time.

Example 1: A centre's mission is the guidepost to which performance in all areas should be measured. Suppose a centre's mission is to increase minoritized students' sense of belonging. The centre has asked themselves the question from Figure 1 under Customer Service Outcomes: How do minoritized students experience tutoring? Based on their mission, the centre would examine, more specifically, the extent to which minoritized students perceive the centre as having contributed to sense of belonging. This would include exploring both whether the students report change in sense of belonging (behavioural/environmental outcome) and how their experience impacted their sense of belonging (customer service outcome). A centre would then examine their outputs for the ways in which their services align with their mission. Offering services in which minoritized students see little benefit would also miss the mark. If outputs don't fi t with the mission, the centre would need to rethink their allocation of resources (inputs). In addition, if an audit reveals use of resources is unaligned with the centre's mission, this would decrease stakeholder's trust in the centre, reducing the centre's legitimacy.

Example 2: Targets for improvement must examine all performance dimensions. Suppose a centre asks themselves the fi rst output question in Figure 1: Does disaggregated centre usage data show minoritized students use the centre proportionally? Suppose the centre fi nds minoritized students attend tutoring at lower rates than their counterparts. To better understand reasons for this, the centre should collect customer satisfaction outcome data through surveys or focus groups with minoritized students. Through this process, the centre may fi nd minoritized students aren't receiving sufficient information about the centre and the centre needs better marketing (organisational capacity). To improve marketing, the centre leader needs resources (inputs) of time and potentially funding. Networks can aid the centre in fi nding existing marketing materials that can be adapted or ways that other university partners can reach students. Although the initial target was to improve minoritized student usage, aspects of almost every dimension are involved in addressing it.

Example 3: Performance assessments can help identify barriers to achieving the mission of the centre and serve as justification to stakeholders to support changes. Often barriers fall within inputs, such as insufficient money, centre leadership time, or expertise to enact change. Centres may also lack a strong network which prevents them from drawing on the resources of others. Stakeholder expectations and values may also hinder efforts of centres to meet their mission. The centre has asked themselves the question from Figure 1 under Network/Institutional Legitimacy: How do the relationships with funders, stakeholders, local DEI experts/practitioners, and MTCs internationally support/constrain DEI change efforts? Specifically, the centre is focused on relationships with stakeholders.


<!-- p:16 -->


Suppose a centre's mission is to increase minoritized students' sense of belonging. In examining their services (output) for alignment with the mission, the centre determines that a lack of training for graduate teaching assistants tutoring in the centre appears to be leading to minoritized students experiencing an unwelcoming environment. The centre seeks permission to train teaching assistants but stakeholders such as faculty are unsupportive (institutional legitimacy). The reasons might involve a difference in values for what is most important. The centre may value the student experience at tutoring while stakeholders are focused on graduate teaching assistants' own coursework and research. The performance assessment can provide evidence linking training, customer service and mission accomplishment. As Johns and colleagues (2023) found, a centre's positive relationship with department leadership and faculty can produce impetus for change by provide backing and communication for altering services.

Example 4: Centre performance can be measured against an ideal, comparison with other centres, or comparison within the centre over time. Suppose a centre has asked themselves the Behavioural/environmental change question in Figure 1, Is identity a mediating factor on the relationship between centre usage and fi nal grades? An ideal would be to find identity is not a mediating factor. A centre may also compare their performance on this measure with that of other MTCs, particularly those with similar structures (Johns et al., 2023). Comparison across centres could provide additional benchmarks for success as a centre strives for the ideal. Comparisons across MTCs would also allow a centre to learn from others and adapt other centres' practices (Network and organisational capacity). For example, suppose Center A found identity to be a strong mediating factor in the relationship between centre usage and fi nal grades while Center B found a weak relationship. Center A might work with Center B to better understand Center B's practices which may contribute to their performance. An example of this might be Center B's training practices (organisational capacity) which appear to impact student belonging in their centre, based on student feedback (customer service outcomes). Finally, a centre may draw comparisons to themselves over time. This would allow the centre to determine what changes have or have not been successful. For example, a centre may fi nd that at Time A's performance assessment, identity was to be a strong mediating factor in the relationship between centre usage and later at Time B, the impact of identity had decreased. The centre can examine other changes in their performance assessment between the two times to identify changes which may have led to this decreased, such as adjustments to tutor training.

## 4. Conclusion

We have described several aspects of an MTC's performance with respect to DEI. Assessing and updating the centre's mission statement (or guiding principles) is a key fi rst step to improving DEI performance. Once the mission is aligned with the centre's DEI vision, the centre should conduct an initial performance assessment. We suggest centres begin this assessment by asking/reflecting on the questions in Figure 1. These questions can aid in determining target areas to address in order to work towards a more diverse, equitable and inclusive centre. We emphasize that the seven performance dimensions inform each other so care must be taken to address factors in multiple performance dimensions which impact the current target area for improvement.


<!-- p:17 -->

The performance assessment can help to identify barriers to achieving the mission of the centre and serve as justification for structural changes such as departmental policy, centre location and centre budget. After conducting the initial assessment, centres should develop short-term and long-term goals with action plans. In addition, systems and structures should be put in place to regularly revisit the performance assessment. This is critical in continually refining and improving the centre's efforts because working to become a centre that supports a diverse group of students and is an equitable and inclusive centre is an ongoing journey that will never be 'achieved' . There is always somewhere to improve, especially as student needs change over time and research continues to grow.

Future work should explore practical applications of this theoretical framework. As Johns et al. (2023) note, the standards to which a centre's performance is measured against may be an ideal, comparison with other centres, or comparison within a centre over time. While this paper discusses potential ideals and best practices based on research, only application of this framework to multiple centres will produce opportunities to compare across centres. In addition, this work aims to promote DEI within common centre structures. This aligns with Gutierrez's (2012) dominant axis, supporting students within the system ('playing the game'). However, there are opportunities for centres to carefully think and reimagine what student support might look like and what is possible, drawing on the input from key stakeholders, including students and tutors, with an aim to also ' change the game' .
