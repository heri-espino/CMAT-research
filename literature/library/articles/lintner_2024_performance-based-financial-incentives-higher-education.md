## Effects of performance-based financial incentives on higher education students: A meta-analysis using causal evidence

Tom  ́ a ˇ s Lintner

####### A R T I C L E  I N F O

Keywords: Systematic review Meta-analysis Higher education Financial incentives Student performance

### 1. Introduction

Many universities (e.g., Crowne, 2022; Graziosi, 2014; Measure of the Faculty of Arts, Masaryk University No. February 2020; Fifth Consolidated Scholarship and Bursary Rules of Charles University, 2022), state-based agencies (e.g., Mulyaningsih et al., 2022; Sjo­ quist &amp; Winters, 2015), and NGOs (e.g., MacDonald et al., 2009; Mayer, Patel, Rudd, &amp; Ratledge, 2015) provide financial incentives for higher education students contingent on their performance. Every year, huge amounts of money are spent on financial incentives for university students - only in the US, over $20 billion are awarded annually to undergraduate students (Mayer, Patel, Rudd, &amp; Ratledge, 2015). The incentives are offered in both fee-paying and free-education contexts. Some organizations provide the incentives simply on the principle of merit-based rewards for students without any specific aim (e.g., Fifth Consolidated Scholarship and Bursary Rules of Charles University, 2022). On the other hand, other organizations provide incentives with the explicit intention of improving student motivation and ultimately student achievement (e.g., MacDonald et al., 2009), with some incentives aimed specifically at low-income

####### A B S T R A C T

Many institutions provide financial incentives for higher education students contingent on their performance, hoping to increase their motivation and achievement. The aim of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the effects of performance-based financial incentives on higher education stu­ dents using causal evidence. A total of 18 randomized controlled trials involving 20,286 students were included. Performance-based financial incentives increased the number of college credits earned,  marginally  improved  student  grade  point  averages,  and  improved  exam  scores  when targeted at a single subject. No evidence was found for differential effects on student populations by gender or first-generation college status. Neither the incentive amount nor a focus on lowincome students influenced the incentive effectiveness in improving student grade point aver­ ages or credits earned. Tentative evidence suggests that incentive designs in which the number of reward recipients is limited are more beneficial to students from the upper median level of high school grade point average. Hence, the study implies that it is more beneficial to provide loweramount incentives attainable by a larger number of students than to provide higher-amount in­ centives attainable only by a small number of students. The results of this meta-analysis are of interest for researchers, policymakers, and stakeholders in higher education involved in designing financial incentive schemes for higher education students - providing a substantial step toward evidence-based practice.

#### 1.1. What we know about the effects of financial incentives so far

There is a line of theoretical reasoning and empirical evidence suggesting that performance-based financial incentives improve student  performance.  The  conventional  economic  theory  postulates  that  financial  rewards  stimulate  extrinsic  motivations  that consequently make incentivized behavior more attractive and lead to higher effort and performance by increasing the marginal monetary benefit  of  performing  (Alchian &amp; Demsetz,  1972;  Becker,  1976;  Frey &amp; Jegen,  2001).  There  are  several  randomized controlled trials (RCTs) suggesting that performance-based financial incentives improve student performance. Manpower Demon­ stration Research Corporation (MDRC) - an American NGO researching and applying various programs and incentives aimed at low-income populations (MDRC, 2022) - conducted several RTCs on the effectiveness of performance-based scholarships. MDRC design has several unique characteristics: the incentives are provided to low-income students, they are provided in a fee-paying context, and they have relatively low benchmarks (usually at least part-time enrollment and at least a ' C ' grade average). MDRCs RCTs have generally supported the thesis that performance-based financial incentives improve student performance (e.g., Barrow et al., 2014; Patel &amp; Valenzuela, 2013; Sommo et al., 2014). In addition to the MDRC studies, several other studies have reported a clear positive effect of incentives on higher education student performance on exams (e.g., Cole et al., 2016; Herranz-Zarzoso &amp; Sabater-Grande, 2018; Raupach et al., 2013).

On the other hand, there is a line of theoretical reasoning and empirical evidence suggesting that performance-based financial incentives decrease student performance. The crowding-out theory postulates that financial rewards adversely affect actors ' intrinsic motives, leading to decreased motivation and performance (Bowles &amp; Polania-Reyes, 2012; Deci, 1975). It has been established that financial incentives produce two kinds of effects simultaneously: the standard price effect makes incentivized behavior more attractive, and the crowding-out effect reduces the intrinsic motives for undertaking an incentivized task (Frey &amp; Jegen, 2001; Gneezy et al., 2011; List et al., 2018). A meta-analysis by Deci et al. (2001) supports this by showing that tangible rewards strongly undermine intrinsic motivation and self-reported interest among college students. There are several RCTs suggesting that performance-based financial incentives decrease student performance. Both Leuven et al. (2010) and De Paola et al. (2012) showed that the incentives decreased performance of low-ability students and widened the differences between high- and low-ability students.

Ultimately, several studies have reported no, negligible, or mixed effects of incentives on higher education student performance. Leuven et al. (2011) reported no effects of incentives on exam performance; Angrist et al. (2014) reported only negligible effects on credits earned and grade point average (GPA). On the other hand, Angrist et al. (2009) reported a negative effect on credits earned and GPA for men, and reported a positive effect for women; the strongest effect was found for women with first-generation college status. This goes against findings from the literature in economics that uniformly expect men to be more sensitive to financial incentives than women. It has been argued that men are more competitive and exert higher effort under financial incentives (Croson &amp; Gneezy, 2009; Sittenthaler &amp; Mohnen, 2020).

There have been several attempts to arrive at more conclusive results on when financial incentives for higher education students work, employing systematic reviews and meta-analyses. However, these attempts either dealt with the effect of need-based aid for lowincome students unconditioned by student performance or with the effect of merit-based aid with incentive benchmarks aimed at high school graduates. They have not focused on performance-based incentives aimed at improving the performance of enrolled higher education  students.  In  a  meta-analysis,  Sneyers  and  De  Witte  (2018)  reported  only  a  small  effect  of  need-based  grants  on  the enrollment, retention, and graduation of higher education students - all at 0.05 Cohen ' s d .  Nguyen et al. (2019) performed a systematic review and meta-analysis on the effect of financial aid (both merit and need-based) on persistence and degree completion among higher education students in the United States ' fee-paying context. They estimated that $1000 improves persistence by 1.8%, on-time degree completion by 2%, and delayed completion by 2.5%, with merit-based incentives having a smaller effect than need-based incentives on on-time completion. In a systematic review of studies dealing with disadvantaged students in higher education, Her­ baut and Geven (2020) found need-based aid to have an effect on graduation; however, the effect on enrollment was present only if the amount of aid was sufficiently large. Furthermore, they found that merit-based aid had no effect on graduation and could even be harmful to the enrollment of disadvantaged students, especially in contexts in which disadvantaged students do not have access to the aid. The authors found limited evidence that performance-based incentives increase graduation rates of disadvantaged students. Finally, in a systematic review brief, LaSota et al. (2022) found that the loss of need-based aid or merit-based aid reduces student persistence and graduation.

Several points can be made based on the literature review - (1) in theory, financial incentives have the potential to produce both the standard direct price effect, making the incentivized behavior more attractive, and the crowding-out effect, reducing the intrinsic motives for undertaking the incentivized task; (2) need-based aid leads to small positive effects on enrollment, retention, and grad­ uation; however, the effect is dependent on the aid amount; (3) merit-based aid aimed at high school graduates entering higher ed­ ucation  has  a  smaller  effect  on  enrollment,  retention,  and  graduation  than  need-based  aid,  with  potentially  harmful  effects  for disadvantaged students; (4) in the context of higher education, findings from empirical studies on the effects of performance-based financial incentives on higher education students are largely contradictory; and (5) the effects of performance-based financial in­ centives have not yet been well explored.

#### 1.2. Present study

This study builds upon recent efforts to synthesize evidence on the impact of financial incentives on academic performance of higher education students. Unlike previous meta-analyses (Nguyen et al., 2019; Sneyers &amp; De Witte, 2018), this study examines the effects of performance-based incentives. Moreover, unlike Deci et al. ' s  (2001) meta-analysis focused on student motivation, this research targets directly observable academic outcomes - credits earned and GPA. This study contributes significantly to the literature by presenting the first meta-analysis that evaluates the effect of performance-based financial incentives on higher education students, drawing on causal evidence. It addresses the following questions.

- (1)  What effects do performance-based financial incentives have on higher education students?
- (2)  What effects do performance-based financial incentives have on different subgroups of higher education students?
- (3)  (How) do different incentive  designs  moderate the  effects  of  performance-based  financial  incentives  on  higher  education students?

The results of this meta-analysis are of interest for researchers, policymakers, and stakeholders in higher education involved in designing financial incentive schemes for higher education students. This study summarizes and aggregates all available evidence on the effects of performance-based financial incentives for higher education students and provides a substantial step toward evidencebased practice.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: First, the methods section details the systematic search, data extraction, and data analysis procedures. Then, the results are presented, including an overview of the included studies, an assessment of risk of bias, and a series of meta-analyses and meta-regressions that show the effects of performance-based financial incentives on higher education students. Finally, these results are discussed in the context of conventional economic theory and the crowding-out theory, and in the context of limitations and implications of this study for future research and policy.

### 2. Methods

This study was pre-registered with a protocol at OSF: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/K53PF.

#### 2.1. Literature search and inclusion criteria

I conducted the literature search in two subject-specific databases - Scopus (limited to the subject area of social sciences) and ERIC - on February 2, 2022. Scopus was chosen as a primary database for peer-reviewed articles and ERIC was chosen to supplement Scopus with its coverage of gray literature. The search and selection were done in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The final search string was created after an iterative process of constructing a logic grid based on (1) level of education, (2) research design, (3) treatment, and (4) outcome - see Table 1, finding and adding relevant terms, and removing terms yielding irrelevant results (Aromataris &amp; Riitano, 2014). No limit on publication date was set. The search was done within all fields. The following string was used for the final search in Scopus:

(university OR college OR ' higher education ' OR ' tertiary education ' ) AND (experiment* OR trial) AND ( 'fi nancial incentive* ' OR 'fi nancial reward* ' OR money OR scholarship* OR ' performance?based ' OR ' merit?based ' ) PRE/5 1 (achievement OR grade* OR gpa)

The initial search yielded 797 results. After removing duplicate references, a total of 785 studies remained. Fig. 1 presents an overview of the literature search, screening, and selection process.

Studies met the inclusion criteria if they (a) employed quantitative experimental or quasi-experimental design, (b) had higher education students as the target population, (c) included interventions that gave students an opportunity to get some form of future financial incentive contingent on their academic performance, (d) had at least one control group of students without the opportunity to get any form of financial incentive contingent on their academic performance, (e) reported at least one quantifiable variable of achievement (e.g., exam score, GPA, credits gained, pass rate) as an outcome of the intervention, and (f) were written in English.

During the initial screening, I reviewed titles and abstracts. In this step, 740 records were excluded. Most studies at this stage were excluded because they did not concern higher education students (e.g., Fryer, 2011) or did not employ an experimental/quasi-experimental design (e.g., Graziosi, 2014). The full texts of the remaining 45 records were assessed for eligibility and their reference lists were checked for other potentially relevant studies. After the full-text screening, 29 records were excluded. Most studies at this stage were excluded because they concerned purely need-based aid and therefore the reported changes in performance were unrelated to the motivation to perform (e.g., Castleman et al., 2018), or the financial incentive (or a larger part of it) given to students as part of the intervention was paid before any of the performative requirements at a higher education institution were met (e.g., Welch, 2014) - the reported changes in performance were therefore more likely to be attributed to the behavior at high school. Two publications from the reference lists of the included studies were added to the final selection, yielding a total of 18 studies in the final selection.

1 The PRE/5 operator was used for the Scopus search only, as ERIC does not support the PRE operator. In the ERIC search, the AND operator was used instead.

#### 2.2. Data extraction

The following data were extracted from the studies: name(s) of the author(s), date of the publication, location of the experiment, date(s) of the experiment, study design, incentive scheme name, target group(s), sample size, treatment(s) (including the incentive amount and requirements imposed on students necessary to get the incentive), outcome(s), and values for the subgroups if the study included any. Authors were e-mailed for information that could not be found in the articles. Some included studies did not present all the necessary data for the subgroup analyses and/or did not present both credits earned and GPA as outcomes despite the primary data including them. Hence, in addition to the data extracted from the 18 studies, primary student-level data from three datasets related to the included studies were extracted - Replication data for: Incentives and Services for College Achievement: Evidence from a Randomized T. Lintner Trial (Angrist et al., 2019) providing data for Angrist et al. (2009); Replication data for: When Opportunity Knocks, Who Answers? New Evidence on College Achievement Awards (Oreopoulos et al., 2018) providing data for Angrist et al. (2014); and MDRC ' s The Higher Education Randomized Controlled Trials Restricted Access File (THE- RCT RAF)(Diamond et al., 2021) providing data for Barrow et al. (2014), Erwin et al. (2021), Mayer, Patel, and Gutierrez (2015), Patel and Valenzuela (2013), Richburg-Hayes et al. (2015), and Sommo et al. (2014). Including the three datasets made it possible to perform calculations beyond the results published in the original studies, to extend the scope of the subgroup analyses as described in the 2.3. Data analysis section, and to minimize the risk of pub­ lication bias as unpublished data were included. Means, SDs, and group sizes of the experimental and control (sub)groups were calculated if they were not present in the published studies but the data were available in the related datasets. The data that were not originally published in the included studies and that were calculated from the datasets are noted by a c footnote in Table 2 in 3.1. Characteristics of the studies section.

During the data extraction procedure, two broad categories of studies emerged. The first category comprised 12 (66.7%) studies providing incentives for benchmarks based on a combination of credits earned and grade point average (GPA) and measuring the outcome of the intervention with overall academic performance for the whole year of study - credits earned and/or GPA. I call this category overall-performance  studies .  The  outcomes  in  the overall-performance  studies were the result  of  student  performances  on multiple assessments. The second category comprised 6 (33.3%) studies providing incentives for benchmarks based on achievement in a single subject measuring the outcome of the intervention with an exam score. I call this category single subject studies . Because of the stark conceptual differences between the two categories of the studies, I decided to analyze them separately and not to pool the results from the two categories.

To allow a better comparison of the treatments used in the studies, the original values of the incentives in the respective currencies were converted into values in international dollars (Int$) of 2021. The international dollar is a hypothetical unit of currency having the same purchasing power parity (PPP) that the US dollar has in the United States at a given point in time. It aims to express the value cleared of differences in PPP between countries. First, the original value in the year of the experiment was converted into 2021 ' s value of the original currency by multiplying the original value by the cumulative inflation rate of the currency resulting in the value of the incentive in the original currency in 2021. Then, the value in the original currency of 2021 was converted into international dollars using the 2021 IMF ' s implied PPP conversion rate (IMF, 2022).

##### 2.2.1. Subgroup data from overall-performance studies

All the overall-performance studies reported the outcomes of the intervention for the whole experimental/control group as well as separately for one or more subgroups. Three kinds of binary subgroups of students emerged during the extraction procedure: by gender (male/female), by high school achievement (upper/lower high school GPA median), and by first-generation college status (first/not first). All available data for the subgroups were extracted to allow subgroup analyses.

##### 2.2.2. Subgroup data from single-subject studies

Unlike the overall-performance studies , the single-subject studies did not report any separate outcomes for subgroups. Subgroup an­ alyses were therefore not performed.

#### 2.3. Assessing risk of bias

I assessed the risk of bias using the revised tool to assess risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2) (Sterne et al., 2019). RoB 2 is structured around a set of five domains of bias - randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results - plus an overall risk of bias. Each domain can either be judged low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias. The tool suggests the level of risk of each domain based on an algorithm based on questions from a series of signaling questions.

#### 2.4. Effect size calculation

To calculate the effect size, I followed the procedures described by Borenstein et al. (2009) and used the R (R Core Team, 2021) package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). Some studies reported multiple treatment arms; however, except for Leuven et al. (2011), they did not pair the different treatment arms with different control groups. Hence, in accordance with Borenstein et al. (2009) and Rücker et al. (2017), to prevent unit-of-analysis errors, I pooled the results from the multiple treatment arms into one for each study before performing the meta-analyses (except for Leuven et al. [2011]). I present all treatment arms in Table 2 in 3.1. Characteristics of the studies section, however, all the meta-analyses include the pooled groups only.

#### 2.5. Data analysis

The whole quantitative synthesis was performed in the R (R Core Team, 2021) package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010).

I followed the procedures described by Borenstein et al. (2009) for the effect size calculation, integration, and subgroup analyses. In agreement with Colquhoun ' s (2014) work, aiming to avoid the entirely arbitrary nature of the established use of statistical significance at p -level of &lt; 0.05, which may lead to misleading interpretations of data, I avoid labelling my results as ' significant ' (including any related terms). Instead, in all models, I state both p -value and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

##### 2.5.1. Analyzing overall-performance studies

For the overall-performance studies ,  I  measured two separate outcomes of the performance-based financial incentives - college credits earned one year after the experiment had commenced and GPA one year after the experiment had commenced. All the included overall-performance studies reported outcomes in either one or both measures one year after the experiment commenced. For both college credits earned and GPA, I decided to include the raw mean difference (MD) between the experimental and control groups as the effect size measure. While the studies differed in the measures of the credits earned and the GPA because of the differences in the credit and grading systems among countries, this was not a reason not to rely on MD, as, for example, opposed to Cohen ' s d . The reason is that both the college credits earned and the GPAs could easily be converted into comparable equivalents before calculating the MD without the need to lose the original and easily understandable scales and the differences in variability among the study populations (Bond et al., 2003). The original college credits earned in each study were converted into equivalents of standard one-year-full-time credit load by dividing the original college credit value with the country one-year-full-time credit load:

For the overall-performance studies , for both the credits earned and GPA outcomes, I employed an overall random-effect model followed by three separate random-effect submodels - for gender (male/female), high school achieve­ ment (upper/lower high school GPA median), and first-generation college status (first/not first). Random effect models were chosen because the studies substantially varied in the requirements that the students needed to meet to get the incentive, target populations, presence of additional treatments, and the context of the different systems of education. I used the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment (Hartung, 1999; Knapp &amp; Hartung, 2003; Sidik &amp; Jonkman, 2002) to counter the problem of the small number of studies (IntHout et al., 2014; R  ̈ over et al., 2015). In addition to reporting the overall effect sizes and the significance levels, I calculated Q statistics, absolute heterogeneity represented by τ statistics showing the variance of the true effect sizes between the studies, and I 2 statistics representing the relative heterogeneity.

After the overall and the submodels were calculated, I performed subgroup analyses. The subgroup analyses compared the effect sizes of each of the paired subgroups with a Wald-type test yielding an estimate of the difference between the subgroups, SE, and p - value - aiming to investigate whether performance-based financial incentives have different effects on students based on their gender, high school achievement, or first-generation college status.

I next fitted several meta-regression models to assess the moderating role of incentive designs. For the overall data - for the credits earned and the GPA outcomes each - I fitted a meta-regression model with two moderator variables (a continuous incentive amount 2 variable and a factorial availability of the incentive to low-income students only variable) and the interaction term between the two, aiming to investigate whether the effectivity of performance-based financial incentives is moderated by variables influencing students ' financial needs. Finally, I fitted meta-regression models with the design limiting the number of reward recipients (De Paola et al., 2012) serving as a single factorial moderator variable to the separate upper and lower high school GPA median subgroups with credits earned and university GPA as outcome each. This was done to identify the crowding-out effect among low-ability students when they have to compete for limited incentives with others, as even their improved performance may not win the reward, and the low-ability students may therefore lose intrinsic motivation to perform. The resulting meta-regression models yielded the same measures as the models without moderator plus the R 2 measuring the amount of heterogeneity accounted for.

##### 2.5.2. Analyzing single-subject studies

For the single-subject studies ,  I  measured a single outcome of the performance-based financial incentives - exam score. Due to heterogeneity in the reported effect sizes, the original effect sizes were transformed into Cohen ' s d . I employed a basic random-effect model yielding the same statistics as the random-effect models of the overall-performance studies . Afterward, I fitted a meta-regression model with a single moderator variable - incentive amount . Since most of the single-subject studies did not report any subgroup dif­ ferences, I did not perform any subgroup analyses.

#### 2.6. Assessment of publication bias

In all models, I employed Egger ' s test (Egger et al., 1997) followed by a contour-enhanced funnel plot analysis (Palmer et al., 2008) to detect asymmetry in the funnel plots caused by publication bias.

#### 3.2. Risk of bias assessment

Fig. 2 shows the individual studies assessed by the risk of bias. The risk of bias assessment suggests that overall, none of the studies had a high risk of bias. The overall-performance studies were mostly rated as having a low risk of bias. Four (33.3%) of the overallperformance studies were rated as having some concerns. In all cases, this was in the selection of the reported result domain, either because the studies did not follow any pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before the unblinded outcome data were available for analysis or because the information about a pre-specified analysis plan was not known. The rest of the overall-performance studies either followed a pre-specified analysis plan or the analysis and the results of the analysis could be checked in a related dataset. All singlesubject studies were rated as having some concerns. None of the studies followed any pre-specified analysis plan or the information about pre-specified analysis plan was not known. Additionally, four (66.6%) of the single-subject studies were rated as having some concerns in the measurement of the outcome domain. This was because the exam scores measured in the studies could have been influenced by the researchers ' knowledge of the intervention received. The results from the meta-analyses should therefore be treated in the context of a possible bias in the primary studies due to researchers not following pre-specified analysis plans - following prespecified plans would have minimized the risks of making arbitrary decisions during the research process - as well as a possible bias due to researcher knowledge of student membership in the intervention/control group influencing measured student exam scores.

#### 3.3. Effects of performance-based financial incentives on credits earned

##### 3.3.1. Overall effects

Fig. 3 shows the results of the overall random effect model as well as the submodels on the effect of incentives on credits earned. The overall model is based on 11 studies with a combined sample of 15,318 students. The overall model suggests a positive effect of in­ centives at 0.039 MD of standard one-year-full-time credit load (the equivalent of 2.3 ECTS credits) at p = 0.001. There was variability in true effect sizes beyond the expected sampling error, as the model yielded Q-statistics at p = 0.074, moderate relative between-study heterogeneity with I 2 of 44.1%, and absolute between-study heterogeneity with τ of 0.019.

##### 3.3.2. Effects on different subgroups

Comparisons of the individual submodels as well as subgroup analyses based on gender, high-school GPA median, and (not)firstgeneration college status did not confirm any differences in the effectiveness of incentives on credits earned between any of the paired subgroups. Although the submodel for women alone yielded more convincing results than the submodel for men (see Fig. 3), the Waldtype tests comparing the effect sizes of the paired subgroups did not confirm any differences (see Table 3).

##### 3.3.3. Effects of different incentive designs

I found no evidence that the incentive amount or its provision to low-income students increased its effectiveness. The model using incentive amount and availability of the incentive to low-income students only as moderator variables suggests that neither of the two variables or the interaction between them has any influence on the study ' s effect size (see Table 4). The model yielded Q-statistics at p = 0.217, relative between-study heterogeneity with I 2 of 29.8%, absolute between-study heterogeneity with τ of 0.014, and the amount of heterogeneity accounted for with R 2 at 50.6%. Including the moderator variables therefore explained some heterogeneity in the overall model. Fig. 4 shows a meta-regression bubble plot with the y axis denoting the study ' s effect size, x axis denoting incentive amount, bubble size denoting study weights in the model, and bubble color denoting availability of incentive to low-income students only.

I found tentative evidence that, compared to designs not limiting the number of reward recipients, designs limiting the number of reward recipients are more effective for students from the upper high school median level for credits earned. In other words, the design limiting the number of reward recipients seems to positively affect the credits earned of students from the upper high school GPA median level, while a null effect is found for students from the lower high school GPA median. ### 4. Discussion

In this meta-analysis, 18 RCTs involving 20,286 students were used to examine the effects of performance-based financial in­ centives on higher education students, including subgroup analyses by gender, high school GPA, and first-generation college status, as well as moderator analyses by incentive amount and focus on low-income students. In sum, this study confirms the positive effect of incentives on credits earned in a year (0.039 of full-time credit load [2.3 ECTS credits]), GPA (0.041 points [on the 4.0 scale]), and exam performance (0.482 Cohen ' s d ). While this study does not confirm any differing effects of the incentives on students by gender and first-generation college status, it provides tentative evidence that incentive designs in which the number of reward recipients is limited are more beneficial to students from the upper median of high school GPA. Overall, this study provides robust evidence of the effectiveness of performance-based financial incentives for higher education students and fills the gap in the recent research synthesis literature on the effectiveness of incentives for higher education students in general - previous literature focused mainly on need- and T. Lintner merit-based aid aimed at high school soon-to-be-graduates (Herbaut &amp; Geven, 2020; LaSota et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2019; Sneyers &amp; De Witte, 2018) and lacked a focus on performance-based incentives for enrolled students.

On the other hand, my results stand in contrast to literature in economics expecting men to be more sensitive than women to financial incentives (Croson &amp; Gneezy, 2009; Sittenthaler &amp; Mohnen, 2020). The more convincing effect of incentives on GPA for women might, to some degree, be explained by the synergy of the incentives ' direct price effect and students ' intrinsic motives; while men tend to be more competitive under financial incentives, women are more sensitive to social cues (Croson &amp; Gneezy, 2009). With most of the RCTs in the overall-performance studies having relatively low benchmarks, and, with the exception of De Paola et al. ' s (2012) design, having limited numbers of rewards, the incentives arguably did not create strong competitive environments. On the other hand, they might have stimulated women ' s intrinsic motives more, with women being more sensitive to the social benefits of their improved performance.

The results of this study suggesting the null moderating effects of the incentive amount and the provision of incentives to lowincome students are in contrast to economic theory and the findings by Nguyen et al. (2019). I offer three explanations for this. The first explanation is that the effectiveness of the incentive is not influenced directly by the incentive amount, but rather by student ' s unmet needs - a compound variable reflecting the difference between student income and expenses including tuition, school supplies, and living expenses. Lacking data on a range of student expenses in the contexts of the individual studies, the incentive amount in combination with the focus on low-income students might not truthfully represent students ' real unmet needs. Another explanation is that the relationship between the incentive amount and its effectiveness is not linear. Incremental changes in the incentive amount might either start playing a role after reaching some minimum amount necessary for students to be motivated, or the role of incre­ mental change might diminish once the incentive amount reaches some benchmark sufficient to motivate students. The range of incentive amounts covered by this study might therefore not capture the amount range in which incentive amount makes a difference - however, this is rather unlikely considering that this meta-analysis includes a range from tens to thousands of Int$. The last explanation is that there is simply no relationship between the incentive amount and its effectiveness, and this study captures the true - null - relationship.

The findings of this study provide some limited evidence for the crowding-out effect (Bowles &amp; Polania-Reyes, 2012). The fact that incentive designs in which the number of reward recipients is limited are more beneficial to students from the upper median of high school  GPA,  while  null  or  negative  effects  were  found  for  students  from  the  lower  median  of  high  school  GPA,  suggests  that low-achieving students might have lost their intrinsic motivation if they perceived the incentive benchmarks to be unattainable. The incentives may work for students with better high school grades because they complement the intrinsic motives of already motivated and able students; they counter the intrinsic motives of students with worse high school grades who might have believed that their efforts would be futile as they had to compete for limited rewards with higher-ability students. In both cases (De Paola et al., 2012; Leuven et al., 2010), the incentives were provided only to a small number of top-performing students in a competitive environment. Drawing from economic theory, incentives with limited numbers of recipients might have worked better for high-ability students because they needed to exert much less effort than their low-ability counterparts to achieve the incentive. Low-ability students therefore had a lower benefit of performing when the effort necessary to achieve the incentive was accounted for. The design in which the number of reward recipients is limited may therefore widen the performance gap between high and low achievers.

#### 4.1. Contributions to the literature

This study enriches the literature by offering the first research synthesis examining the effects of performance-based financial incentives on the performance of higher education students. It compiles all available primary sources, providing a solid foundation for stakeholders in higher education to design evidence-based incentive schemes. The analysis reveals varied effects of incentives on different academic metrics, including credits earned, GPA, and exam scores. Additionally, the study identifies consistent effects of incentives across subgroups differentiated by gender and first-generation status. Notably, it highlights how competitively designed incentives can exacerbate performance disparities between high and low achievers. This research thereby serves as a crucial reference for future studies and policy formulation aimed at optimizing incentive structures in higher education.

#### 4.2. Limitations

Several limits of this meta-analysis should be pointed out. First, while I supplemented the published primary research with three large datasets containing unpublished data, the number of the included studies remains relatively low - especially when subgroups are considered. Apart from the fact that the low number of studies limits the statistical power of the models, it limits the possibilities of T. Lintner modlel weaighite working with more appropriate models, for example, multilevel models that would contain the nested nature of the studies by Leuven et al. (2011) and MDRC. The low number of studies also limited my ability to more appropriately address publication bias, such as by using selection models. Similarly, the results of the meta-regression model on the moderating role of designs limiting the number of reward recipients for students from the upper high school median on credits earned should only be taken as tentative evidence since it is based on a comparison of a single study against three other studies. Furthermore, this work focused on a range of outcome variables, which were available in the included studies; however, it does not capture all dimensions of academic performance, such as timely degree completion. While the quality of the included studies concerning the risk of bias was found to be good, most studies were found to have some concerns of risk of bias stemming from researchers not following pre-specified analysis plans. Finally, all included research comes from developed high-income countries and the findings may therefore be of limited applicability to educational systems in developing and low-income countries.

#### 4.3. Implications

This study supports the use of performance-based financial incentives for higher education students to increase their credits earned, GPA, and exam performance - although the cost-effectiveness of the incentives was not a subject of this study. This meta-analysis T. Lintner provides evidence that, overall, performance-based financial incentives for higher education students increase all three studied out­ comes. However, it also shows that the effects are not homogenous and the design limiting the number of reward recipients might be more beneficial to students with better high school achievement, hence widening the gap between high and low achievers. Incentive schemes aimed at improving the performance of a wide spectrum of higher education students should be designed in a way that reflects these heterogenous effects - the incentives ' benchmarks should be attainable by most - if not by all - students, and the number of the recipients should not be limited by a strict number or percentage of the best-performing students. When accounting for the null finding on the moderating role of the incentive amount, a tentative claim can be made - it is more beneficial to provide lower-amount in­ centives attainable by a larger number of students than to provide higher-amount incentives attainable only by a small number of students.


<!-- p:19 -->


This study also provides guidance for future research. It would be beneficial to conduct RCTs that examine a wide array of effects of performance-based incentives on higher education students, including their impact on degree completion. Furthermore, I found un­ explained variance between the studies, and adding terms on incentive amount and provision to low-income students does not fully explain this. It would therefore be beneficial to explore what moderates the effectiveness of performance-based incentives in more detail - perhaps accounting for students ' unmet needs and measures of intrinsic motivation. The problematics of intrinsic motivation among financially incentivized higher-education students would also benefit from qualitative inquiry aiming to explore how student motivation  changes  with  the  introduction  of  incentives. ### 5. Conclusion

The use of performance-based financial incentives in higher education is widespread. Often, as with other practices in education, the incentives are designed and offered without any regard to the available evidence. However, even if stakeholders wanted to consult the research on the effects of incentives, the findings were far from conclusive. This meta-analysis synthesized all the existing causal evidence on the effect of performance-based incentives on higher education students. While this study provides results which are still far from definitive and many questions remain unanswered, I believe it provides a solid foundation for evidence-based incentive schemes in higher education.
