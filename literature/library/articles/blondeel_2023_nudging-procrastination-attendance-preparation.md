#### A little push in the back: nudging students to improve procrastination, class attendance and preparation

Eva Blondeel , Patricia Everaert and Evelien Opdecam

Department of Accountancy, Corporate Finance and Taxation, Ghent University, Ghent, Belgium

## ABSTRACT

Procrastination is a prevalent problem in higher education, leading to lower performance. Procrastination is often linked with low class attendance and little class preparation, which are two important foundations to process learning materials. Hence, interventions are recommended to overcome procrastination. One promising intervention is nudging. Based on the motivational Expectancy-Value Theory, the current study aims to (1) examine the change in procrastination throughout a semester and (2) investigate whether a nudging intervention can reduce procrastination, increase class attendance and class preparation, and, subsequently, improve performance. A random assignment to treatment experiment is executed in the exercise classes of a fi rst-year undergraduate course ( N = 211). The treatment group receives fi ve di ff erent nudges throughout the semester, the control group receives no nudges. Nudging was implemented by including an additional sentence (with a link) in the announcements on the Virtual Learning Environment (VLE). Procrastination is measured four times. Class attendance and class preparation are measured weekly. Results show increasing procrastination in the fi rst part of the semester, followed by decreasing procrastination. Additionally, simply providing students with nudges does not yield bene fi cial e ff ects. However, students clicking more intensively on the nudges report lower procrastination and higher class attendance and preparation. Moreover, the decreased procrastination and increased class preparation caused by intensively clicking on the nudges subsequently resulted in higher performance. This paper contributes to higher education practice by informing educators about the change in procrastination throughout a semester and by o ff ering educators and policy-makers information about the e ff ect of nudging on procrastination.

## Introduction

Many higher education students procrastinate consistently and problematically (van der Hulst and Jansen 2002), making it a predominant problem in education (van Eerde and Klingsieck 2018). Procrastination is a voluntary and irrational delay of an intended course of action (Steel 2007), leading to unfavourable consequences such as lower performance (Steel et al. 2018). This delay is caused by poor self-regulation skills, resulting in suboptimal learning choices. Given these negative implications, procrastination has gained scholarly interest during the past decades (Patrzek et al. 2015).

First-year students are especially vulnerable to the problem of procrastination (Trautwein and Bosse 2017). Research con fi rms that many fi rst-year students drop out of higher education (Malm,

Bryngfors, and Mörner 2012), due to procrastination and the accompanying lower performance. Additionally, the problem of dropout and procrastination is prevalent in most European universities, which have an open-gate system and low tuition fees (Sneyers and De Witte 2017). An open-gate system is characterised by no admission tests, frequently resulting in high heterogeneity among incoming students in terms of prior knowledge, attitudes, and skills (Pinxten et al. 2019). In addition, low tuition fees result in limited resources for the universities, leading to large class units (Cavalletti et al. 2021), where class attendance is not mandatory. Given the high anonymity in voluntary classes with large student groups, students are more vulnerable to procrastination behaviour (Broos et al. 2020; Sangster, Stoner, and Flood 2020), making it a tremendously important challenge for higher education institutions to help these students progress through their educational process (Arnold 2015).

In order to help students combat procrastination, information is needed on the lapse of procrastination. Hence, the fi rst objective of this paper is to examine procrastination behaviour throughout the semester of a fi rst-year undergraduate course at an open-gate university.

Literature states that, in general, students tend to procrastinate on completing assignments (Katz, Eilot, and Nevo 2014). More speci fi cally, Gafni and Geri (2010) point out that students procrastinate more on voluntary assignments compared to mandatory assignments. Additionally, low class attendance (Credé, Roch, and Kieszczynka 2010) and low class preparation (van Blerkom 2001) are resulting from students lacking self-regulation (Stewart, Stott, and Nuttall 2016), which is induced by procrastination. However, previous research shows that class attendance and class preparation are needed for better information retention and performance (Sothan 2019). By following the class pace and by preparing before coming to class, students automatically adopt the bene fi cial spaced studying approach (Yumu ş ak 2020), spreading study time over several di ff erent study sessions, leading to favourable learning outcomes, instead of the unfavourable cramming approach (Credé, Roch, and Kieszczynka 2010), where students do most of their studying in a few days before the examination.

Previous studies discussing students ' procrastination throughout the semester mention several views on the lapse of procrastination. Some argue procrastination to be a steady concept, stable across di ff erent tasks and contexts (Steel 2007). Other studies have found that students ' procrastination starts low, increases during the semester, and decreases again at the end of the semester when approaching the exam (Moon and Illingworth 2005; Wessel, Bradley, and Hood 2019). Other studies claim procrastination to be a dynamic behaviour, changing over time depending on the task and the context (Kljajic and Gaudreau 2018; Moon and Illingworth 2005). In the current setting with only one exam at the end of the semester, it is consequently expected that procrastination decreases as the exam approaches, i.e. procrastination is decreasing throughout the semester (hypothesis 1).

As procrastination is expected to decrease towards the exam, it is important to implement an intervention which triggers students early on in the semester. Hence, the second objective of this study is to examine if a nudging intervention can e ff ectively reduce procrastination, increase class attendance, and increase preparation before coming to class, and, subsequently, results in higher performance. Nudging is de fi ned as ' altering people ' s behaviours in a predictable way without forbidding any options or changing their incentives ' (Thaler and Sunstein 2021, 8). These nudges guide behaviour towards desired outcomes, i.e. lower procrastination, higher class attendance and higher class preparation. Students are nudged to set a standard, the so-called work and study habits, to come to class and to prepare beforehand (van Azam et al. 2021; Eerde and Klingsieck 2018), positively impacting their self-regulatory skills. This is important for fi rst-year students at an open-gate university, where learning is less controlled (Torenbeek, Jansen, and Suhre 2013), and which requires more support to develop self-regulatory skills (Cameron and Rideout 2022).

Nudging is a successful, low-cost and subtle strategy implemented in various fi elds (Thaler and Sunstein 2021). However, nudging interventions in education are scarce (Weijers, de Koning, and Paas 2020), despite the faculty ' s desire to give students a little push in the back in the direction of better educational choices (Damgaard and Nielsen 2018). Hence, this study implements a random assignment to treatment experiment with a nudged treatment group and a non-nudged control group to determine if nudging reduces procrastination and leads to higher performance.

Damgaard and Nielsen ' s (2018) literature review suggests the e ff ectiveness of nudging, but it depends on the context and the type of nudge, indicating that there is no single nudge that is e ff ective in all situations. Additionally, inconsistent results have been found on the e ff ect of nudging on procrastination (Fouh, Lee, and Baker 2021; Weijers et al. 2022) and performance (Azam et al. 2021; Hellings and Haelermans 2022), possibly due to the use of only one nudge, with the type of nudge and the course setting of the nudge being di ff erent in each study.

Therefore, this study uses fi ve di ff erent nudges throughout the semester (a graph showing the bene fi ts of class attendance and class preparation, a template to structure the exercise, a summary slide of the theory, automatic enrolment to the mock exam, and a hint about how to solve a di ffi cult question in the assignment), as repeating the same nudge is less e ff ective (Damgaard and Nielsen 2018). Adding variation to the nudges is in line with the variation theory, stating that students ' see, understand, or experience a given phenomenon or message in a certain way ' (Bussey, Orgill, and Crippen 2013, 9). By adding variation to each nudge, students ' awareness related to the message distributed by the nudge will increase and will positively in fl uence their self-regulatory skills, as suggested by Stewart, Stott, and Nuttall (2016). The nudges are made available on the Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) for all treatment group students, but students voluntarily choose whether or not they click on the nudge. It is expected that nudging will decrease procrastination (hypothesis 2), increase class attendance (hypothesis 3), increase class preparation (hypothesis 4), and increase performance (hypothesis 5). It is also expected that reduced procrastination, increased class attendance and increased preparation will lead to an increase in students ' academic performance as they are mediators in the relation between nudging and performance (hypotheses 6a, 6b, 6c).

The study responds to the call for more applications of nudging in education (Weijers, de Koning, and Paas 2020) and adds to higher education literature in several ways. First , this study implements fi ve di ff erent nudges, avoiding students being bored by the same nudges. Other studies mainly implemented only one or two nudges (Damgaard and Nielsen 2018) but found ambiguous results.

Second , the e ff ect of the nudging intervention is measured on three di ff erent outcome variables in addition to performance: (1) procrastination, (2) class attendance, and (3) class preparation. Few studies have included class attendance and class preparation within the context of procrastination, even though many students skip some classes or do not prepare for class (Credé, Roch, and Kieszczynka 2010).

Third , earlier research mainly measured the outcomes of nudging at one point in time (Weijers et al. 2022). This study measures the e ff ect of nudging on procrastination, which is measured at four di ff erent moments in time (week two, fi ve, nine and 12) with both a self-reported measure and an objective measure collected through the VLE for procrastination. In addition, the e ff ect of nudging is also investigated on class attendance and class preparation, which are both measured weekly (week two through 12). Finally, the e ff ect of nudging on performance is investigated, where performance is measured once at the end of the semester.

Fourth , most studies examined either the e ff ect of nudging on performance, or the e ff ect of nudging on procrastination (Weijers, de Koning, and Paas 2020). To the best of our knowledge, no other research focuses on both elements. Therefore, this study made it possible to test both the direct e ff ect of nudging on performance, as well as the indirect e ff ect by including procrastination, class attendance, and class preparation as mediators in the relation between nudging and performance.

Finally , the study tracks procrastination throughout the semester to identify when procrastination is at its highest. It supplements prior literature on investigating the dynamic nature of students ' procrastination throughout the semester (Moon and Illingworth 2005; Wessel, Bradley, and Hood 2019) and provides educators and policy-makers information on when to introduce nudging interventions.

## Theoretical framework and hypotheses

### Theoretical framework

The Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT) of achievement motivation (Wig fi eld and Eccles 2000) is used as a theoretical framework and has been earlier applied to di ff erent educational settings (de Vaughn, Taasoobshirazi, and Johnson 2020; Zordo, Hagenauer, and Hascher 2019). EVT states that motivation determines why a student chooses to engage in an academic task or not (Wig fi eld and Eccles 2000). Both motivation and self-regulation are widely examined concepts in relation to procrastination, as decreased motivation and low self-regulation cause higher procrastination (Steel 2007). Elevated levels of procrastination lead to students not engaging in the academic tasks.

Motivational theories in education, such as the EVT, try to understand how a student ' s choice, persistence, and e ff ort are related to their motivation. The EVT states that students choose to engage in and achieve an academic task if they believe they will be capable of performing the task (expectancy of success) and if they value or care about the task (subjective task value). Both components are referred to as students ' motivational beliefs. These motivational beliefs in fl uence students ' achievement-related choices (de Zordo, Hagenauer, and Hascher 2019); they in fl uence if a student is motivated to engage in the task. Applied to this study, both motivational beliefs in fl uence whether or not students will procrastinate less, come to class more often, and prepare more for class. Under the EVT, it is assumed that when a student believes they have the capacity to succeed the task and value the task, they will engage in the task, which will result in higher performance. By nudging students, the belief in their ability to succeed in the task and the value of the task are positively in fl uenced. This is because the nudges of this study focus on enhancing students ' motivation and self-regulatory skills related to procrastination, class attendance, and class preparation. An overview of the EVT within the current study can be found in Figure 1.

The hypotheses state that procrastination decreases throughout the semester and that nudging leads to lower procrastination, higher class attendance, and higher class preparation, resulting in higher performance. Figure 2 shows an overview of the hypotheses.

## Materials and methods

### Setting

This study took place in a fi rst-year undergraduate fi nancial accounting course at a researchoriented, open-gate European university. Study participants were 18 or 19 years old. The course consisted of 12 weeks of classes, with a weekly in-person theory lecture (2.5 h) and an exercise class (1.25 h). Each week, two exercises were handled during the exercise class. Class attendance and class preparation are not mandatory and do not a ff ect students ' course grade. At the end of the semester, there is a closed-book summative paper-based exam. There are no midterm exams or homework assignments, which is usual in a Central-European context.

The intervention relates to the weekly exercise classes. These take place in a large auditorium with 150 - 200 students and include practical exercises covering applications of the content taught during the theory lectures. The study spanned from the fi rst exercise class (week two) until the last (week 12). No exercise classes took place in week eight, resulting in ten exercise classes. ### Research design

Students were randomly assigned to either the control or the treatment group. The control group receives no nudges, while the treatment group receives fi ve nudges throughout the semester. Hence, the experiment uses a longitudinal interrupted time series design, with multiple observations taken before, during, and after treatment.

### Nudging intervention

All students receive, fi ve days before the exercise class, a weekly announcement on the VLE where the instructor announces which exercises will be covered in the upcoming exercise class. However, for students in the treatment group, one sentence was added, with a link referring to the nudge. Students choose themselves whether or not they click on this link, but the additional sentence invites them to do so. The control group receives the same announcements, but without that additional sentence.

The fi ve di ff erent nudges are: (1) a video with a graph showing the bene fi ts of class attendance and class preparation, (2) a template to structure the exercise, (3) a summary slide of the theory, (4) automatic enrolment to the mock exam (with the possibility to decline), and (5) a hint about how to solve a di ffi cult question in the assignment. The nudges were established after focus group conversations with prior year students. During the exercise classes, the template (2), summary slide (3) and hint (5) were disclosed to all students, as well as the information on nudge (1) and (4). This was done for ethical reasons, in order for all students to have the same learning material.

The fact that all students received the same announcement prevents a potential spillover e ff ect of nudging information between both groups before the exercise class takes place. Moreover, this risk is also minimised by thoroughly thinking about how to set up the control and treatment group. More speci fi cally, all students are randomly allocated to twelve small groups at the beginning of their fi rst undergraduate year, based on the university system. In these smaller groups, they played a welcome game before the start of the academic year and followed two language courses in these smaller groups. Therefore, it can be expected that most of the students got to know their friends at university in that small group as this was the fi rst group of peers they got to meet when starting at university. Based on these small groups, we randomly assigned students to the control group or the experimental group on the ' small group ' level (i.e. all students of one group are assigned to either the control or the treatment group). By doing this, we could assume that chances are high that friends are together in the control group or together in the experimental group. Furthermore, the instructor of the exercise classes never received questions on this matter.

### Measurement scales

Nudging is measured with two variables. Nudging group is a dummy variable indicating if a student is part of the control group (nudging grou p = 0) or the treatment group (nudging grou p = 1). Nudging intensity shows how many nudges a student of the treatment group clicked on, hence only including students from the treatment group.

Two surveys were operationalised during the experiment. All surveys were paper-based and conducted during class time by all students who were present. Students completed an informed consent form beforehand. The researcher stressed that students ' answers on the survey will not impact their course performance and will not be read by the instructor, reducing a potential social desirability bias. A fi rst questionnaire measures students ' procrastination. More speci fi cally, procrastination was measured with the validated fi ve item Academic Procrastination Scale - Short (APS-S) questionnaire (Yockey 2016) at the start (week two), two times during the semester (week fi ve and week nine) and at the end (week 12) with a fi ve-point Likert scale. Translation and backtranslation from English to Dutch was performed, and items were made course speci fi c. Cronbach ' s alpha was high for all four timings (0.813, 0.875, 0.889, 0.934), showing convincing factor loadings (Table 1). Missing data was handled with the expectation-maximization algorithm for Maximum Likelihood Estimation (Schafer and Graham 2002). Little ' s MCAR test shows that the data points are missing completely at random ( χ 2 (254) = 278.36, p = .141), allowing to use this algorithm.

To supplement the self-reported procrastination measure, an objective procrastination measure is included which is used as robustness check. 1 The objective procrastination measure is constructed by collecting log data from the VLE, registering whether or not a student has downloaded the exercise assignment before the exercise class and whether or not a student has downloaded the solution of the assignment within one week after the exercise class. Both download measures are summed up to construct the objective procrastination measure. The variable is indicated as a percentage relative to the total available downloads (0 - 100).

The second survey collects weekly data on students ' class attendance and class preparation for the exercise class. Class attendance measures the number of attended exercise classes for each student throughout the semester (1 - 10). Students who fi lled out the weekly questionnaire were registered as present in-class, other students were marked as absent. Students who never attended one exercise class are removed from the sample. Class preparation represents the total number of exercises that the student prepared at home weighted by the number of classes that the student was present (0 - 200). Each week, students fi lled out how many exercises they prepared for the exercise class. Two exercises were handled during each exercise class. Hence, students could prepare a maximum of 20 exercises throughout the semester. The sum of the number of prepared exercises throughout the semester is multiplied with the number of exercise classes attended during the semester. By this, more weight is given to students attending more classes compared to students attending less classes. This is justi fi ed by the fact that the course content gradually builds up. It is therefore important that a student prepares each week instead of only a couple of weeks during the semester. This is why a weighted variable is operationalised. For example, Student A attended fi ve exercise classes during the semester and prepared two exercises for each of the fi ve classes. Hence, the sum of exercises prepared is 10; the number of classes attended is 5. To calculate the variable class preparation, 10 is multiplied by 5, resulting in s score of 50. Student B also attended all ten exercise classes, but only prepared one exercise for each class. Hence, the sum of exercises prepared is again 10, but the number of attended classes is 10, resulting in a score of 100 (10 multiplied by 10). This score is twice the score of student A, since student B had prepared for double the amount of topics. Hence, more weight is given to the class preparation of student B as it is more important to catch up with the material each week instead of focusing on the material of only a few weeks.

Performance is measured as the score on the fi nal summative exam (score on 20). Students should obtain 10 out of 20 to pass the course.

To control for potential endogeneity of unobserved student characteristics, di ff erent control variables were included. Ability is measured by the grade point average (GPA) of the student for the fi rst semester courses excluding the accounting course (Fall), and is calculated as a weighted average of the achieved grades for all courses of the fi rst semester, weighted by study credits (total score on 100). 2 Gender is measured as 0 for male and 1 for female and students ' level of procrastination at t 0 is measured at week two. By including these control variables, the e ff ect of the nudging intervention can be examined regardless of students ' ability, their gender, and procrastination level at the start of the semester.

### Sample

Out of the 302 enrolled students (150 randomly assigned to the treatment group and 152 to the control group), 211 were included in the study. 28 students of the treatment group and 36 students of the control group never attended one exercise class during the semester. These students were removed from the sample, due to the risk of measuring dropout instead of procrastination. Of these, 11 students of the treatment group and 13 students of the control group did not take part in the fi nal exam. Another three students (one of the treatment group and two of the control group) were excluded from the sample, as these were master students, taking the course as an elective. This resulted in a fi nal sample of 211 students: 110 in the treatment group and 101 in the control group.

### Correlations

Variable correlations are presented in Table 4. ## Discussion

The current study introduces a nudging intervention for fi rst-year higher education students ( N = 211), as more fi rst-year support regarding procrastination is needed (Trautwein and Bosse 2017). The study examines how procrastination evolves throughout the semester and how students can be nudged towards lower procrastination, higher class attendance, higher class preparation, and subsequently higher performance.

The data revealed that there is no signi fi cant di ff erence in procrastination between the fi rst and last measurement, not supporting H1. Procrastination fl uctuates throughout the semester, with an initial increase until week fi ve, followed by a decrease. This curvilinear trajectory is in line with other studies (Moon and Illingworth 2005) which also found procrastination to increase and then decrease by the end of the semester as the fi nal exam approaches (Damgaard and Nielsen 2018). However, this di ff ers from Steel ' s (2007) meta-analysis, where procrastination had a steep increase followed by stabilisation. Nonetheless, the signi fi cant increase at the beginning of the semester is problematic and shows, consistent with Steel et al. (2018), that the nature of procrastination remains irrational due to poor self-regulation skills (Torenbeek, Jansen, and Suhre 2013).

To ensure a successful intervention, and since students seem to be at ease at the beginning of the semester, the study implements fi ve di ff erent nudges based on the Expectancy-Value Theory. The nudges focus on increasing students ' motivational beliefs and self-regulatory skills.

The results con fi rm that students who intensively clicked on the nudges did experience lower procrastination (H2), higher class attendance (H3), higher class preparation (H4), and higher performance (H5). Nudging is consequently an e ff ective tool to steer students in the direction of a certain desired behaviour. However, just providing students with nudges is not su ffi cient, as signi fi cant results are lacking for the nudging group variable. Hence, students need to click on the nudges in order to experience bene fi cial e ff ects.

The current innovative nudging design adds to the existing higher education literature as some previous studies failed to nudge students towards lower procrastination (Fouh, Lee, and Baker 2021),


<!-- p:17 -->


16

higher class attendance (Weijers et al. 2022) or higher performance (Hellings and Haelermans 2022). The current study serves as an e ff ective solution to curtail procrastination and boost class attendance and preparation, as suggested by Sothan (2019), and to enhance performance (e.g. Azam et al. 2021). As expected based on the principles of the EVT (Wig fi eld and Eccles 2000), clicking on the nudges makes students become more self-regulated (Cameron and Rideout 2022; Stewart, Stott, and Nuttall 2016) and aware of the bene fi ts of lower procrastination and higher class attendance and preparation. The positive motivational beliefs turn into better academic-related choices, exhibited by lower procrastination and higher class attendance, preparation, and performance.

The mediation analyses proved that nudging intensity partially a ff ects performance via lower procrastination and better class preparation, con fi rming H6a and H6c. Students intensively clicking on the nudges experience lower procrastination at the end of the semester and higher class preparation, leading to a higher score on the fi nal exam. This is in line with the EVT, as the positive academic-related choices lead to higher performance (Wig fi eld and Eccles 2000). The positive e ff ect of class preparation on performance is in line with prior research by Pearce (2018), as preparation requires active involvement within the learning process (Bridgstock and Backhouse 1982). In contrast to previous studies (Credé, Roch, and Kieszczynka 2010; Sothan 2019), the current research was not able to prove that the e ff ect of nudging intensity on performance was explained by the positive association of class attendance, rejecting H6b. Accounting, i.e. the subject under study, is a technical topic and just attending class without preparing at home is not su ffi cient to perform well on the exam.

However, some critical notes are required. Despite all e ff orts to nudge students throughout the semester, 25 of the 110 students (23%) did not click on any nudge during the semester, indicating a tremendous problem with student engagement in the learning process. Previous studies have also mentioned the low levels of student engagement (Blondeel, Everaert, and Opdecam 2022). Van der Hulst and Jansen (2002) mention the ' competition for attention ' between di ff erent courses as a potential reason. However, research remains scarce on why students procrastinate during the semester.

Moreover, no e ff ect could be found with the nudging group variable. In other words, just providing nudges to students does not seem enough. However, when students click intensively to these nudges, positive e ff ects are found. Students need to be actively engaged in the learning process and click on the nudges in order to experience a bene fi cial e ff ect. Reasons for this lack of e ff ect remain unexplored. Potential explanations could be that the sample size was too small to obtain signi fi cant results, that the nudges were too subtle and not compelling enough for students to look at, or that there were too many or not enough nudges. However, previous research also found ambiguous results related to nudging (Damgaard and Nielsen 2018). The fact that this study has not found an e ff ect of just providing nudges could potentially explain the lack of signi fi cant results in other studies, as intensive exposure to the nudges is necessary in order to experience bene fi cial impacts.

Additionally, many students clicked on only one nudge. This could be due to the di ff erent types of nudges. More speci fi cally, the fi rst nudge (graph) is not directly related to the course material, whereas the four other nudges are designed to be course related, for example providing additional help for exercises. Consequently, it is di ffi cult to interpret the speci fi c group of students within the treatment group who clicked on only one nudge. It is possible that by clicking on this particular nudge, students lost interest in the course, although this was absolutely not the intention. However, it can also be possible that a novice e ff ect was present, i.e. students click the fi rst time they see something new.

## Limitations, recommendations for future research, and conclusions

The study has limitations, creating opportunities for future research. First , data collection relies on self-reported measures for procrastination and preparation. Log data was investigated to supplement the subjective measure on procrastination, leading to robust results. However, the


<!-- p:18 -->


17

assumption that absent students have not prepared the exercises at home, could not be tested otherwise. Future studies are encouraged to use other methods to measure preparation at home (e.g. by tracking log time for online exercises).

Second , as previously mentioned, a limitation of this study is that many students only clicked on one nudge. Future research could focus on why students engaged with the nudge (i.e. by clicking on the link) and whether there is a di ff erence in engagement based on the type of nudge and/or the timing during the semester. The current research design does not allow to conclude which nudge is more e ff ective, as only one sequence of nudges has been investigated. An appropriate design should implement di ff erent sequences of nudges to di ff erent groups, in order to rule out a possible sequence e ff ect (i.e. students engaging more with a certain nudge because of the content of the nudge or because of the timing when the nudge was distributed). Therefore, a call is raised for more research to identify the most e ff ective nudge and the most e ff ective timing of the nudges.

Third , the weekly in-class questionnaires may have unintentionally nudged both the control and the treatment group, explaining why no e ff ect of the nudging group variable was found. Additionally, the e ff ect of the nudges could be mitigated by students exchanging information between the control and the treatment group and by students giving socially desirable answers on the survey.

Fourth, the data does not allow to examine the di ff erence in clicking on a nudge (for example clicking on the template) versus acting on that nudge (for example using the template to prepare the exercise). Future research should gather more in-depth data which allows to disentangle the di ff erence between both actions and consequently examining its e ff ectiveness.

Fifth , the study does not include the in fl uence of students ' motivation on their clicking behaviour on the nudges. However, the proven results regarding nudging intensity could potentially be explained by the e ff ect of motivation as more motivated students might be more likely to click on all the nudges, procrastinate less, attend all classes, prepare all exercises, and obtain a higher grade. Hence, it is recommended for future research to examine if more motivated students react more intensively on the nudges. We recommend future research to build on the theoretical foundations of the Self-Determination Theory (Deci and Ryan 2000), as this theory suits with the design of the di ff erent nudges. Moreover, the current results do not elaborate on potential di ff erences in students ' ability levels and its in fl uence on the intensity with which students click on the nudges. Hence, it would be interesting for future research to examine these potential di ff erences.

To conclude, three main recommendations for higher education educators can be derived from this study. First , procrastination varies throughout the semester, with the highest peak in week fi ve. Hence, it might be an idea to implement interventions to help students combat procrastination, not in week one or two, but after a few weeks into the semester. Second , this study con fi rmed the positive e ff ects of a nudging intervention on procrastination, class attendance, class preparation, and performance. Students engaging with the nudges (i.e. regularly clicking on the link in the announcement on the VLE), exhibit less procrastination, come to class more often, prepare more before coming to class, and obtain a higher grade on the fi nal exam. Third , both procrastination at the end of the semester and preparation of the exercises at home play an important role in explaining students ' course grades at the end of the semester. Initiatives should therefore focus on preparing the exercises at home and boosting active learning participation rather than simply increasing class attendance for the exercise classes. Hence, the implemented nudges should focus on establishing work habits and improving self-regulation.
