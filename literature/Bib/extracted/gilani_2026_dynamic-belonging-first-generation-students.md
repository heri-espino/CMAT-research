##### Dynamic belonging - how student belonging changes over time for first-generation students

David Gilani a ,  Liz Thomas b and  Daniel Mcarthur b

a Head of Access and Participation, Middlesex University, London, United Kingdom; b Centre for Research on Education and Social Justice, University of York, York, United Kingdom

## ABSTRACT

Whilst  students'  sense  of  belonging  (SoB)  -  an  important  concept  in relation  to  student  success  -  is  accepted  as  changing  over  time,  most existing  research  investigates  this  concept  as  a  static  phenomenon, using  single,  snapshot  measurements.  This  mixed-method  study  utilises a  longitudinal  approach  to  measure  students'  sense  of  belonging  at multiple  points  throughout  the  first  year  of  undergraduate  study.  101 students  from  two  English  universities  completed  questionnaires  and online  diaries  as  part  of  a  larger  study  on  student  success  and  SoB. Linear  mixed  models  and  data  visualisation  analyses  show  that  overall students'  sense  of  belonging  declines  over  the  first  year  of  study.  This has  implications  for  institutions  in  how  they  plan  their  approaches  to support students with their sense of belonging, including challenges for potential  'frontloading'  of  welcome  interventions  near  the  start  of  the first  term.  The  analyses  also  found  that  first-generation  students  had significantly  lower  SoB  than  their  non-first-generation  counterparts,  but only  later  in  the  first  year  of  study.  Coding  matrix  queries  were  then utilised  based  on  students'  detailed  responses  to  online  diaries  to  help interpret  and  contextualise  the  quantitative  findings.  These  subsequent analyses  found  that  fragile  relationships  with  peers  on  the  course, cultural  barriers,  and  a  lower  propensity  to  talk  about  finances  or  feel that  they  mattered  to  staff  were  more  common  among  first-generation students.  Overall,  this  article  furthers  understanding  of  the  dynamic nature  of  belonging  and  how  it  is  experienced  differently  by  firstgeneration  students,  with  insights  into  what  may  be  causing  these divergent experiences over time.

## Introduction

Students' sense of belonging (SoB) is an increasingly investigated concept in higher education litera­ ture  (Gilani  and  Thomas  2025;  Nguyen  et  al.  2025),  due  to  its  prominent  connections  to  student success. Existing research has shown that a strong sense of belonging amongst students is signifi­ cantly  predictive  of  positive  academic  performance  (Veldman,  Meeussen,  and  van  Laar  2023), engagement (Zumbrunn et al. 2014), mental wellbeing (Kahu, Ashley, and Picton 2022) and retention rates (Gopalan and Brady 2019; Gilani, McArthur, and Thomas 2024; Nguyen et al. 2025). It is this comfort, safety and authenticity, which allows students to engage in meaningful ways across their studies and wider aspects of uni­ versity life; ultimately driving the successful outcomes sought after by students, universities and the governments that fund them.


<!-- p:3 -->

Belonging  is  recognised  as  being  dynamic,  influenced  by  various  factors  such  as  social  inter­ actions,  institutional  support,  and  personal  reflections  (Thomas  2012;  Gravett  and  Ajjawi  2021; O'Shea 2020). Despite this, the majority of existing research into this topic investigates belonging as  a  static  phenomenon,  by  only  measuring  or  exploring  belonging  at  a  single  point  in  time (Gilani,  McArthur,  and  Thomas  2024;  Nguyen  et  al.  2025).  Such  approaches  can  only  provide  a limited  understanding  of  SoB,  as  they  can't  explore  how  it  changes  over  time  and  thus  how different  factors  -  or  efforts  to  try  to  improve  students'  SoB  -  may  only  be  impactful  at  certain points in students' higher education journeys.

Belonging - or a lack thereof it - is especially important at the start of students' time at university. Students are most likely to withdraw from their studies in the first year (Hillman 2021) and often make key decisions about whether they will stay at university by the end of the first term (Christie, Munro, and Fisher  2004;  Webb  and  Cotton  2018).  Given  this  increased  'risk'  of  withdrawal  earlier  in  the  student journey,  universities  are  often  advised  to  provide  supportive  interventions  as  early  as  possible (Austen et al. 2021). However, this frontloading approach can often mean that universities do not main­ tain their efforts to support belonging throughout the student journey. Some longitudinal research into students' SoB suggests that it decreases across the first year of study (Hausmann et al., 2007). Changes in SoB may also vary differently based on students' demographic background or routes to joining univer­ sity  (O'Sullivan  et  al.,  2019;  Ruedas-Gracia  et  al.,  2023).  To  ensure  that  these  different  experiences  of belonging can be fully explored, this study utilises the conceptual framework of the Bronfenbrenner model  of  human  ecological  development  to  better  investigate  how  individuals'  experiences  -  and their sense of belonging - are influenced by wider socio-economic and historical systems.

These complexities highlight the importance and potential benefit of longitudinal research into student belonging that can provide detail into when students may be most likely to report negative changes in their sense of belonging, which groups of students are most at risk of these changes, and what is happening within students' experiences to drive these changes. The purpose of this research was to address these gaps through the following three research questions:

RQ1: How does students' SoB change over the first academic year of study?

RQ2: Do changes in students' SoB vary based on demographic variables?

RQ3: What factors could be influencing a decline in SoB amongst first-generation students (students where no parents or caregivers previously attended university)?

The  study  utilised  a  longitudinal,  mixed-methods  approach  with  101  participants  from  two English universities. To address RQ1 - how SoB changes over the first academic year - descriptive statistics,  linear  mixed  models,  and  line  graphs  revealed  a  consistent  decline,  from  an  average score  of  78.1  in  October  to  72.9  in  May,  echoing  trends  found  in  prior  research  (Hausmann  et al., 2007). For RQ2 - whether SoB changes vary by demographics - linear mixed modelling ident­ ified  first-generation  status  as  the  only  significant  factor,  with  a  sharper  decline  in  SoB  among first-generation students. To explore this decline and address RQ3, thematic analysis of students' reflective online diaries was conducted using NVivo. First-generation students more frequently reported isolation and social fragmentation and were less likely to describe help-seeking beha­ viours or feeling valued by staff. Findings support existing literature and highlight the need for targeted, timely belonging interventions, as well as continued efforts to address the systemic bar­ riers to first-generation students found across universities. Methodological limitations - including a very small sample size - are acknowledged, with suggestions for future research improvements.

## Literature review

### Conceptualising sense of belonging in higher education

Research into the nature of belonging amongst students, including its links to successful student out­ comes, affecting factors and efforts to enable it, is increasingly prevalent (Gilani and Thomas, 2025). Broadly defined, sense of belonging (SoB) in higher education refers to a student's perceived social support on campus, a feeling of connectedness, and the experience of 'mattering' or being important to the institution (Strayhorn, 2019). Whilst this concept is accepted as multi-dimensional, messy and complex (Ajjawi et al., 2023), enhancing students' SoB is seen as a beneficial aim within the higher edu­ cation context, due to its close connections to various aspects of student success (Gillen-O'Neel, 2021; Veldman et al., 2023; Gilani and Thomas, 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025).

### Evolution of belonging in HE literature

The  conceptualisation  of  student  belonging  has  undergone  a  significant  evolution,  shifting  from institutional 'fit' to a more nuanced psychological and sociological need. The concept was arguably first  introduced  to  higher  education  literature  via  Tinto's  (1987;  1997)  work  on  student  retention. Tinto  theorised  that  student  persistence  was  driven  by  'integration'  -  the  extent  to  which  a student felt part of the academic and social communities of their classrooms.

However,  the  specific  positioning  of  'belonging'  as  a  distinct  construct  is  widely  attributed  to Hurtado and Carter (1997). They challenged the earlier focus on integration, which often implied stu­ dents must assimilate into the existing culture, by arguing for the importance of considering 'campus climate'. Their work shifted the focus onto how the wider university environment and 'institutional habitus' (Thomas, 2002) influence a student's sense of membership. This evolution was furthered by Strayhorn (2019), who conceptualised student belonging as a basic human need and a prerequisite for success. Strayhorn's model identifies core elements of belonging: it is a motive that drives behav­ iour, is time-dependent and context-specific, relates to mattering, and is heavily influenced by a stu­ dent's intersecting identities.

### Challenges in definition and scope

One notable and frequent complication in the literature around SoB research is how the concept is  defined differently across different studies and disciplines (Dias-Broens et al., 2024), as well as often being conflated with similar terms, such as engagement or involvement (Strayhorn, 2022). Furthermore,  student  perceptions  of  belonging  are  deeply  subjective.  When  asked  about defining  belonging,  students  often  talk  about  feelings  and  perception:  'Perception  is  at  the heart  of  belonging'  (Cook-Sather  and  Seay,  2021,  p.740).  This  is  often  described  metaphorically as  feeling  at  home  (Ahn  and  Davis,  2020)  and  of  being  able  to  be  one's  true,  authentic  self (Picton et al., 2017).

### Conceptual framework: an ecological approach

Studies  also  find  that  students  may  also  have  different  belonging  needs  due  to  many  different characteristics; such as their involvement in religious communities (Holloway-Friesen, 2019), distance that  they  live  from  university  (Pokorny  et  al.,  2016),  other  demographic  factors  (Means  and  Pyne, 2017; Fernández et al., 2023) and types of personality (Stahl and McDonald, 2024; Stubblebine et al.,  2024).  For  this  study,  it  is  argued  that  a  conceptualisation  of  students'  SoB  needs  to  consider all  of  these  'external  to  HE'  factors  that  could  be  influencing  students'  sense  of  belonging  in  a different way for each student. As is argued by Strayhorn, 'What works for helping one to belong may not work for someone else' (2019, p.99).

Numerous studies have conceptualised this aspect of individualised belonging needs through the Bronfenbrenner Ecological Model of Human Development (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Hamwey et al., 2019; Mulisa, 2019). This framework recognises the different environments through which individ­ uals' experiences - and their sense of belonging - are influenced by wider socio-economic and his­ torical  systems,  both  within  and  beyond  students'  experiences  of  higher  education  (Renn  and Arnold, 2003). For the example of first-generation students, this framework helps to contextualise how these students not only may not have access to family members with experience of higher edu­ cation but also be less likely to have attended schools that placed the same importance on HE par­ ticipation (Collier and Morgan, 2007).

Whilst the Bronfenbrenner Model places the individual at the centre, it recognises how the indi­ vidual is shaped by various social, environmental, cultural and political factors over time (Bronfen­ brenner,  1979;  Figure  1).  These  are  all  domains  through  which  student  belonging  could  be enabled or thwarted, and these vary depending on the individual (Hamwey et al., 2019). Utilising

### How student belonging changes over time

Despite  belonging  being  fluid  and  transient  in  nature  (Gravett  and  Ajjawi,  2021),  most  existing research  looks  at  belonging  at  a  single  point  in  time.  Where  longitudinal  research  does  exist looking into students' SoB it contributes to our understanding of how belonging fluctuates across students' course of study, and how the factors effecting belonging may change over time.

Longitudinal qualitative research has been utilised to explore how a factor or aspect of belonging is likely to affect students at different points throughout their studies (Picton et al., 2017; Viola, 2021). Whilst many studies that took this qualitative longitudinal approach did not explore how belonging changed over time, they are still useful in considering how institutions may need to support students in different ways across their higher education journeys (Means and Pyne, 2017; Kahu et al., 2020). Where longitudinal,  qualitative  research  did  explicitly  examine  how  different  aspects  of  students' SoB  changed  throughout  their  studies,  this  was  especially  welcome.  For  example,  Kahu  et  al.'s (2022) approach to their longitudinal qualitative research allowed them to explore how academic aspects  of  students'  SoB  were  more  precarious  -  prone  to  fluctuation  -  than  interpersonal  and social  aspects  of  belonging,  which  was  steadier  across  time.  However,  the  multi-dimensional nature  of  belonging  means  that  we  cannot  fully  separate  these  different  domains;  students  are more likely to develop their academic SoB if they already feel that they belong socially (Axxe, 2023).

In  some  cases  where  quantitative  longitudinal  research  has  taken  place,  belonging  is  only measured at one point and then compared to other variables at another timepoint to investigate whether  those  other  variables  were  predictive  of  higher  levels  of  belonging  (Veldman  et  al., 2023). Gillen O'Neel's work (2021) is one of the few studies that has measured belonging at multiple points in a short period of time. Their research looked at changes in belonging and engagement levels on a daily basis over a week and did find an antecedent correlation showing that increases in  belonging  led  to  subsequent  increases  in  engagement.  Where  studies  have  been  conducted that measure belonging at multiple points, results show that belonging tends to decrease during the first year of study (Hausmann et al., 2007; O'Sullivan et al., 2019), but that it is generally stable from year-to-year (Barringer et al., 2023; Ruedas-Gracia et al., 2023).

### Belonging amongst different demographics of students

Longitudinal belonging studies often caveat that their generalised findings vary when examining students from different demographics and contexts. Students entering through contextual admis­ sions  routes  and  those  from  minoritised  racial  backgrounds  often  experience  sharper  declines  in sense  of  belonging  (SoB)  relative  to  cohort  averages  (O'Sullivan  et  al.,  2019;  Ruedas-Gracia  et  al., 2023).  However,  these  disparities  are  not  merely  demographic  markers  but  are  deeply  rooted  in the classed history - and indeed present - of higher education.

Scholars such as Reay (2018) argue that higher education remains a steeply hierarchical system where  working-class  students  and  other  outsiders  are  still  expected  to  adjust  to  institutional spaces  designed  with  elite  or  middle-class  learners  in  mind.  This  sense  of  alienation  is  often  a result  of  a  mismatch  between  the  student's  personal  identities  and  the  'institutional  habitus':  the lived  culture  and  expectations  of  the  university  (Thomas,  2002;  Reay,  2017).  For  some  students from historically  underrepresented backgrounds, the university environment does not simply feel foreign  but  also  asks  students  to  engage  in  'class-straddling'  by  forcing  them  to  navigate between  the  different  expectations  and  norms  of  their  home  communities  and  the  university environment (Ardoin, 2018;  Bettencourt, 2021).  This  can  lead  to  'dual  alienation',  where  workingclass  students  feel  both  disconnected  from  their  institution  overall,  but  also  disconnected  from other  students  who  seem  to  effortlessly  integrate  into  campus  communities  (Bettencourt,  2021). These  negative  experiences  can  lead  to  students  adopting  social  concealment  strategies  as  an attempt  to  hide  their  'othered'  identity,  which  in  itself  can  result  in  a  lower  sense  of  belonging (Harrel-Hallmark et al., 2022; Veldman et al., 2023).


<!-- p:7 -->


Validating relationships with staff and relatable mentors are perhaps even more critical for SoB amongst students from low-income backgrounds or first-generation students, as these relationships help provide them with guidance of how to navigate and overcome institutional barriers (Kitchen, 2021).  However,  these  are  also  the  students  who  are  mostly  likely  to  face  barriers  to  developing these positive relationships - either through living further away from campus - commuter students - or having a greater need for part-time work to cover costs of studying, which results in less time on campus (Mendoza et al., 2016).

This classed experience is often conflated with first-generation status. While first-generation stu­ dents  are  frequently  reported  to  have  lower  levels  of  belonging  (O'Shea,  2020),  the  literature  is nuanced. Some research suggest that belonging is significantly lower for students whose parents did not attend university (Pedler et al., 2022), while others have found that first-generation students can actually develop a higher sense of belonging by their final year as they successfully navigate unfamiliar institutional  cultures  and  expectations  (Hunt  et  al.,  2017;  Dias-Broens  et  al.,  2024).  This suggests  that  for  first-generation  and  working-class  students,  SoB  is  a  dynamic,  longitudinal process of negotiation rather than a static deficit.

### Challenges for longitudinal belonging research

Through existing studies, multiple challenges have been identified to adequately measure changes in belonging. Studies have questioned how early we can begin reliably asking students to self-report SoB at university in a meaningful way, with little agreement. While some studies purposely excluded any measurements of belonging that were gathered within the first term of the first year of study (RuedasGracia et al., 2023), others decided to measure students' SoB even before they had formally begun their teaching (O'Sullivan et al., 2019). The extent to which SoB can predict outcome measures - such as retention  -  may  depend  on  when  SoB  is  measured,  with  measurements  at  the  start  of  students' studies  being  less  reliable  (Gilani  et  al,  2024).  One  of  the  other  challenges  presented  in  assessing the  existing  longitudinal  research  and  how  belonging  changes  over  time  is  the  variety  of  scales  or measures used to quantitatively assess students' SoB (Lingat et al., 2022). Despite critiques of assessing complex concepts of belonging with single question items (Lingat et al., 2022), there is a pressure on longitudinal studies to use abridged versions of belonging scales (Hausmann et al., 2007) or single-item measurements of belonging (Gopalan et al., 2019) to reduce survey fatigue from participants.

Given the importance of belonging to wider student success outcomes, a key consideration for uni­ versities is exploring the role they can take in enhancing students' SoB over time. Belonging is dynamic in its nature and so universities need to understand how it changes for students, so that they can effec­ tively develop and evaluate interventions to enhance it. Existing research has established how different factors affecting belonging may play a more significant role at different points in the student journey. Whilst in the long-view belonging is relatively stable, fluctuations are more apparent when examining this concept in closer timeframes. During students' first year of study, SoB may decrease - however, this may be due to unreliable measurements of belonging so close to the start of studies.

## Methodology

### Participants and data collection

All first-year undergraduate students (n = 5619) at two English universities were invited to take part in  a  wider  longitudinal  study  around  student  success  -  which  included  the  completion  of  online surveys  and  online  diaries  about  their  experiences  throughout  the  first  year  -  with  101  students eventually taking part (Figure 2), resulting in a sample size of 1.8%. Students were invited as part of email  communications  to  new  students,  informing  them  about  supportive  activities  over  the Welcome period.

Figure 2. Methods of data collection used within the research project, with numbers of participants at each stage.

To expand the external validity of the study, the two universities were chosen for their differences in student populations. One had less selective recruitment approaches, resulting in a majority of stu­ dents coming from widening participation demographics (39% mature, 25% Black, 41% free school meals  eligible,  24%  from  quintile  1  of  the  index  of  multiple  deprivation  -  a  UK-based  ranking  of neighbourhoods considering factors such as crime, health outcomes, and education access) - i.e. his­ torically underrepresented student groups - whilst the other was a highly selective institution (7% mature, 6% Black, 9% free school meals eligible, 7% from index of multiple deprivation quintile 1). Within  the  demographics  of  participating  students  themselves:  62%  of  these  students  were female,  48%  had  no  parents  attend  university  and  23%  had  only  one  parent  attend  university, 41% were international students, 46% had a commute greater than 30 min, and 65% had no experi­ ence of private education prior to university. Full demographic details of each participant are listed in Appendix 3.

Participant  consent  was  gathered  when  students  signed  up  to  take  part  in  the  overarching research  study,  in  line  with  the  ethical  approval  provided  by  the  University  of  York's  Education Ethics  Committee.  All  students  were  provided  with  full  details  of  the  study,  including  what  data they  would  be  asked  to  provide  and  how  this  would  be  processed.  Belonging  surveys  were issued to students four times in the academic year (October, December, February and May). Partici­ pants were incentivised for their participation in each survey with a £5 voucher.

All 101 participants were also invited to an online diaries aspect of the wider study. 65 of these 101 students took part, contributing a total of 304 online diaries and open text survey responses between  them.  Participants  were  given  the  choice  of  how  they  wish  to  provide  their  reflections for  each  of  the  online  diary  entries  -  which  could  take  the  form  of  either  blogs,  video  diaries  or audio recordings - and were prompted to share their thoughts on their SoB roughly every three weeks  (Appendix  1).  Online  diaries  were  chosen  as  the  method  of  data  collection  as  they  allow for  longitudinal  collection  of  data  near  to  when  events  happen,  thus  reducing  the  potential  for errors in recall (Cucu-Oancea, 2013). In addition, the method allows for data collection without inperson  interaction between  the  researcher  and  the  participants,  thus  reducing  the  risk of establishment of bonds that could influence SoB, as well as providing a distance that may allow par­ ticipants to disclose information that they perceive as too personal to share in interviews (Day and Thatcher,  2009).  Similar  to  the  surveys,  for  each  completed  diary  entry  participants  received  a  £5 voucher.

With 101 students participating in the study, this represents a sample size of just 1.8% of the total eligible population of new students across the two participating institutions. The sample is further decreased when considering just the online diaries respondents. This low response limits the gen­ eralisability of this study, as it introduces a high risk of non-response bias. However, this is mitigated in this study through specific analyses exploring missing data, reporting of significance values and effect sizes within quantitative results, and through the connecting of quantitative results with the subsequent qualitative aspect of the study. Potential reasons for the low take-up of the study are discussed further within the limitations section.

### Measures

The Yorke belonging scale (2016) was utilised for both the baseline measurement of students' SoB and follow-up surveys. This was utilised due to it being created and tested within the context of UK higher education, covering a breadth of domains of belonging (Ahn and Davis, 2020) and due to its relatively short length, which is especially important to reduce fatigue in a longitudinal study. Cron­ bach's Alpha analysis was conducted to assess the scale's internal validity - the extent to which each student responded consistently to questions within the scale. This analysis showed that the Yorke belonging scale has a Cronbach's Alpha score of 0.842 - representing a very strong level of internal consistency.

### Data analysis - surveys

To prepare survey data for analysis, results for the six Yorke scale items were converted to numeric values (i.e. strongly agree = 5, strongly disagree = 1), averaged and then normalised to produce an overall belonging score between 0 and 100. A generalised linear mixed model was used to assess whether  baseline  or  subsequent  levels  of  belonging  were  predictors  of  students  missing  future surveys or diary entries. Descriptive data and visualisations are presented around students' changing level  of  belonging,  which  are  then  explored  further  through  the  use  of  linear  mixed  models  and linear  regression  models.  Linear  mixed  models  were  used  to  ensure  that  multiple  data  points from the same students could be accounted for. As part of follow-up analyses to explore different types  of  parents'  educational  status,  pairwise  comparisons  were  made  of  estimated  marginal means  from  linear  mixed  models.  This  provided  a  way  to  assess  whether  differences  between having  one  or  two  parents  previously  attend  universities  were  in  themselves  significant  without having to recode reference values within models. All analyses were conducted in R Studio.

### Data analysis - online diaries

Although quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, this study follows an expla­ natory  logic,  with  the  quantitative  findings  guiding  the  subsequent  qualitative  analysis.  This approach aligns with a sequential explanatory mixed methods design (Taherdoost, 2022), allowing for  contextualisation  of  statistical  trends  in  participants'  SoB  through  exploration  of  their  online diaries.  As  part  of  the  wider  study,  full  analysis  of  participants'  online  diaries  was  conducted through a reflexive  thematic  analysis  method  (Braun  and  Clarke,  2006),  which  utilised  a  primarily deductive  approach  in  theme  identification  around  the  connections  between  student  belonging and agency. NVivo software was utilised for the coding and identification of themes.

To ensure rigor and trustworthiness in the qualitative findings, several steps were taken to align with the principles of credibility, dependability, and confirmability (Nowell et al., 2017). In the initial


<!-- p:10 -->


1071

coding stage (full coding list available in Appendix 2), participants were not classified based on their demographic data or institution of study. This partially 'blinded' the data from the researcher, so that no assumptions about the students' experiences based on demographic details - including first-gen­ eration status - could be made throughout the coding process, aiding authenticity and trustworthi­ ness  (Spiegelhalter,  2019).  As  suggested  by  Braun  and  Clarke  (2006),  several  stages  of  theme identification and reconsideration took place before themes were finalised, with researchers return­ ing  to  the  data  after  each  stage  to  check  for  credibility  -  i.e.  whether  themes  were  appropriately drawing from codes and whether codes were labelled accurately - before refining themes and gen­ erating thematic visuals. Coding comparison queries - a commonly applied tool to improve both credibility  and  dependability  -  were  used  to  ensure  that  each  theme  was  sufficiently  distinct,  as well  as  internally  consistent.  Sentiment  coding  analysis  was  conducted  manually  and  used  to support the development of theme generation.

The full findings of the analysis of participants' online diaries are not presented within this article. Instead NVivo's matrix coding queries were utilised to contextualise the findings of RQ2: that firstgeneration students  had  a  significant  decline  in  their  SoB  compared  to  their  non-first-generation counterparts.  40%  of  online  diary  contributions  came  from  first-generation  students,  so  codes were  interrogated  where  matrix  coding  queries  showed  first-generation  students  had  dispropor­ tional representation - in this case where they made either less than 30% or greater than 50% of the contributions (Appendix 2). A 10% deviation threshold was selected as a meaningful indicator of thematic divergence between the two groups. Codes that met this above criterion of dispropor­ tional representation then underwent further reflexive thematic analysis, replicating the same steps as above, but with codes already generated, to arrive at a series of themes that are discussed within the latter part of the results section. By presenting detailed participant quotes and explaining the specific  connections  between  SoB  decline  and  the  emerging  themes,  this  study  offers  generalis­ ations  that  explain  how  and  why  the  quantitative  trends  occurred,  making  the  mechanisms  that affected belonging visible.

## Results

### Preliminary analyses

The demographic profile of the participants was assessed against the overall student body at the two institutions to evaluate sample representativeness. Two-sample z-tests showed that there were no significant  differences  in  proportions  across  gender  or  age  between  the  study  sample  and  the overall institutional populations at either university. Whilst there were also no significant differences in proportions for international students or first-generation students at the selective-recruiting insti­ tution,  at  the  less-selective-recruiting  institution  international  students  (z = 6.4971, p = 0.011)  and first-generation students (z = 36.8, p &lt; 0.001) were significantly more likely to register for the study.

One challenge with longitudinal studies is the prevalence of missing data. Whilst students were encouraged to take part in all survey data points, the design of the overarching research recognised that other time commitments would make this unlikely. Missing data can be problematic in longi­ tudinal  studies  if  participants'  likelihood  to  miss  data  points  is  correlated  with  the  subject  being investigated. In this instance, if students were less likely to take part in future surveys if they had a  lower  SoB,  then  it  would  mean  that  the  data  points  that were submitted  are  artificially  higher than we should expect for the participants overall. To address this potential problem, this section explores whether students' SoB is a predictor of them missing future surveys submission points.

Given that whether a student missed their subsequent submission point is a binary outcome (Yes/ No), a generalised linear mixed model was used to explore this relationship. The model tested whether a student's prior SoB score predicted the probability of non-submission at the following time point. The analysis found that SoB was not a statistically significant predictor of subsequent data missingness (β = - 0.005, p = 0.682, n = 192). Usefully, the GLMM also revealed a substantial variability in the completion likelihood across students (Random Intercept Variance = 0.85, SD = 0.92), confirming that while individ­ ual  students  differ  in  their  overall  likelihood  to  complete  follow-up  surveys,  this  variation  is  not explained by their self-reported belonging levels - i.e. the outcome variable.

### RQ1 - how belonging changes over time

The results reported within this section focus on exploring the descriptive data from students' survey responses.  Each  participant's  belonging  journey  is  visualised,  also  allowing  for  discussion  of  the average - both mean and median - results across the entire study population. Means and standard deviations for each survey are presented in Table 1 and visualised in Figure 3. Figure 3 helps to visu­ alise  the  absolute  belonging  scores  that  students reported  within surveys  and how  these change across the academic year.

These data show, more prominently, that students' SoB decreases through the first year of study for these undergraduate participants. On average, students' SoB decreased about two points at each survey measurement point, resulting in an overall decrease in belonging from 78.1-72.9 - represent­ ing a five-point decrease.

Whilst  there  was  a  concern  that  there  might  be  a  divergence  between  the  mean  and  median values of students' SoB scores, the values are almost identical throughout the year. The only excep­ tion to this is a slight divergence for the May survey results, where the median value is 75, compared to a mean of 72.9. Overall, this suggests that most students participating in the study saw a decrease in their SoB from the start of the year.

Whilst these analyses describe the trend amongst students on average, the trajectory of each indi­ vidual student was considered. For students who took part in both the October and May surveys (i.e. the first and last measurement points), 31% had an increase in their SoB, 22% no change and 47% a decrease. This aligns with the previous analyses, showing that more students saw a decrease in their sense of belonging than an increase across the first academic year.

Linear mixed modelling was used to explore whether survey timepoints are predictors of changes in belonging (Table 2), confirming an overall decreasing trend in mean SoB across the academic year. Specifically, the final survey point in May showed a statistically significant decline compared to the October baseline ( β = - 5.195, p = 0.007, n = 101). The intermediate time points (December and Feb­ ruary) did not show a statistically significant decline from baseline (Table 2).

In  addition to the changing nature of belonging, these results also highlight differences in the variability  of  the  data  across  timepoints.  Standard  deviations  for  each  survey  increase  across  the year; from 12.8 for the overall October survey data to 17.1 for the May survey. As is visible within Figure  3,  almost  no  students  report  a  SoB  below  50  within  the  October  survey  measurement, however more students do report such variability in their belonging in later surveys. Descriptively, this indicates that student experiences become less uniform over time. Although a regression analy­ sis of these standard deviations (Table 3) did not reach statistical significance ( p &gt; 0.05), this is likely due to the limited number of timepoints (n = 4).

#### Fragility of social connections

Within the online diaries, some first-generation students noted that they had begun the academic year with optimism and a sense of connection, but that this had declined over time. Social isolation was commented upon by first-generation students not just when  other  students  were  attending  less,  but  due  to  natural  changes  in  teaching  schedules during periods of increased deadlines: 'During this period of less teaching, more independent learn­ ing and deadlines, I have found it harder to maintain a SoB.' In  contrast,  first-generation  students  were  more  likely  than  their  non-first-generation  counter­ parts to talk about friendships outside of their course - in accommodation, clubs, part-time work, or social events:

There were no explicit references from participants as to being first-in-family to attend university. However, many points were made by mature and international first-generation students about chal­ lenges fitting in  with  the  culture.  Students  talked  about  the  barrier  of  not  understanding  cultural references, which slowed their ability to connect with peers:

'I am unsure of what topics are appropriate to discuss, and I am mindful of cultural differences that may cause offense.' (P38)

Even  when  other  students  were  welcoming,  not  being  able  to  instantly  'click'  (P3)  hindered belonging.

#### Supportive staff as anchors

An overall theme from all participants was the importance of mattering to staff, especially academic staff,  as  a  route  towards  belonging.  Whilst  many  first-generation  students  acknowledged  positive relationships with staff, they were less likely to do so than non-first-generation students. First-gen­ eration students appreciated knowing about available support:

'Everyone also had an academic adviser, which I personally never had to use. But ... I knew that if I ever needed anything, I would be able to ask' (P14).

Whilst  there  were  many  comments  from  students  about  approachability  and  responsiveness, first-generation  students  were  more  likely  to  have  negative  comments  about  this,  noting  how 'incredibly frustrating' (P43) it was to get no or slow responses to queries.

Many students felt that staff wanted them to succeed, which was an important part in feeling that they belonged: 'they do actually care about you and your grades' (P14). Students emphasised the importance of building up relationships with staff who knew them personally and thus understood what their goals were. When student voice was utilised by staff to make change, students empha­ sised how this made them feel 'respected' (P43, P44).

#### Silence around finances

Whilst finances were talked about frequently by other students, there was a notable absence of any remarks  related  to  finances  from  first-generation  students.  Whereas  other  students  described actively  managing  their  finances,  planning  holidays,  and  budgeting  -  suggesting  a  level  of financial literacy, control and optimism - there was no mention of these topics from first-generation students. ## Discussion

### What causes students' sense of belonging to decrease?

The  results  of  this  study  provide  three  main  areas  of  contribution  to  knowledge  on  the  topic  of student  belonging:  how  students'  SoB  changes  over  the  first  year  of  undergraduate  study,  how changes  in  students'  SoB  may  be  predicted  by  their  parents'  educational  status,  and  what  may explain these negative changes in belonging for first-generation students.

Results show that students' SoB slightly decreases through the first academic year of study, align­ ing closely with previous studies (Hausmann et al., 2007; O'Sullivan et al., 2019). While some of this may be attributed  to  the  reliability  of  early  measurements  of  students'  SoB  (Ruedas-Gracia  et  al., 2023)  with  students  potentially  putting  on  a  'brave  face'  at  the  start  of  their  university  journey, there are also potential explanations offered through the expansive literature on student transitions. The 'honeymoon period' of high-energy welcome activities and supportive scaffolding of university induction provides a temporary boost to students' SoB that often masks the challenges of transition (Fisher and Hood, 1987). As the thrill of the new setting wears off, students then have to accept the reality of academic rigor and the distance from existing communities and systems of support prior to starting  university  (Wilcox  et  al.,  2005;  Christie  et  al.,  2008).  The  former,  experiential  justification, based on the extent of transition activity, would suggest that institutions should consider how activi­ ties to support students' SoB can be continued longer into the first year of study. The latter, psycho­ logical  reason,  suggests  that  early  measurements  of  students'  SoB  may  be  artificially  high  and therefore should be used cautiously or avoided.

While  early  belonging  interventions  often  rely  on  the  common  assumption  that  belonging  is  a process  that  naturally  increases  over  time  as  students  adjust  (Walton  &amp;  Brady,  2017),  this  study's findings suggest that for many this process is not linear. Instead of a cumulative growth, there was an erosion of students' SoB, after an initial boost of welcome activities, suggesting a lingering precarity that must be actively maintained (Crawford et al., 2024). The significant drop by the end of the first year suggests that induction should not be seen as an event, but as a longitudinal process of orientation (Fernandez  et  al.,  2023).  Furthermore,  although  the  overall  trend  in  belonging  was  negative,  the observed decline in SoB only reached statistical significance when comparing the final May timepoint to the October baseline. This pattern suggests that students' sense of belonging may be more resilient or stable during the initial phase of the academic year, a period often characterised by high engage­ ment and institution-led support. Conversely, the significant drop in belonging by May could reflect a cumulative effect of academic fatigue, assessment-related stress, and loss of peer and staff relation­ ships  as  formal  teaching  periods  conclude.  This  finding  highlights  that  the  erosion  of  belonging  is not a smooth, linear process but rather a stepped or accelerated phenomenon that becomes critical towards the end of the academic year, underscoring the need for targeted, later-cycle interventions.

### Belonging for first-generation students

Results  also  further  complicate  literature  on  first-generation  students  and  SoB.  Whilst  previous studies generally find that first-generation students have lower levels of belonging (O'Shea, 2020), this can depend on whether first-generation status is defined as having neither parents or caregivers as previous attendees of university or not both parents or caregivers (Pedler et al., 2022). Within this


<!-- p:17 -->


1078

study,  initial  measurements  of  belonging  at  the  start  of  the  academic  year  were  almost  identical regardless of parents' education status, contrasting with prior studies that suggest student demo­ graphics are the primary predictor for initial belonging (Strayhorn, 2019). However, by the end of the first academic year, students who had no parents or caregivers attend university had significantly lower SoB compared to non-first-generation students.

The subsequent qualitative analysis sheds light on what may have been happening during the academic year to negatively impact on the SoB of the first-generation students within this study. Whilst  many  previous  studies  have  commented  on  the  importance  of  building  connection  with peers on the course (Thomas, 2012; Kahu et al., 2022), first-generation students seemed to report that  these  relationships  were  fragile  and  so  prone  to  disruption  through  changes  across  the  aca­ demic year. This fragility could be explained by significant external commitments that first-gener­ ation  students  often  have  to  balance  alongside  their  studies,  such  as  family  care  and  part-time work  (Ardoin,  2018).  These  competing  identities  mean  that  social  connection  on-course  is  often deprioritised not by choice, but by necessity (Bettencourt, 2021).

When initial attempts at friendship are met with cultural barriers (O'Shea, 2016; Reay, 2018), firstgeneration students may retreat to pre-existing social  circles outside  the  university  to  maintain  a sense of psychological safety. This explains why first-generation students in this study sought con­ nection  elsewhere  -  either  outside  of  the  course  or  beyond  the  university  entirely;  a  rational response to an environment that feels 'foreign or unknown' (Dias-Broens et al., 2024).

Comments from all students aligned with previous research showing the importance of building relationships with staff towards students' SoB (Hausmann et al., 2007). However, first-generation stu­ dents were much less likely to talk about feeling that they mattered to staff, compared to their nonfirst-generation counterparts. Whilst there were still many positive stories from students about mat­ tering  to  staff,  first-generation  students'  online  diaries  also  highlighted  frustration  with  slow responses to queries. This aligns with previous research suggesting how first-generation students may  lack  the  cultural  capital  to  navigate  unclear  expectations,  as  they  have  fewer  people  who they can turn towards to check what they can and cannot expect from academic staff (Collier and Morgan, 2007). Any negative impact on SoB from unclear expectations would be likely to impact stu­ dents more as the academic year progresses (Means et al., 2017), explaining the steeper decline in belonging for first-generation students seen within the quantitative findings.

Finally, there was a notable absence of any comments from first-generation students within their online diaries about finances. Given that first-generation students are likely to face the same financial challenges as other students - or perhaps even greater challenges, with first-generation students being more likely to come from lower socio-economic backgrounds (Adamecz-Volgyi et al., 2021) -  these  findings  suggest  that  there  may  be  an  unwillingness  or  discomfort  around  discussing finances unless directly prompted. This aligns with prior research that explores how first-generation and working-class students often perform 'class straddling', attempting to hide financial stress to fit into the perceived middle-class norm of the carefree student (Bettencourt, 2021). Not only do these social concealment strategies place an additional emotional burden on first-generation students, but they also may impact help-seeking behaviours, leading to further financial challenges (Harrel-Hall­ mark et al., 2022; Veldman et al., 2023).

### Limitations and future research

The primary limitation for this study has been its sample size of participants. This was likely due to the positioning of the study as inviting students to both surveys and online diaries, which may have been felt by some students as 'too much'. The low response could mean that those partici­ pating in the study are not representative of their overall student populations or have a particular interest  in  the  topic  of  student  belonging,  which  may  have  influenced  their  involvement  and results. To address this limitation, two-sample z-tests were used to understand significant differ­ ences between the overall student populations and study populations across key demographic


<!-- p:18 -->


1079

variables. The likelihood that students would have entered the study due to a specific interest in student belonging was also mitigated by how the study was positioned when promoted to poten­ tial  participants,  as  this  focused  more  generally  on  student  success  as  a  topic.  Furthermore,  all quantitative  analyses  report  both  statistical  significance  and  effect  sizes  to  ensure  that  the influence  of  a  smaller  sample  can  be  clearly  understood  by  readers.  Finally,  the  quantitative results  from  this  study  are  complemented  by  the  rich  qualitative  data  presented  by  students through the online diaries.

Whilst 101 participants took part in the study, not all participants completed each follow-up survey or online diary opportunity. This is a challenge with any longitudinal research study and has been addressed in this article through analysis to test whether students' SoB was a predictor of  completing  or  missing  future  data  collection  points.  The  limitations  to  sample  size  have meant that this article  was  not  able  to  look  at  intersectionality  between  student  demographics in  a  meaningful  way,  beyond  those  inherent  in  the  linear  mixed  model  analyses.  Furthermore, no  analyses  were  conducted  comparing  results  between  the  two  institutions,  due  to  lower participation  from  the  selective-recruiting  university.  This  limits  the  external  validity  of  the study, as it means that analyses were not able to show whether key findings - such as the signifi­ cant decrease in students' SoB from October to May was significant at both types of institutions independently.  In  addition,  as  not  all  follow-up  surveys  were  completed  by  every  student,  this led to varying response rates in each case. The number of observations included within each indi­ vidual analysis have been included either in the caption or within tables themselves to address this.

Further research could help to address these gaps by:

- 1) Attempting to gather large numbers of participants at a variety of institution types, to see if these trends in belonging changes are replicated across contexts
- 2) Reconsider  study  design  by  focusing  initial  recruitment  just  for  the  surveys  -  where  large response  rates  are  most  important  -  and  then  offering  separate  recruitment  routes  for  those who wish to additionally take part in online diaries
- 3) Exploring whether these changes in belonging continue beyond the first academic year of study.

## Conclusion

This article has explored how students' SoB changes over the first year of study and how this is predicted  by  students'  first-generation  status.  Building  on  previous  research,  results  show  that students'  SoB  significantly  decreases  throughout  the  first  year  of  study.  Whilst  beginning  the year with similar levels of belonging, first-generation students saw decreasing levels of belonging compared to their non-first-generation counterparts. Qualitative analysis of the rich online diaries kept by students during the year, suggests that the main differences in experiences of belonging for first-generation students were more fragile social connections with coursemates, cultural bar­ riers, less frequent feelings of mattering to staff, and a reluctance to discuss the impact of finances on  their  SoB.  Results  suggest  that  universities  should  consider  developing  interventions  to support students' SoB later into the academic year to address expected decreases in belonging. Furthermore, universities may be able to address declining SoB amongst first-generation students by considering how changes to teaching schedules and wider attendance patterns may impact social  connections,  how  unclear  expectations  of  staff  responsiveness  may  impact  relationships with academic staff, and how talking about and seeking help around finances may need to be normalised.
