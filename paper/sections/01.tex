\abstract{
Mathematics support is often evaluated by comparing students who attend with those who do not, although this comparison combines the decision to engage with the frequency of subsequent use and may also obscure differences in how adverse course outcomes are recorded. We analysed 6,627 first eligible attempts at a first-year university mathematics course, matching mathematics-support visits to the same academic period and standardising final grades within instructor--period groups. Of the students, 1,234 attended the mathematics support centre at least once. Any recorded attendance was associated with a $0.361$ standard-deviation higher final performance and a 15.3 percentage-point higher probability of passing after adjustment for instructor--period fixed effects and degree programme. Among users, however, outcomes did not differ jointly across the pre-specified groups 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6+ visits for either standardised performance ($p=0.317$) or pass probability ($p=0.152$), and no pairwise contrast survived Holm adjustment. The composition of adverse outcomes differed more sharply: among students who did not pass, attendance was associated with a 13.2 percentage-point higher probability of an administrative rather than numeric non-passing outcome. This pattern was concentrated in voluntary withdrawal (BV), for which the adjusted difference relative to numeric failure was 16.8 percentage points, whereas the corresponding contrast for temporary withdrawal was near zero. The findings suggest that attendance records may capture both a performance margin and differences in the management of adverse academic trajectories. These associations are observational and do not establish causal effects of mathematics support or directly measure student engagement.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics and statistics support has become an established component of higher education provision, particularly where students enter quantitatively demanding programmes with heterogeneous prior preparation \citep{matthews2013,lawson2020,mullen2024}. Drop-in centres and related forms of additional support are intended to complement formal teaching rather than replace it, yet their evaluation is methodologically difficult because routine usage records show who attended and how often without revealing why students attended, what happened during a visit, or whether observed users and non-users were comparable before support was used \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}. The literature therefore contains a tension that is central to institutional evaluation: attendance is one of the most readily observable indicators of engagement with support, but it is also a behavioural measure produced by student choice, institutional encouragement and academic need.

A substantial part of the empirical literature has examined whether students who use mathematics support obtain more favourable academic outcomes than students who do not. Studies of first-year mathematics and calculus courses have reported positive associations between support-centre attendance and course grades, including analyses that account for measures of prior attainment \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}. In a longitudinal TEAMAT study, \citet{jacob2019} distinguished students who attended once from those who never attended and then grouped progressively higher visit frequencies, thereby recognising that initial engagement and repeated use need not represent the same behavioural process. The distinction is consequential because a binary user/non-user comparison can identify a broad separation associated with entering the support system while saying little about whether a second, third or later visit corresponds to a further difference in academic outcomes.

Recent work has made this conceptual separation particularly explicit. Using mathematics and statistics support data from Coventry University, \citet{gokhool2026} examine two dimensions of engagement: whether a student engaged at least once and, conditional on engagement, the number of visits made. Their two-stage hurdle-model formulation uses a binomial model for initial engagement and a negative-binomial model for visit frequency among users. We use this study only to motivate the distinction between initial and repeated engagement; the present manuscript was prepared from the published abstract of that article rather than its full text, so we do not attribute to it results or conclusions that are not stated in the abstract.

This distinction also matters because non-engagement is not equivalent to indifference or low motivation. Survey evidence shows that students may not use mathematics learning support for multiple reasons, including perceptions that support is unnecessary, limited awareness, practical barriers and affective concerns \citep{macanbhaird2013}. Likewise, attendance frequency should not be treated as a direct measure of help-seeking disposition. The broader literature on academic help-seeking treats help-seeking as a motivated and self-regulated process, whereas observed tutoring or support-centre attendance is a behavioural indicator that may arise from several different underlying mechanisms \citep{fong2023}. Administrative attendance records should therefore be interpreted as records of service use rather than measurements of latent engagement, motivation or need.

The causal interpretation of attendance differences is further constrained by selection. Randomised encouragement and other identification-oriented designs have produced more mixed performance evidence than conventional user/non-user comparisons. \citet{paloyo2016} used a randomised encouragement design for supplemental instruction and obtained a positive but imprecise instrumental-variable estimate of attendance on grades, while \citet{pugatch2018} increased peer-tutoring take-up through randomised messages but found little evidence that the induced tutoring attendance changed academic performance. Using propensity-score matching and difference-in-differences, \citet{buchele2024} reported no direct effect of a course-specific mathematical learning and support centre on mathematics performance, although several affective outcomes were more favourable. These studies do not invalidate observational evaluation, but they make it important to separate description of attendance-associated outcomes from claims about the effect of an additional visit.

A second issue has received much less attention in routine mathematics-support evaluation: a binary pass/non-pass outcome can combine substantively different academic trajectories. A student who does not pass may remain enrolled and receive a numeric grade below the pass mark, or may leave the course with an administrative outcome. If these outcome types are distributed differently among users and non-users, then part of the apparent attendance--performance relationship may concern not only final numeric performance but also how an adverse academic trajectory ends. This distinction is especially relevant when non-numeric outcomes are incorporated into continuous-performance analyses through imputation, because changes in their prevalence can alter the lower tail of the completed grade distribution.

The present study therefore separates two related margins. The \emph{performance margin} concerns final standardised course performance and the probability of crossing the institutional pass mark. The \emph{academic-management margin} concerns the composition of adverse final outcomes among students who do not pass, distinguishing numeric failure from voluntary withdrawal (BV), temporary withdrawal (RT) and academic withdrawal (BA). We do not assume that these administrative codes have a common behavioural interpretation, nor do we treat them as direct measures of engagement. Instead, we ask whether their observed composition differs with mathematics-support attendance and then consider broader academic engagement or institutional navigation only as a possible mechanism in the Discussion.

The analysis uses administrative records from the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla. Visits are aligned to the same academic period as the student's first eligible attempt at Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU), and final performance is standardised within instructor--period groups to account for differences in local grading environments. Before examining Paper 2.1 outcomes, we selected the positive-attendance grouping 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits using an outcome-blind support audit based on student counts and instructor--period overlap. A more granular 1--6 and 7+ specification is retained as a sensitivity analysis.

We address four questions. First, is the broad difference between no recorded attendance and any recorded attendance present for both standardised final performance and pass probability? Second, among students who attend at least once, do these outcomes differ across attendance frequencies? Third, does the composition of non-passing outcomes differ between students with zero attendance and those with any attendance, and is any such difference concentrated in a particular administrative code? Finally, do the continuous imputed and numeric complete-case distributions suggest that attendance-frequency differences are broadly distributed across numeric performance or are instead related principally to the lower tail and the composition of administrative outcomes? Throughout, the analysis is observational and does not estimate a causal effect of CMAT attendance.
