\abstract{
Attendance at university mathematics support centres is commonly evaluated by contrasting users with non-users, although the distribution of visits may contain additional information about repeated help-seeking and institutional participation requirements. We examine 6,627 first eligible university mathematics-course attempts across 190 instructor--period grading contexts, linking CMAT support-centre visits to the corresponding academic period. The outcomes are instructor--period-standardised final performance and passing at the institutional threshold of 7.5. Fixed-effect models adjust for instructor--period and degree programme, with clustered uncertainty, omnibus tests and Holm-adjusted Wald contrasts across visit frequencies. The outcome-blind frequency-support audit favoured grouping visits as 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ among users; we also report an exploratory finer grid separating exact six visits from 7+. Any recorded attendance was associated with 0.361 standard deviations higher adjusted final performance (95\% CI 0.302--0.421) and a 15.3 percentage-point higher adjusted passing probability (95\% CI 12.4--18.1). In the support-based user-only grouping, the adjusted omnibus tests did not reject equal performance across visit frequencies (p=0.317 for standardised grades and p=0.152 for PASS), and no within-user pair survived Holm adjustment. In the exploratory eight-group model including non-users, the 7+ group had a 14.5 percentage-point higher adjusted passing probability than the one-visit group (Holm-adjusted p=0.0206), but this result was not reproduced as a Holm-significant user-only contrast. These findings distinguish a pronounced first-attendance association from more uncertain variation across repeated use, without identifying causal effects of additional visits.
}

\section{Introduction}

University mathematics and statistics support centres provide supplementary learning opportunities for students entering programmes with different levels of prior mathematical preparation. Reviews have documented the development of such services and the methodological difficulty of evaluating them with attendance records alone \citep{matthews2013,lawson2020,mullen2024}. Participation is a decision made by students within an academic and institutional context rather than an assigned intervention. A centre can be used to resolve an immediate question, repeatedly revisit difficult material, or satisfy an institutional participation activity; the same number of recorded visits need not represent a comparable amount or type of instruction.

Research on mathematics support often reports positive associations between service attendance and academic outcomes, including after adjustment for some measures of prior preparation \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}. Nevertheless, studies with experimental encouragement and more explicit identification strategies show why associations based on actual service use should not automatically be interpreted as treatment effects \citep{paloyo2016,pugatch2018,buchele2024}. Students who attend may differ from non-attendees in academic need, motivation, expectations, available time or access to instructors, all of which can also influence final grades.

A second question follows after distinguishing users from non-users. Studies that code attendance as a binary variable implicitly combine a student with one visit and a student who uses the centre many times. Conversely, comparisons between every possible number of visits can lack precision in the sparsely populated upper tail and generate numerous tests whose unadjusted significance is misleading. These problems are particularly relevant when attendance is recorded through an institutional participation programme: early visits may reflect the incentive as well as the demand for mathematical help.

This paper examines the association between recorded CMAT visit frequency and final outcomes in the first eligible attempt at a university mathematics course at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla, Mexico. It separates the benchmark comparison of no recorded attendance versus any attendance from the central analysis among students who used the centre. We examine two outcomes: a within-instructor--period-standardised final-grade measure and the probability of passing. The primary inference uses instructor--period and degree-programme adjustment, cluster-robust omnibus tests and pairwise Wald contrasts whose multiplicity is controlled by the Holm procedure. The positive-frequency grouping originally selected from an outcome-blind support audit is reported separately from a more finely resolved exploratory attendance grid.

Our questions are whether adjusted academic outcomes differ between students with no and some recorded attendance, whether positive attendance frequencies can be statistically distinguished after accounting for multiplicity, and whether the observed frequency profile is compatible with an abrupt change around the institutionally salient third visit. We also assess how upper-tail grouping and clustering at the instructor rather than instructor--period level affect the conclusions. This study describes observational differences in final outcomes; it cannot establish the effect of assigning a student additional mathematics-support visits.
