\abstract{
Mathematics support is often evaluated by comparing students who attend with those who do not, although this comparison combines initial engagement with subsequent frequency of use and can obscure differences in how adverse course outcomes are recorded. We analysed 6,627 first eligible attempts at a first-year university mathematics course, matching support-centre visits to the same academic period and standardising final performance within instructor--period groups. Of these students, 1,234 attended the mathematics support centre at least once. Because an institutional first-semester participation requirement could be satisfied through three CMAT visits during at least part of the study period, visit frequency was treated as an institutionally influenced exposure rather than as a pure dose of voluntary help-seeking. The main descriptive and pairwise displays use eight attendance groups---0, exact 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 visits, and 7+ visits---so that the zero-attendance benchmark and the upper tail are visible in a common frame. After adjustment for instructor--period fixed effects and degree programme, any recorded attendance was associated with 0.361 standard deviations higher final performance and a 15.3 percentage-point higher probability of passing. In the eight-group continuous-outcome model, the omnibus difference was strong and every zero-versus-positive contrast remained significant after Holm correction, whereas no positive-versus-positive contrast did. The corresponding pass-odds point estimates showed the same broad separation, while multiplicity-aware PASS inference was based on the stable fixed-effect linear-probability specification. The adjacent two-versus-three comparison did not show an abrupt change at the institutionally incentivised third visit. The zero-versus-positive separation was also present when administrative outcomes were excluded and failure was defined only from observed numeric grades, indicating that it was not created solely by numerical representation of non-numeric outcomes. Distribution-shape analyses showed that, among numeric complete cases, a two-Gaussian representation was preferable to a single strongly skewed distribution for zero, one and two visits but not consistently at higher frequencies; after administrative outcomes were numerically represented, the mixture was preferred more broadly and its lower component was strongly enriched in administrative outcomes. The results therefore distinguish a pronounced observational separation associated with entering mathematics support from weaker and less regular differences across repeated attendance frequencies, while showing that institutional participation incentives and administrative-outcome composition both matter for interpreting routine support-centre data.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics and statistics support has become an established component of higher education provision, particularly where students enter quantitatively demanding programmes with heterogeneous prior preparation \citep{matthews2013,lawson2020,mullen2024}. Drop-in centres and related forms of additional support are intended to complement formal teaching rather than replace it, yet their evaluation is methodologically difficult because routine usage records show who attended and how often without revealing why students attended, what happened during a visit, or whether observed users and non-users were comparable before support was used \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}. The literature therefore contains a tension that is central to institutional evaluation: attendance is one of the most readily observable indicators of engagement with support, but it is also a behavioural measure produced by student choice, institutional encouragement and academic need.

A substantial part of the empirical literature has examined whether students who use mathematics support obtain more favourable academic outcomes than students who do not. Studies of first-year mathematics and calculus courses have reported positive associations between support-centre attendance and course grades, including analyses that account for measures of prior attainment \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}. In a longitudinal TEAMAT study, \citet{jacob2019} distinguished students who attended once from those who never attended and then grouped progressively higher visit frequencies, thereby recognising that initial engagement and repeated use need not represent the same behavioural process. The distinction is consequential because a binary user/non-user comparison can identify a broad separation associated with entering the support system while saying little about whether a second, third or later visit corresponds to a further difference in academic outcomes.

Recent work has made this conceptual separation particularly explicit. Using mathematics and statistics support data from Coventry University, \citet{gokhool2026} examine two dimensions of engagement: whether a student engaged at least once and, conditional on engagement, the number of visits made. Their two-stage hurdle-model formulation uses a binomial model for initial engagement and a negative-binomial model for visit frequency among users. This formulation is useful here because it treats initial engagement and repeated use as related but distinct features of support-centre participation.

This distinction also matters because non-engagement is not equivalent to indifference or low motivation. Survey evidence shows that students may not use mathematics learning support for multiple reasons, including perceptions that support is unnecessary, limited awareness, practical barriers and affective concerns \citep{macanbhaird2013}. Likewise, attendance frequency should not be treated as a direct measure of help-seeking disposition. The broader literature on academic help-seeking treats help-seeking as a motivated and self-regulated process, whereas observed tutoring or support-centre attendance is a behavioural indicator that may arise from several different underlying mechanisms \citep{fong2023}. Administrative attendance records should therefore be interpreted as records of service use rather than measurements of latent engagement, motivation or need.

The causal interpretation of attendance differences is further constrained by selection. Randomised encouragement and other identification-oriented designs have produced more mixed performance evidence than conventional user/non-user comparisons. \citet{paloyo2016} used a randomised encouragement design for supplemental instruction and obtained a positive but imprecise instrumental-variable estimate of attendance on grades, while \citet{pugatch2018} increased peer-tutoring take-up through randomised messages but found little evidence that the induced tutoring attendance changed academic performance. Using propensity-score matching and difference-in-differences, \citet{buchele2024} reported no direct effect of a course-specific mathematical learning and support centre on mathematics performance, although several affective outcomes were more favourable. These studies do not invalidate observational evaluation, but they make it important to separate description of attendance-associated outcomes from claims about the effect of an additional visit.

Institutional stakeholders also have an a priori reason to expect a mathematics support centre to be beneficial because the service exists precisely to improve students' opportunities to succeed. That programme expectation is substantively legitimate, but it can become a source of confirmation bias if exposure groups, outcomes or significance thresholds are chosen after results are inspected. We therefore treat the institutional expectation as context rather than evidence: the primary attendance grouping was fixed from exposure support before outcome comparisons were examined, multiplicity-adjusted inference is reported alongside unadjusted diagnostics, and conclusions are triangulated across outcomes that depend differently on the treatment of administrative records.

A second issue has received much less attention in routine mathematics-support evaluation: a binary pass/non-pass outcome can combine substantively different academic trajectories. A student who does not pass may remain enrolled and receive a numeric grade below the pass mark, or may leave the course with an administrative outcome. If these outcome types are distributed differently among users and non-users, then part of the apparent attendance--performance relationship may concern not only final numeric performance but also how an adverse academic trajectory ends. This distinction is especially relevant when non-numeric outcomes are incorporated into continuous-performance analyses through imputation, because changes in their prevalence can alter the lower tail of the completed grade distribution.

The present study therefore separates two related margins. The \emph{performance margin} concerns final standardised course performance and the probability of crossing the institutional pass mark. The \emph{academic-management margin} concerns the composition of adverse final outcomes among students who do not pass, distinguishing numeric failure from voluntary withdrawal (BV), temporary withdrawal (RT) and academic withdrawal (BA). We do not assume that these administrative codes have a common behavioural interpretation, nor do we treat them as direct measures of engagement. Instead, we ask whether their observed composition differs with mathematics-support attendance and then consider broader academic engagement or institutional navigation only as a possible mechanism in the Discussion.

The analysis uses administrative records from the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla. Visits are aligned to the same academic period as the student's first eligible attempt at Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU), and final performance is standardised within instructor--period groups to account for differences in local grading environments. An outcome-blind support audit showed that exact six visits had weaker instructor--period overlap than a pooled 6+ category, so the earliest inferential specification used 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits. The current manuscript nevertheless reports the full zero-inclusive sequence 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ visits as its main descriptive and pairwise display because this grouping makes both the zero-attendance benchmark and the upper tail explicit. The pooled 6+ analysis is retained as a robustness check on tail sparsity rather than treated as a competing substantive result.

Because the continuous outcome requires numerical representation of BV, RT and BA, the empirical strategy deliberately triangulates evidence across outcomes with different dependence on that representation. PASS uses all students but requires no latent numeric value for administrative outcomes; the numeric-failure analysis removes administrative outcomes entirely; the complete-case distribution analysis examines only observed numeric grades; and the imputed distribution is then used to assess how administrative outcomes alter the lower tail.

We address four related questions. First, how do standardised final performance and passing vary across the full sequence 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ visits, and how much of that pattern is driven by the distinction between zero and positive attendance? Second, do the positive-frequency contrasts show any regular progression or an abrupt change around the institutionally incentivised third visit? Third, does the composition of non-passing outcomes differ between students with zero attendance and those with any attendance, and is any such difference concentrated in a particular administrative code? Finally, what do the numeric complete-case and imputed distributions reveal about the shape of academic outcomes and the contribution of administrative outcomes to the lower tail? Throughout, the analysis is observational and does not estimate a causal effect of CMAT attendance.
