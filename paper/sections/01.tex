\abstract{
Evaluating mathematics support using administrative records is difficult because attendance is voluntary, attendance records may span several academic periods, and raw grades may not be directly comparable across classes. We examine the relationship between attendance at the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) and performance in a first-year university mathematics course among 6,627 students. Visits are counted only when they occur in the same academic period as a student's first eligible course attempt, and final grades are standardised within instructor--period classes. Students are grouped by 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more recorded visits. Overall, 18.6\% of students attended CMAT at least once during the relevant academic period. Mean classroom-relative performance was $-0.061$ standard deviations among students with no recorded visits and $0.219$, $0.247$, $0.278$, and $0.355$ among students with 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits, respectively. Games--Howell post hoc comparisons showed that each attendance group differed significantly from students with no recorded visits, whereas none of the six comparisons among students who attended different numbers of times was significant at the 5\% level. Models with class fixed effects and adjustment for degree programme gave the same overall pattern: estimated differences relative to students with no visits were $0.305$, $0.359$, $0.373$, and $0.461$ standard deviations for 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits. The findings therefore show a clear association between attendance at mathematics support and higher classroom-relative performance, but weaker evidence that more frequent attendance is associated with progressively higher performance. Because attendance is voluntary and prior mathematical attainment is incompletely observed, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics learning support is now an established feature of higher education in many countries, particularly as institutions respond to differences in students' prior mathematical preparation and to the quantitative demands of a widening range of degree programmes \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}. A substantial literature has examined the impact and evaluation of mathematics support, but evaluation remains difficult because participation is usually voluntary. Students who attend may differ systematically from those who do not in prior attainment, motivation, perceived difficulty, study effort, time availability, and willingness to seek academic support. Self-selection is therefore a central limitation of observational evaluations of mathematics support \citep{mullen2024}.

Studies of mathematics support centres have commonly compared the grades or pass rates of students who use the service with those of students who do not \citep{macanbhaird2009,jacob2019}. \citet{jacob2019}, in particular, found that students who attended once should be considered separately from students who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. Simply showing that students who attend mathematics support perform better than those who do not would therefore add little to the existing literature. A more useful question is whether the observed relationship is retained when attendance is matched to the academic period of the course, grades are compared within the class in which they were awarded, and different frequencies of attendance are examined separately.

The broader literature on academic help-seeking provides a useful framework for interpreting attendance. Help-seeking is generally understood as a self-regulated and motivated learning strategy, and its relationship with achievement depends on both the form of help sought and the characteristics of the student \citep{fong2023}. Attendance at CMAT can therefore be treated as an observable form of formal academic help-seeking, but the number of recorded visits is not a direct measure of the amount or quality of tutoring received. Students may return for different reasons, including continuing difficulty, persistence, confidence in the service, scheduling constraints, or recommendations from others.

This study uses administrative data from the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla and makes three design choices intended to improve the interpretation of the attendance--performance relationship. First, CMAT visits are matched to the same academic period as the student's first eligible attempt at Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU), rather than using attendance accumulated across different periods. Second, final grades are standardised within instructor--period classes so that students are compared within a common grading context. Third, attendance is represented by the groups 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits, rather than assuming a linear relationship between number of visits and performance. These choices do not remove self-selection into mathematics support, but they address two avoidable measurement problems: attributing visits from other academic periods to the course result being analysed and pooling grades across classes with different grade distributions.

The study therefore addresses two questions: (1) how is attendance at CMAT during the MU academic period associated with classroom-relative performance in a student's first eligible MU attempt; and (2) among students who attend CMAT, is there clear evidence of performance differences across different frequencies of attendance? The analysis is explicitly observational and does not attempt to identify a causal effect of mathematics support.
