\abstract{
Mathematics-support attendance is often summarised as a binary indicator, although repeated use may reflect different mixtures of academic need, voluntary help-seeking and institutional encouragement. We analysed 6,627 first eligible attempts at a first-year university mathematics course, linking support-centre visits to the same academic period and standardising final performance within instructor--period groups. The main reporting grid distinguishes 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ visits. During at least part of the study period, three CMAT visits could satisfy a first-semester PPA participation activity, so the first three visits cannot be interpreted as a purely voluntary dose of support. Any recorded attendance was associated with 0.361 standard deviations higher adjusted final performance and a 15.3 percentage-point higher adjusted probability of passing. In the eight-group continuous model, every zero-versus-positive contrast survived Holm correction, whereas no positive-versus-positive contrast did. PASS showed a stronger upper-tail signal: the 7+ group had an 89.1\% observed pass rate, and its adjusted pass probability exceeded the one-visit group by 14.5 percentage points after Holm correction; the corresponding logistic point estimate gave 2.75 times the odds of passing. Descriptively, mean standardised performance rose from 0.267 to 0.382 between four and five visits and from 0.288 to 0.457 between six and 7+ visits, although neither adjacent contrast was statistically resolved. Distributional analysis revealed a second layer of heterogeneity. With imputed administrative outcomes, two-Gaussian mixtures were supported for most attendance groups and separated a relatively stable higher-performance mode from a lower-performance mode enriched in adverse outcomes; among users, the higher-component mean remained near 0.54--0.65 standard deviations while the lower-component weight fell sharply at five and again at 7+ visits. Using only observed numeric grades, however, evidence for two distinct components was much weaker: a two-Gaussian model was clearly supported for one and two visits, while several higher-frequency groups were adequately described by a single skew-normal distribution and the apparent 7+ lower component represented essentially one extreme failed observation. The results therefore identify a strong zero-versus-positive attendance margin and a distinctive high-frequency 7+ profile, while showing that the apparent two-regime structure depends materially on whether administrative outcomes are incorporated into the continuous outcome. These patterns are observational and should not be interpreted as causal effects of additional CMAT visits.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics and statistics support has become a routine component of higher education, especially in institutions where students enter quantitatively demanding programmes with heterogeneous mathematical preparation \citep{matthews2013,lawson2020,mullen2024}. Evaluation is nevertheless difficult because the most accessible administrative measure---attendance---is behavioural rather than assigned. Students choose whether to enter a support centre, may return because they are struggling, may return because they find the service useful, and may also be encouraged or required by the institution to participate. Attendance frequency therefore contains information about support use, but not a uniquely interpretable treatment dose.

Much of the observational literature has focused on the first margin: whether students who attend mathematics support obtain more favourable outcomes than those who do not. Positive associations have been reported in first-year mathematics and calculus settings, including studies that adjust for observed measures of prior attainment \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}. At the same time, identification-oriented studies using randomised encouragement, instrumental variables or quasi-experimental adjustment have produced more mixed estimates of direct academic effects \citep{paloyo2016,pugatch2018,buchele2024}. These findings make a distinction between description and causation essential: a strong user/non-user difference may be institutionally important without showing what would happen if an otherwise identical student were assigned one additional visit.

Repeated use introduces a second empirical margin. \citet{jacob2019} separated one-time use from higher attendance frequencies, and recent work by \citet{gokhool2026} has formalised the distinction between initial engagement and visit frequency among users. This is especially relevant in the present setting because attendance was not generated solely by voluntary help-seeking. During at least part of the study period, first-semester students could satisfy a PPA participation activity by completing three CMAT visits. The institutional purpose of PPA is to encourage participation in activities offered by the university, so attendance counts up to three may combine genuine demand for mathematics support with an incentive to complete a participation requirement. A fourth or later CMAT visit is less directly explained by that specific three-visit option, although it remains selected and may still reflect academic need, motivation or other unobserved characteristics.

This institutional threshold changes the substantive question. Rather than treating the sequence 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots as a homogeneous dose scale, it is useful to ask where the outcome profile changes after the incentivised range. Three empirical regions are therefore of particular interest: zero attendance, the first one to three visits that may be partly PPA-compatible, and repeated use beyond that threshold. Within the latter region, the data also permit a separate 7+ category, which captures students who return substantially more often than required to satisfy the three-visit participation option.

A third issue concerns the shape rather than only the mean of academic outcomes. A single average can conceal a distribution in which some students perform very well while another subset remains in the lower tail despite using support. This possibility is especially important when administrative final states are incorporated into a continuous outcome. A student who receives a numeric grade below the pass mark, a voluntary withdrawal (BV), a temporary withdrawal (RT) or an academic withdrawal (BA) has reached a different recorded endpoint, and assigning numerical values to administrative outcomes can strengthen or even create apparent lower-tail components. For that reason, evidence for a two-component outcome distribution must be examined twice: once using only observed numeric grades and again using the completed continuous outcome after the pre-specified administrative-outcome imputation.

The present study combines these three problems---frequency, institutional incentives and distributional heterogeneity---using administrative records from the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla. Visits are aligned to the same academic period as the student's first eligible attempt at Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU), and grades are standardised within instructor--period groups to account for local grading environments. The main reporting grid uses 0, exact 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 visits, and 7+ visits. An earlier outcome-blind support audit had favoured pooling six or more visits because exact six had weaker instructor--period overlap; that pooled-tail specification is retained as a robustness check, while the current manuscript resolves six and 7+ so that the upper-frequency pattern can be examined directly.

The analysis deliberately separates several levels of evidence. Adjusted mean performance and PASS comparisons identify where average outcomes differ across attendance frequencies; raw and Holm-adjusted pairwise tests distinguish local signals from family-wise evidence; numeric-failure analyses remove administrative outcomes entirely; and Gaussian-mixture, skew-normal, bootstrap and cross-validation diagnostics examine whether the outcome distribution is better represented by two modes than by one asymmetric distribution. Mixture components are treated as descriptive density features rather than literal latent student classes.

We address four questions. First, where do adjusted performance and PASS outcomes differ across 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ visits, and does the high-frequency 7+ group separate from lower-frequency users? Second, is there any evidence of an abrupt change at the institutionally incentivised third visit, or do more visible descriptive shifts occur only after the PPA-compatible range, particularly from four to five visits and from six to 7+? Third, do the observed numeric-grade and imputed continuous distributions support a recurring higher-performance and lower-performance structure among CMAT users, and how does the evidence change when administrative outcomes are included? Fourth, how does the composition of numeric failure, BV, RT and BA differ across attendance patterns? Throughout, the study is observational: the aim is to characterise the structure of outcomes associated with recorded CMAT use, not to estimate a causal dose-response effect of attendance.
