\abstract{
Evaluating mathematics support using administrative records is difficult because students are not randomly assigned to attend, attendance records may span several academic periods, and grade distributions may differ across teaching contexts. We examined 6,627 students on their first attempt at a first-year university mathematics course, matching visits to the same academic period as the course and standardising final grades within instructor--period groups. Students were grouped by 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits; 18.6\% attended the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at least once. Mean standardised grades were $-0.061$, $0.219$, $0.247$, $0.278$, and $0.355$ standard deviations across the five groups. Games--Howell comparisons showed that each group of students who attended CMAT had a higher mean grade than students with no recorded visits, whereas none of the six comparisons among attendance-frequency groups was significant at the 5\% level. Regression models with instructor--period fixed effects and adjustment for degree programme estimated differences of $0.305$ to $0.461$ standard deviations relative to students with no visits. Sensitivity analyses again showed higher mean grades among students who attended than among those who did not, but the ordering of the attendance-frequency groups was not consistent. CMAT attendance was associated with higher standardised grades, while the relationship between attendance frequency and performance was less consistent. The analysis is observational and does not establish a causal effect of mathematics support.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics learning support is now an established feature of higher education in many countries, particularly as institutions respond to differences in students' prior mathematical preparation and to the quantitative demands of a widening range of degree programmes \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}. A substantial body of research has examined the impact and evaluation of mathematics support, but studies based on attendance records face a persistent problem: students are not randomly assigned to use the service. Participation is often voluntary and may also be shaped by institutional arrangements. Students who attend may therefore differ from those who do not in characteristics that are themselves related to academic performance. Self-selection is consequently a central concern in observational evaluations of mathematics support \citep{mullen2024}.

Studies of mathematics support centres have commonly compared the grades or pass rates of students who attend with those of students who do not \citep{macanbhaird2009,jacob2019}. \citet{jacob2019}, in particular, found that students who attended once should be considered separately from students who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. Building on this work, the present study examines whether this pattern remains when attendance is matched to the relevant course period, grades are compared within the same instructor--period grading context, and different frequencies of attendance are considered separately. This distinction matters because usage data---who uses mathematics support, when, and how often---are widely used in evaluation, yet a single visit and repeated attendance need not carry the same interpretation \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}.

The broader literature on academic help-seeking provides a useful framework for interpreting attendance. Help-seeking is generally understood as a self-regulated and motivated learning strategy, and its relationship with achievement depends on both the form of help sought and student characteristics \citep{fong2023}. Attendance at CMAT can be viewed as an observable form of formal academic help-seeking, but visit counts do not indicate the quality or success of the support received \citep{lawson2020}. Repeated attendance can reflect both continuing need and continued engagement with the service, which complicates the interpretation of visit counts.

The present analysis addresses these issues in three ways. First, CMAT visits are matched to the same academic period as the student's Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU) course attempt rather than accumulated across different periods. Second, final grades are standardised within instructor--period groups so that students are compared with others whose work was evaluated in the same local grading context. Third, students are grouped by 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits rather than assuming that each additional visit has the same relationship with performance. These choices do not remove self-selection or other unmeasured differences between students, but they avoid assigning visits from other academic periods to the course result being analysed and reduce the influence of differences in grade distributions across teaching contexts.

The study addresses two questions: (1) what is the association between CMAT attendance during the MU academic period and the standardised grade on a student's first MU attempt; and (2) among students who attend CMAT, how does academic performance vary with the number of visits? The analysis is observational throughout and does not estimate a causal effect of mathematics support.
