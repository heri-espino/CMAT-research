\abstract{
Evaluating mathematics support using administrative records is difficult because students are not randomly assigned to attend, attendance records may span several academic periods, and grade distributions may differ across teaching contexts. We examined 6,627 students on their first attempt at a first-year university mathematics course, matching visits to the same academic period as the course and standardising final grades within instructor--period groups. Students were grouped by 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits; 18.6\% attended the Centro de Aprendizaje de Matem\'aticas (CMAT) at least once. Mean standardised grades were $-0.061$, $0.219$, $0.247$, $0.278$, and $0.355$ standard deviations across the five groups. Regression models with instructor--period fixed effects, degree-programme adjustment, and standard errors clustered at the instructor--period level estimated differences of $0.305$ to $0.461$ standard deviations relative to students with no visits. All four comparisons with zero visits remained statistically significant after Holm adjustment, whereas none of the six pairwise comparisons among the four attendance-frequency groups did. Sensitivity analyses again showed higher mean grades among students who attended than among those who did not, but the ordering of the attendance-frequency groups was not consistent. CMAT attendance was associated with higher standardised grades, but the attendance-frequency groups did not show a stable ordering across analyses. The analysis is observational and does not establish a causal effect of mathematics support.
}

\section{Introduction}

Mathematics learning support is now an established feature of higher education in many countries, particularly as institutions respond to differences in students' prior mathematical preparation and to the quantitative demands of a widening range of degree programmes \citep{matthews2013,lawson2020,mullen2024}. Evaluating these services is nevertheless difficult because evidence on usage and activity is not equivalent to evidence on academic impact, and studies based on attendance records generally compare students who have chosen, or have been encouraged, to use support with students who have not \citep{matthews2013,mullen2024}. Participation is often voluntary and may also be shaped by institutional arrangements, so students who attend may differ from those who do not in characteristics that are themselves related to academic performance. Research on non-engagement reinforces this concern because students who might benefit from mathematics learning support do not necessarily use the services available to them \citep{macanbhaird2013}. Self-selection is therefore a central limitation in observational evaluations of mathematics support.

Studies of mathematics support centres have commonly compared the grades or pass rates of students who attend with those of students who do not \citep{macanbhaird2009,jacob2019,rickard2018}. For example, \citet{rickard2018} reported a positive association between tutoring-centre attendance and Calculus I grades after adjusting for prior academic ability, while \citet{jacob2019} found that students who attended once should be considered separately from students who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. The present study uses a similarly disaggregated treatment of attendance while matching visits to the relevant course period, comparing grades within the same instructor--period grading context, and examining different numbers of visits separately. This distinction matters because usage data---who uses mathematics support, when, and how often---are widely used in evaluation, yet a single visit and repeated attendance need not carry the same interpretation \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}.

Designs intended to address selection more directly have produced more mixed evidence. In a randomized-encouragement evaluation of Peer Assisted Study Sessions, \citet{paloyo2016} reported a positive but statistically imprecise instrumental-variable estimate of the effect of attendance on grades. \citet{pugatch2018} showed that randomized messages could increase take-up of peer tutoring but found little evidence that the induced tutoring attendance improved learning outcomes. Using propensity-score matching and difference-in-differences in a course-specific mathematical learning and support centre, \citet{buchele2024} found no direct effect on mathematics performance, although they reported favourable effects on several affective outcomes. These findings do not imply that observational attendance comparisons are uninformative, but they underline the need to distinguish an attendance--performance association from a causal effect of support.

The broader literature on academic help-seeking provides useful context but also indicates an important measurement distinction. \citet{fong2023} describe help-seeking as a motivated, self-regulated process and distinguish formal sources of help, including academic support centres, from behavioural indicators such as the frequency of tutoring attendance; the latter do not directly measure help-seeking tendencies. We therefore treat CMAT visit counts as usage data rather than as a measure of a student's help-seeking disposition. Such data show who uses mathematics support, when, and how often, but do not establish why a student attended, what occurred during a visit, or the quality or success of the support received \citep{lawson2020}.

The present analysis addresses these issues in three ways. First, CMAT visits are matched to the same academic period as the student's Matem\'aticas Universitarias (MU) course attempt rather than accumulated across different periods. Second, final grades are standardised within instructor--period groups so that students are compared with others whose work was evaluated in the same local grading context. Third, students are grouped by 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits rather than assuming that each additional visit has the same relationship with performance. These choices do not remove self-selection or other unmeasured differences between students, but they avoid assigning visits from other academic periods to the course result being analysed and express each grade relative to the instructor--period context in which it was awarded.

The study addresses two questions: (1) what is the association between CMAT attendance during the MU academic period and the standardised grade on a student's first MU attempt; and (2) among students who attend CMAT, do standardised grades differ between those who attend once, twice, three times, or four or more times? The analysis is observational throughout and does not estimate a causal effect of mathematics support.
