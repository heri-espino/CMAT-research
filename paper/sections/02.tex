\section{Institutional context}

CMAT is the mathematics support centre at Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla, Mexico. It operates as a dedicated room with tables and whiteboards where mathematics professors are available outside regular classes. During the study period the centre was staffed from 08:00 to 17:50, Monday to Friday. Students were informed about the service by mathematics professors, could seek assistance from a professor other than their course instructor, and registered their attendance when entering the centre. Each registration is treated as one visit. The attendance records identify use of the centre but do not record visit duration, content or the staff member involved.

The available CMAT records cover 11 consecutive Spring and Autumn academic periods from Spring 2019 through Spring 2024. During at least part of this interval, an institutional first-semester participation requirement allowed students to satisfy a PPA participation activity by completing three CMAT visits. The third recorded visit can therefore have a different behavioural meaning from an otherwise identical additional visit: for some students it may mark completion of an institutional participation requirement rather than a purely self-initiated decision to seek further mathematical help. Attendance was consequently not determined solely by voluntary help-seeking, and the present study does not interpret visit count as a treatment dose or evaluate the causal effect of the PPA requirement itself.

\section{Data and methods}

\subsection{Study population}

The unit of analysis was the student's first eligible observed attempt at MU during a period with CMAT attendance coverage. Administrative equivalency, revalidation and other records that did not correspond to an observed instructor--period grading context were excluded. Numeric final grades were retained together with the adverse non-numeric codes baja voluntaria (BV; voluntary withdrawal), retiro temporal (RT; temporary withdrawal) and baja acad\'emica (BA; academic withdrawal). The resulting study sample contained 6,627 students distributed across 190 instructor--period groups.

Visits were linked by student and academic period. A CMAT registration counted towards exposure whenever it occurred during the same academic period as the MU attempt, irrespective of the subject label recorded for that visit. This period-wide definition is intentional because CMAT is a shared mathematics-support environment rather than a course-specific tutoring programme.

\subsection{Attendance-frequency groups}

The main reporting grid contains eight categories: 0, exact 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 visits, and 7+ visits. Their sample sizes are 5,393, 517, 245, 170, 96, 55, 50 and 101, respectively. This zero-inclusive grouping is used in the principal descriptive figures, Table~1 and the main pairwise outcome displays so that the large zero-versus-positive separation, the institutionally relevant three-visit threshold and the upper attendance tail can be inspected in a common frame.

The grouping evolved from an outcome-blind support audit. Exact counts of one through six visits contained 517, 245, 170, 96, 55 and 50 students, but separating exact six visits from the upper tail reduced the weakest overlap between adjacent positive-frequency groups from 26 to 15 instructor--period groups. The initial inferential specification therefore pooled six or more visits. Because the present paper is explicitly concerned with where differences appear across the attendance distribution, we subsequently resolved that tail for reporting and re-estimated the distributional models separately for exact six and 7+ visits rather than mechanically splitting a fitted 6+ model.

This choice should not be interpreted as making exact six and 7+ equally well supported inferential categories. The pooled 6+ specification remains a robustness check on sparse-tail inference, and the exact-six and 7+ estimates are interpreted with particular caution because of their smaller samples and weaker overlap. No upper cut was selected from outcome significance.
\subsection{Performance outcomes}

The primary continuous outcome was final MU performance standardised within instructor--period group. For student $i$ in group $c$,
\begin{equation}
Z_{ic}=\frac{Y_{ic}-\bar{Y}_{c}}{s_c},
\end{equation}
where $Y_{ic}$ is the completed final-grade value and $\bar{Y}_{c}$ and $s_c$ are the corresponding instructor--period mean and sample standard deviation. This standardisation is substantive because MU instructors set and grade their own assessments; differences in raw-grade location and dispersion across instructors and periods are therefore not treated as student-performance differences.

Numeric grades were left unchanged. For the continuous primary outcome, BV, RT and BA were assigned values below the institutional pass mark of 7.5 using the distribution of observed numeric grades below 7.5 within the same instructor--period group. A Gaussian kernel-density estimate with SciPy's default Scott bandwidth was used when the within-group pool was non-degenerate, with draws restricted to $[0,7.5)$ and random seed 42. Degenerate pools used empirical resampling, and groups with no observed sub-pass numeric grade used a uniform fallback below 7.5. A numeric complete-case standardised outcome was retained as a sensitivity analysis.

The binary outcome PASS equalled one for a numeric final grade of at least 7.5 and zero for a numeric grade below 7.5 or an outcome of BV, RT or BA. Unlike the continuous outcome, PASS requires no latent numeric value for administrative outcomes and therefore provides a direct robustness check against the possibility that the principal attendance contrast is generated by the imputation procedure.

\subsection{Academic-result composition}

To avoid treating all non-passing outcomes as substantively identical, we classified final records into four descriptive states: PASS, numeric grade below 7.5, BV/RT and BA. A second table retained five states by separating BV and RT.

We then examined the composition of outcomes conditional on non-PASS. The broad academic-management contrast compared any administrative outcome (BA/BV/RT) with a numeric grade below 7.5. Because the administrative codes need not reflect the same process, narrower contrasts compared BV/RT with numeric failure and, most specifically, BV with numeric failure after excluding RT and BA. RT versus numeric failure was retained as a diagnostic contrast. These comparisons describe the type of observed adverse outcome; they do not identify why a student received or selected an administrative outcome.

Administrative outcomes may occur before the end of the academic period and can therefore provide less opportunity to accumulate CMAT visits.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

The broad benchmark compared 0 visits with 1+ visits using instructor--period fixed effects, degree-programme indicators and standard errors clustered at the instructor--period level. For the continuous outcome, coefficients are expressed in standard-deviation units. For PASS and the conditional academic-management outcomes, linear-probability models were used so that coefficients are interpretable as adjusted percentage-point differences.

The main attendance-frequency analysis used all 6,627 students and indicators for 0, exact 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 visits, and 7+ visits. For the continuous outcome, we tested the joint null of equal adjusted outcomes across the eight groups and estimated all 28 pairwise contrasts. Pairwise $p$-values were adjusted within the full family using the Holm procedure. The same fixed-effect linear-probability specification provided multiplicity-aware PASS inference. This specification is retained for hypothesis testing because it produced a stable instructor--period-clustered covariance matrix across the complete eight-group design.

For interpretability of binary effect magnitude, we additionally fitted a fixed-effect logistic model with the same attendance indicators, instructor--period fixed effects and degree-programme adjustment, and display pairwise odds-ratio point estimates. In the full eight-group logistic fit, the cluster-robust covariance was rank-deficient when clustering at the same instructor--period level represented by the fixed effects, so logistic standard errors and $p$-values are not used for inference. The PASS $p$-value dashboard therefore reports the stable linear-probability-model tests, while the odds-ratio panel is used only to display direction and relative magnitude on a familiar multiplicative scale.

The pairwise effect dashboard places the continuous and binary results in a common 0--7+ frame. Separate significance dashboards show unadjusted pairwise $p$-values on the left and Holm-adjusted values on the right. The raw matrix is reported only to locate where local contrasts arise before family-wise multiplicity correction; inferential interpretation relies on the omnibus test and Holm-adjusted family. Because the PPA participation incentive occurs at three visits, all pairwise dashboards visually mark the boundary between two and three visits. This is not analysed as a regression-discontinuity or threshold-treatment design because assignment to the institutional requirement, its detailed historical implementation and other determinants of reaching three visits are not modelled as exogenous. We did not conduct a formal equivalence test because no substantively defensible equivalence margin had been specified independently of the observed estimates; failure to reject equality is therefore not interpreted as evidence of equivalence.

We additionally described the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of the continuous outcome and repeated the summaries using numeric grades only. The original pooled 6+ specification was retained as a support-based robustness check, while exact six and 7+ were re-estimated separately for the distributional analyses shown in the main figures.

To isolate completed numeric failure from administrative outcomes, we defined numeric failure as a final numeric grade below 7.5 and fitted fixed-effect logistic models among records with numeric final grades. This robustness analysis was developed under the earlier zero-inclusive 0/1/2/3/4/5/6+ grouping and is retained as a pooled-tail check rather than used to define the main eight-group result. Adjusted failure probabilities were obtained by standardising fitted probabilities over the empirical covariate distribution; their standard errors and 95\% confidence intervals used the delta method with the cluster-robust covariance matrix. Pairwise odds ratios were Holm-adjusted within each analysis family.

These outcomes were interpreted as an explicit robustness hierarchy. PASS tests the broad attendance association in the full cohort without imputing a latent grade to BV, RT or BA; numeric failure then removes administrative outcomes altogether; the numeric complete-case density analysis examines distributional shape using only observed grades; and the imputed continuous distribution is used last to evaluate how numerical representation of administrative outcomes modifies that shape. Agreement across the earlier stages indicates that an attendance--outcome association is not mechanically created by imputation, whereas divergence between complete-case and imputed density results identifies the extent to which administrative outcomes contribute to the lower-tail structure.

Because instructors recur across academic periods, we retained the earlier positive-attendance sensitivity with covariance clustered by instructor while preserving instructor--period fixed effects and the original instructor--period standardisation of grades. This sensitivity changes only the level at which residual dependence is allowed and uses the pooled 6+ tail; it is supplementary to the main eight-group reporting grid.

Finally, finite Gaussian mixtures were used to describe distributional shape rather than to classify students into latent types. For groups 0 through 5, univariate mixtures with $K\in\{1,2,3\}$ components were fitted to the numeric complete-case standardised grade using multiple EM initialisations. Exact six and 7+ visits were then re-estimated separately with the same procedure, rather than obtained by partitioning posterior assignments from the pooled 6+ fit. For $K=2$, candidate starts included empirical quartiles and the fixed pair $(-1.1,0.5)$ as additional starts rather than imposed component locations. We compared BIC and ICL, posterior entropy, mean maximum responsibility and Ashman's $D$, and used a parametric-bootstrap likelihood-ratio comparison of $K=1$ versus $K=2$. Component composition was summarised using posterior responsibilities rather than hard assignment. The same procedure was repeated on the imputed continuous outcome. To distinguish multi-component structure from ordinary asymmetry, a one-component skew-normal distribution was fitted to the same observations and compared with the one-Gaussian and two-Gaussian candidates using AIC and BIC, a parametric bootstrap under the fitted skew-normal null and repeated five-fold cross-validation of held-out log predictive density. Mixture components are interpreted throughout as flexible density approximations rather than identified student types.
