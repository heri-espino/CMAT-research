Students who attended CMAT during the MU academic period had higher mean standardised grades than students with no recorded visits. This difference was observed for each of the four attendance groups. By contrast, none of the six pairwise comparisons among the one-, two-, three-, and four-or-more-visit groups was statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons, and the ordering of these groups changed across the sensitivity analyses. Taken together, the results show a consistent descriptive difference between no recorded attendance and each level of recorded attendance, but not a stable ordering of performance by attendance frequency.

CMAT attendance should therefore be interpreted as service usage rather than as a direct measure of a student's help-seeking tendency. This distinction is especially important because some attendance was shaped by an institutional participation requirement. Consistent with the distinction made by \citet{fong2023} between help-seeking constructs and behavioural indicators such as tutoring attendance, the CMAT records establish who attended and how often but not the student's motivation for attending. They also do not record what occurred during a visit or the quality or success of the support received, which are recognised limitations of usage data \citep{lawson2020}.

The comparison with \citet{jacob2019} is particularly relevant. Their longitudinal TEAMAT study found that students who attended mathematics support once should be distinguished from those who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. In our data, the one-visit group also had a higher mean standardised grade than the zero-visit group, and the adjusted comparison remained statistically significant after Holm correction; by contrast, no pairwise comparison among the four attendance-frequency groups remained significant after adjustment. The studies differ in institutional setting, attendance grouping and outcome definition, so the patterns across attendance frequencies are not directly comparable.

Matching attendance records to the MU academic period and standardising grades within instructor--period groups align visits and grades more closely in time and grading context, but they do not address self-selection. Reviews of mathematics-support evaluation identify self-selection as a central limitation because users and non-users are compared without random assignment, while routine administrative records often contain only a limited set of variables with which to characterise those differences \citep{lawson2020,mullen2024}. Attendance in the present study was also partly shaped by an institutional requirement. The available data therefore cannot separate the observed association with attendance from unmeasured differences between students who attended and those who did not.

For mathematics support centres using routine attendance data for evaluation, the results suggest three practical considerations. Attendance should be matched to the academic period of the course grade being studied rather than accumulated across unrelated periods. Evaluations should account for differences in grading practices or grade distributions across teaching contexts rather than treating all raw grades as directly comparable. Finally, where sample size permits, students with no recorded attendance should be distinguished from students with different numbers of visits before assuming a linear relationship between attendance frequency and performance. These steps do not remove self-selection bias, but they make observational comparisons easier to interpret.

\section{Limitations}

The results should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the available data do not contain a comprehensive measure of prior mathematical attainment for the full study sample, so differences between students who attend CMAT and those who do not may reflect unobserved differences present before or during the course. Second, the CMAT records identify visits but not their duration or content, so attendance frequency measures use of the centre rather than the amount or type of support received. Third, attendance and academic performance are observed within the same academic period, so students may begin attending in response to difficulties that emerge during the course. In addition, the exact dates of BV, RT and BA withdrawals are unavailable; students who withdraw earlier may have less opportunity to accumulate visits, mechanically linking some adverse outcomes to lower attendance counts. Restricting the continuous outcome to numeric final grades removes these withdrawal outcomes but conditions the analysis on course completion and therefore does not fully resolve this timing problem. Fourth, the primary continuous outcome uses a single seeded imputation for the withdrawal outcomes, so its standard errors do not include uncertainty about the assigned latent grades; the sensitivity analyses show that the ordering and magnitude of the attendance-group means depend on how those outcomes are represented. Fifth, institutional requirements and other contextual factors may influence attendance frequency, and the attendance process may vary across academic periods, so differences between visit categories should not be interpreted as the effect of an additional visit or assumed to be constant over time. Finally, comparisons involving specific attendance frequencies draw on fewer instructor--period groups than the broader comparison between students who did and did not attend, particularly for the three-visit group. Nevertheless, 176 of the 190 instructor--period groups contain both students who attended CMAT and students with no recorded visits, and the adjusted estimates change little when instructor--period groups with fewer than 20 students are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

Among students observed on their first MU attempt, CMAT attendance during the same academic period was associated with higher standardised grades. Mean performance increased descriptively across the five attendance groups in the main analysis; after Holm adjustment, however, only comparisons between students with no recorded visits and each group of students who attended remained statistically significant, while no pairwise comparison among the four attendance-frequency groups did. The ordering of the attendance-frequency groups also changed when non-numeric course outcomes were handled differently. Matching attendance to the course period and standardising grades within instructor--period groups provides a more interpretable observational comparison, but it does not identify a causal effect of mathematics support. Visit counts therefore represent attendance frequency rather than the amount of support received.
