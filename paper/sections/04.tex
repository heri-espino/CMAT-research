The central contribution of this study concerns measurement and interpretation rather than the rediscovery of a positive user--non-user difference. Previous mathematics-support research has already shown that even one visit can distinguish students who engage with support from students who never attend \citep{jacob2019}. Our results instead show that a sizeable positive association remains after two common sources of contamination are removed: support contacts are aligned to the academic period of the focal course, and academic performance is expressed within the grading environment shared by students in the same instructor-period classroom. Once visit count is represented transparently as 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more contacts, the point estimates increase with use in the primary outcome, but inferential separation among positive-use groups is much weaker than the separation between non-use and positive use.

This interpretation connects naturally to the broader literature on academic help-seeking. \citet{fong2023} characterise help-seeking as a self-regulated and motivated strategy whose relationship with achievement depends on the form of help-seeking and on student characteristics. In that framework, the present results are more informative about the behavioural state of observed formal help-seeking than about a production function for tutoring sessions. A student who registers once has crossed from non-use into observed formal support, while subsequent registrations may reflect heterogeneous need, persistence, confidence, scheduling constraints, adviser recommendations, or other institutional conditions. Administrative visit count alone cannot distinguish those mechanisms.

The comparison with \citet{jacob2019} is therefore informative. Their longitudinal TEAMAT study showed that one visit should be distinguished from never attending and also reported increasingly favourable outcomes at higher attendance levels. Our results reproduce the importance of separating non-use from any observed attendance in a different institutional setting, but the exact positive-use groups are not cleanly distinguishable from one another after multiplicity-aware pairwise testing. This difference does not imply that additional visits are ineffective; rather, it shows that the appearance of a dose gradient depends on the outcome definition, exposure construction, institutional setting, and selection processes that generate repeated attendance.

The measurement choices also clarify what the study does not solve. Temporal alignment prevents later or earlier support contacts from being misclassified as exposure to the focal MU course, while classroom standardisation prevents pooled grading differences from mechanically generating the result. Neither adjustment makes users and non-users exchangeable. A favourable association can arise because support is beneficial, because students who seek help also engage in other productive behaviours, because students respond to academic difficulty in heterogeneous ways, or through combinations of these mechanisms. Negative selection is also plausible if students experiencing greater difficulty are more likely to seek support, in which case a crude user--non-user comparison could understate an underlying benefit. The available administrative data do not identify the balance among these pathways.

The sensitivity analysis reinforces this distinction. The broad sign pattern---negative mean classroom-relative performance among non-users and positive means among each observed user group---is preserved when adverse outcomes are handled differently, while the ordering of positive-use groups changes. In the complete-case specification, for example, four-or-more visits no longer produce the highest group mean. A simple statement that ``more visits correspond to better outcomes'' is therefore less robust than the statement that observed formal support use is associated with higher classroom-relative performance than non-use.

For mathematics-support evaluation practice, the analysis suggests several transferable reporting choices. Administrative studies should align support contacts to the academic period of the focal outcome rather than attach lifetime or later accumulated attendance to a single course grade; they should inspect whether raw grades are comparable across local grading contexts; they should separate non-use from positive use before imposing a linear attendance effect; and, when sample sizes permit, they should display exact or flexible positive-use intensities rather than allowing a convenient pooling rule to determine the visual result. These practices do not solve self-selection, but they make the remaining observational comparison more transparent and easier to reproduce across institutions.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations constrain interpretation. First, the study lacks a comprehensive, uniformly observed pre-enrolment measure of mathematics proficiency, and classroom fixed effects do not address student-level self-selection into formal help-seeking. Second, CMAT records count registrations rather than tutoring duration or content, and manual registration can introduce duplicate or missing contacts. Third, visits and course performance are observed within the same academic period, so help-seeking can respond to evolving academic difficulty; temporal alignment improves measurement relevance but does not establish temporal exogeneity. Fourth, treatment of non-numeric adverse outcomes affects the ordering and magnitude of the positive-use group means, although the broad separation between zero visits and positive use is preserved. Fifth, institutional conditions can shape the distribution of observed visit counts, so differences among positive-use categories should not be interpreted as unconstrained marginal responses to additional tutoring. Finally, the exact positive-use contrasts are identified from fewer classrooms than the broad any-use comparison, especially for the three-visit group, although 176 of 190 classrooms contain both users and non-users and the adjusted estimates are stable when classrooms with fewer than 20 students are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

For first-attempt university mathematics, contemporaneous CMAT use is strongly associated with higher classroom-relative academic performance, but the main contribution is not the existence of that association. By aligning support contacts to the focal course period, comparing performance within a common grading environment, and displaying visit count transparently as 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more registrations, the analysis separates a robust positive-use versus non-use pattern from a much less certain dose interpretation among users. The primary point estimates rise with visit count, but pairwise differences among positive-use groups are not statistically distinct after multiplicity-aware testing and their ordering is sensitive to outcome construction. These results do not identify a causal effect of tutoring; they provide a more defensible observational description of mathematics-support use and a reproducible framework for evaluating similar administrative data without conflating better measurement with causal identification.
