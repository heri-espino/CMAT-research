\section{Discussion}

This study separates the broad distinction between entering mathematics support and not doing so from the more granular question of attendance frequency among users. The first distinction was pronounced: students with any recorded CMAT attendance had substantially higher adjusted standardised final performance and a higher probability of passing than students with no recorded visits. The second distinction was much less clear. Under the primary instructor--period-clustered specification, the 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ groups did not differ jointly in either principal performance outcome, and none of the pairwise comparisons survived multiplicity adjustment. Resolving the upper tail into exact six and 7+ visits produced several unadjusted contrasts between 7+ and the 1--3 visit groups, but none survived Holm correction; importantly, the adjacent two-versus-three comparison was weak for both performance and passing, so the adjusted outcomes showed no abrupt empirical break at the institutionally incentivised third visit. Instructor-level clustering produced more suggestive evidence---a continuous-outcome omnibus $p$-value of 0.0477 and one Holm-significant, non-adjacent 1-versus-6+ pass contrast---but no adjacent contrast survived. Across covariance specifications and grouping sensitivities, the data therefore support a strong observational separation between zero and positive attendance and leave open some broader low-versus-high frequency separation, while providing no support for a simple visit-by-visit dose staircase.

This two-stage empirical structure is conceptually consistent with treating initial engagement and repeated use as distinct dimensions of participation. \citet{gokhool2026} make the same distinction in modelling engagement with mathematics and statistics support, separating the binary decision to engage at least once from visit frequency among those who engage. Our outcome analysis addresses a different question, but the parallel is useful: a centre may show a marked difference between users and non-users even when variation in the number of visits among users is comparatively difficult to distinguish. This also helps explain why reducing attendance to a single linear count can be misleading.

The results broadly align with earlier observational mathematics-support studies that report more favourable academic outcomes among users \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}, while adding two qualifications. First, a favourable user/non-user difference did not translate into a robust ordering across positive visit frequencies. \citet{jacob2019} likewise distinguished a single visit from non-attendance and used progressively pooled upper-frequency categories, underscoring the practical difficulty of resolving increasingly sparse visit counts. Second, the present findings remain observational. Identification-oriented studies have shown that interventions can increase participation without necessarily producing precisely estimated performance effects \citep{paloyo2016,pugatch2018}, and \citet{buchele2024} similarly illustrates that adjustment for selection can yield more nuanced conclusions than raw user/non-user comparisons.

The institutional mission of CMAT also matters for how these findings should be communicated. A university creates and maintains a mathematics support centre because it expects the service to help students, so a favourable association is not an institutionally neutral result. That expectation does not invalidate evaluation, but it increases the importance of guarding against confirmatory analysis. In this study the primary frequency grouping was fixed without using outcomes, the zero-versus-positive benchmark is distinguished from within-user frequency contrasts, raw pairwise $p$-values are shown only as exploratory diagnostics alongside Holm-adjusted values, and conclusions are required to agree across several outcome representations before being interpreted as robust. The resulting evidence is favourable to CMAT at the broad user/non-user margin, but it should not be presented as proof that the centre caused the observed differences.

The most distinctive finding concerns the composition of adverse outcomes. Students who used CMAT did not have more administrative outcomes overall; indeed, the combined BA/BV/RT share was slightly lower than among students with no visits. However, conditional on not passing, users were much less likely to finish with a numeric grade below 7.5 and correspondingly more likely to have an administrative outcome. Crucially, this pattern was not shared by the administrative codes. The adjusted contrast was concentrated in BV, while the corresponding RT contrast was essentially null and BA was rare among users.

One possible interpretation is that CMAT attendance marks a broader pattern of academic engagement or institutional navigation. Students who seek support may also differ in how closely they monitor academic progress, whether they seek advice, or how they use available academic procedures when a course is going poorly. Under this interpretation, BV may represent one way in which an adverse academic trajectory is managed before it becomes a numeric failure. The administrative records cannot test this mechanism directly. They do not record students' knowledge of regulations, motivation, concern for grade-point average, advice received, reasons for withdrawal, or the timing of the withdrawal decision. The BV result should therefore be treated as evidence about outcome composition, not as proof that CMAT users are intrinsically more engaged or that attendance causes voluntary withdrawal.

This distinction is important for mathematics-support evaluation more generally. A pass/fail variable treats a student who completes a course with a low numeric grade as equivalent to a student whose record ends administratively, whereas those outcomes may represent different academic trajectories. Conversely, a continuous analysis that assigns latent numeric values to administrative outcomes can make differences in withdrawal composition appear as changes in the lower tail of performance. In the present data, the imputed outcome showed more separation in the lower quantiles than the numeric complete-case outcome, while the complete-case frequency pattern was comparatively flat. The evidence is therefore most informative when read across complementary endpoints rather than when any single representation is treated as uniquely definitive.

The distribution-shape analysis clarifies how much of this lower-tail structure is attributable to simple asymmetry versus a multi-component density. A single Gaussian described every complete-case frequency group poorly, but rejection of one Gaussian alone was not treated as evidence for two populations because a Gaussian mixture can approximate an ordinary skewed density. The direct comparison with a one-component skew-normal therefore provides the more relevant diagnostic. For zero, one and two visits, BIC, the parametric bootstrap under the fitted skew-normal null and repeated held-out log predictive density all favoured the two-Gaussian representation. For three, four and 6+ visits, the same diagnostics were instead compatible with a single strongly left-skewed distribution, while the small five-visit group gave mixed predictive evidence and was treated as inconclusive. The complete-case results therefore support distributional heterogeneity in some attendance groups but not a universal two-population interpretation.

The imputed outcome was qualitatively different: a two-Gaussian mixture outperformed the skew-normal in all seven groups under the penalised-fit, bootstrap and predictive diagnostics. Posterior responsibilities showed why this difference matters substantively: among CMAT users, the imputed lower component was approximately 55.5\% administrative outcomes, nearly half of its expected mass being BV, whereas the higher component was approximately 97\% PASS and only about 2\% administrative. Administrative-outcome representation therefore materially sharpens the lower component, although the complete-case evidence for zero, one and two visits shows that imputation is not the sole source of distributional complexity.

The exact-six versus 7+ EM rerun reinforces the caution required in the upper tail. For numeric complete cases, exact six visits were adequately represented by a single skew-normal, whereas the 7+ group produced BIC and bootstrap support for a two-Gaussian fit whose lower component contained only about 1\% of the observations and collapsed to the numerical variance floor. That is more consistent with an extreme-observation component than with a stable latent subgroup. On the imputed outcome, the 7+ mixture was more coherent, while exact six remained specification-sensitive. Splitting the old 6+ category therefore adds descriptive information but does not reveal a clean distributional transition at six or seven visits, and it strengthens the case for retaining the pooled 6+ category as the primary inferential specification.

The four outcome representations provide a useful robustness sequence. PASS uses the full sample without assigning latent grades to administrative outcomes and preserves the pronounced zero-versus-positive attendance contrast. The numeric-failure analysis removes administrative outcomes altogether and shows the same broad separation. The complete-case density analysis then distinguishes groups whose observed grades require more than a single skewed distribution from groups for which skewness is an adequate parsimonious explanation. Only after those checks does the imputed density show a consistently clearer lower component, which is strongly enriched in administrative outcomes. This sequence supports the narrower conclusion that imputation amplifies lower-tail separation rather than creating the entire attendance--outcome pattern. It does not justify interpreting the fitted Gaussian components as literal student classes, because the models describe density shape, component overlap remains substantial in several groups and component weights do not follow a monotone attendance sequence.

The findings also caution against interpreting non-attendance as evidence of low motivation. Students may fail to use mathematics support for multiple reasons, including lack of perceived need, limited awareness, practical constraints or affective barriers \citep{macanbhaird2013}. Similarly, CMAT attendance itself is not a direct measure of help-seeking disposition \citep{fong2023}. Some attendance in the present setting was influenced by an institutional first-semester PPA participation requirement in which three CMAT visits could satisfy a participation activity. This makes the third visit especially important for interpretation because a student reaching three visits may be completing an institutional requirement, seeking support voluntarily, or both. The absence of a clear two-versus-three outcome contrast does not estimate the effect of that incentive, but it argues against reading the third visit as an observed performance threshold. More generally, the user/non-user separation may reflect a mixture of support use, prior preparation, academic need, institutional exposure and unmeasured student characteristics.

\subsection{Implications for mathematics-support evaluation}

Four implications follow for evaluations based on routine administrative data. First, initial engagement and subsequent frequency should be analysed separately when the data permit, because a strong user/non-user contrast does not imply that every additional visit corresponds to a distinct academic outcome. Second, high-frequency categories should be defined from exposure support rather than selected retrospectively from outcome significance. In this study, the 6+ upper category was chosen from student counts and instructor--period overlap before outcome differences across visit frequencies were examined. Third, institutional incentives attached to specific visit counts should be mapped explicitly into the interpretation of frequency: a threshold such as three visits can change why students accumulate visits even when no outcome discontinuity is visible at that count. Fourth, administrative outcome codes should not automatically be collapsed into a generic failure category when they represent substantively different ways in which an academic attempt ends.

These recommendations do not solve self-selection. They instead make the descriptive target clearer: what differs between students who enter a mathematics-support system and those who do not, what can be distinguished among users with different attendance frequencies, and whether adverse outcomes have the same composition across those groups.

\section{Limitations}

The central limitation is unmeasured selection. The administrative data available for this study do not provide a comprehensive pre-enrolment measure of mathematical preparation for the full cohort, so the user/non-user differences may partly reflect characteristics present before MU. Adjustment for an observed preparation measure, if available with adequate and comparable coverage, would reduce one source of confounding but would still not measure motivation, institutional knowledge or advice received and would not establish causality.

Attendance and academic outcomes are also observed within the same academic period, so students may begin attending because difficulties emerge during the course. The records measure entries to CMAT but not visit duration, content or quality of support. Attendance was also partly shaped by the three-visit PPA participation requirement, making visit count a mixture of voluntary and institutionally influenced use. The available analytic data do not provide a period-by-period indicator of individual assignment to, compliance with or motivation for that requirement, so the three-visit threshold cannot be treated as an exogenous discontinuity or used here to estimate a causal incentive effect.

Administrative outcomes require additional caution. Exact withdrawal dates are not available, so a student who leaves earlier may have had less opportunity to accumulate visits. We therefore keep this timing point brief rather than using it as the primary explanation for the observed composition. More importantly, the reasons for BV, RT and BA are not observed. Although the present pattern is concentrated in BV, we cannot infer that students selected BV to protect their academic average, that they knew the relevant procedure because of greater engagement, or that CMAT attendance influenced the decision. Any statement about the formal consequences of BV or RT for grade-point average or transcripts should be tied to verified institutional rules applicable during the study period.

The primary continuous outcome also requires numerical representation of BV, RT and BA. The within-instructor-period imputation preserves their adverse status while avoiding a fixed arbitrary score, but the exact latent grades are not observed and imputation uncertainty is not propagated through multiple imputation. The numeric complete-case analysis avoids that assumption but conditions on receipt of a numeric final grade. The contrast between these outcomes is therefore informative about sensitivity rather than a means of identifying a single true latent performance value.

The upper attendance groups contain fewer students and fewer overlapping instructor--period contexts than the lower-frequency groups. The outcome-blind grouping rule mitigates this problem but does not eliminate limited precision in the tail. Students are also nested within instructors who may teach in multiple periods. Re-clustering by instructor reduced the effective number of clusters to 51 among users and produced more suggestive frequency inference than the primary instructor--period covariance estimator, including one non-adjacent pass contrast after Holm adjustment. This sensitivity is reported because it weakens any interpretation that all positive-frequency groups are empirically identical, while the absence of adjacent adjusted contrasts still limits a dose-like interpretation.

\section{Conclusion}

Students with recorded CMAT attendance differed substantially from students with no recorded visits in both standardised final performance and probability of passing. The same broad separation was evident when administrative outcomes were excluded and failure was defined only from observed numeric grades, so it cannot be attributed solely to the imputation used for the continuous outcome. At the same time, the stronger lower-component structure of the imputed distribution shows that administrative outcomes materially shape the lower tail. Among students who did not pass, CMAT users were more likely to end with an administrative outcome rather than a numeric failure, and the adjusted contrast was concentrated in voluntary withdrawal rather than temporary or academic withdrawal.

These differences did not extend to a robust visit-by-visit ordering across 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits among users. The finer 1--6 and 7+ sensitivity produced unadjusted contrasts concentrated in the 7+ tail rather than at the institutionally incentivised third visit, and none survived Holm adjustment. The results therefore distinguish initial support use from subsequent attendance frequency and numeric failure from administrative outcomes, while showing why continuous, binary and complete-case representations should be interpreted jointly. They are consistent with the institutional expectation that CMAT users should fare better at the broad descriptive margin, but the same data also show why that expectation cannot be converted into a causal claim: attendance is selected, partly institutionally incentivised, and intertwined with academic need and administrative trajectories.
