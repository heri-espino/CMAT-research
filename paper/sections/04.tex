\section{Discussion}

The results suggest that CMAT attendance is structured by more than one process. The strongest and most stable contrast is between no recorded attendance and any use of the centre, but the full 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ analysis also reveals a distinctive high-frequency tail. Every positive-frequency group had higher adjusted standardised performance than the zero group after Holm correction, and every positive-frequency group had a higher adjusted PASS probability than zero. Within users, continuous-outcome differences were much weaker, yet the 7+ group separated from the one-visit group on PASS even after multiplicity adjustment: its adjusted pass probability was 14.5 percentage points higher, and its logistic point estimate corresponded to 2.75 times the odds of passing.

That upper-tail result is substantively important because seven or more visits are difficult to explain by the three-visit PPA activity alone. During at least part of the study period, three CMAT visits could satisfy a university participation requirement intended to encourage students to engage with activities offered by the institution. The first three visits can therefore mix academic demand with an institutional incentive. A student who attends four, five, six or seven or more times has necessarily gone beyond completion of that specific three-visit CMAT option, even though later attendance remains self-selected and may reflect continuing difficulty, perceived benefit, motivation, schedule flexibility or other unobserved characteristics.

The data do not show an outcome discontinuity at the PPA threshold itself. Two and three visits were nearly indistinguishable in both adjusted standardised performance and PASS. This matters because it argues against interpreting the third visit as an empirical academic threshold merely because it is an institutional participation threshold. Instead, the more visible descriptive changes occur later. Mean standardised performance increased from 0.267 at four visits to 0.382 at five, and from 0.288 at exact six to 0.457 at 7+. Neither adjacent contrast was statistically resolved after adjustment, so these should not be presented as causal jumps. They are nevertheless useful features of the observed profile because they occur beyond the attendance range most directly compatible with the PPA incentive.

The 7+ group deserves particular attention without being overstated. It had the highest observed pass rate and the highest mean standardised outcome, and PASS remained significantly higher than in the one-visit group after Holm correction. At the same time, 7+ did not differ significantly from exact six on either principal outcome, and the continuous 1-versus-7+ contrast did not survive multiplicity correction. The evidence therefore supports describing 7+ as a distinctive high-frequency profile, especially for PASS, but not as a demonstrated threshold effect beginning precisely at the seventh visit.

This interpretation refines the usual distinction between engagement and frequency in mathematics-support research. Prior observational studies have reported more favourable outcomes among users than non-users \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}, while recent work has explicitly separated initial engagement from subsequent visit frequency \citep{gokhool2026}. The present results show why that separation matters in an institutionally incentivised setting: low-frequency attendance may partly reflect participation requirements, whereas high-frequency attendance is less directly explained by that mechanism. Even so, the observational design cannot determine whether repeated attendance improves outcomes, whether students who are likely to succeed are more willing to return, or whether both processes operate simultaneously.

\subsection{A high-performing mode and a lower-performance mode}

The distributional results add a second dimension to the frequency analysis. On the imputed continuous outcome, most attendance groups were better represented by a two-Gaussian mixture than by a single skew-normal distribution. Among CMAT users, the higher-performance component was remarkably stable: its mean remained between approximately 0.54 and 0.65 standard deviations from one through 7+ visits. The lower component remained below that mode and was strongly enriched in adverse outcomes, especially BV. This produces a useful descriptive picture of two outcome regimes among users: a large group concentrated around relatively strong performance and a smaller lower-outcome group whose members either fail numerically or end the course administratively.

The relative weight of that lower component changes much more than the location of the higher component. In the imputed outcome, the lower-component weight was 35.7\% at four visits but only 9.1\% at five, then 25.2\% at exact six and 14.3\% at 7+. Those movements line up with the descriptive increases in mean performance from four to five and from six to 7+. The pattern suggests that some of the apparent frequency differences arise because the proportion of observations in the lower part of the outcome distribution changes, rather than because the higher-performing mode steadily shifts upward with every additional visit.

This two-regime description must, however, be distinguished from a claim that there are literally two stable types of students who use CMAT. When the analysis is restricted to observed numeric grades, the evidence is much less uniform. A two-Gaussian model is well supported for one and two visits, but a single skew-normal is adequate for three, four and exact six visits, while the five-visit group is inconclusive. The apparent 7+ lower component in observed grades represents essentially one extreme failed observation and collapses to the numerical variance floor. In other words, the observed-grade data do not support a universal two-population interpretation.

The contrast between observed and imputed outcomes is itself substantive. The stronger two-component structure after imputation shows that administrative outcomes are not a peripheral technical detail; they materially define the lower part of the completed academic-outcome distribution. Posterior composition reinforces this point. At one, two and three visits, approximately half or more of the imputed lower component was BV, whereas the corresponding higher components were roughly 95--97\% PASS. At exact six and 7+, the higher components were again about 98\% PASS, while the lower components contained substantial BV and numeric non-passing mass. Thus the lower regime is better understood as a mixture of poor academic outcomes and administrative endings than as a pure failed-grade class.

This distinction also clarifies the idea of students who attend but still do poorly. Such students are present in the data, but they are represented differently depending on the outcome definition. In observed numeric grades they form a small and sometimes unstable lower tail; in the imputed completed outcome they are joined by students whose course attempt ended in BV, RT or BA, producing a more coherent lower-performance component. Reporting both versions is therefore necessary to avoid attributing to numeric failure what is partly an administrative-outcome phenomenon.

\subsection{Academic-management outcomes}

The composition analysis supports that interpretation. CMAT users were less likely than non-users to end with a completed numeric grade below 7.5, but among students who did not pass they were more likely to have an administrative outcome. The adjusted contrast was concentrated in BV rather than RT, while BA was rare among users. This does not imply that CMAT attendance caused withdrawal. It indicates that the lower outcome margin differs not only in level but also in type.

One possible explanation is broader academic engagement or institutional navigation. Students who seek help may also be more likely to monitor their progress, ask for advice or use available administrative procedures when a course is going poorly. The available records cannot test that mechanism because they do not contain students' reasons for withdrawal, their understanding of institutional rules, the advice they received, or the timing of the decision relative to CMAT attendance. BV should therefore be treated as an observed component of the academic trajectory, not as evidence of strategic behaviour caused by the centre.

\subsection{Institutional expectations and interpretation}

The university has an institutional reason to expect CMAT to be useful: the centre exists to provide additional mathematics support and PPA explicitly encourages participation in university activities. That expectation is legitimate programme context, but it also makes confirmatory interpretation a particular risk. A favourable attendance association should not be treated as validation simply because the institution expects the centre to work.

Several features of the analysis are intended to separate evidence from expectation. The zero-versus-positive benchmark is distinguished from repeated attendance; exact pairwise comparisons are shown rather than selecting only favourable groups; raw $p$-values are displayed beside Holm-adjusted values; the 7+ result is interpreted together with its small sample and overlap limitations; and the apparent two-component structure is tested against a flexible skew-normal alternative and reported separately for observed and imputed outcomes. The resulting picture is favourable to CMAT at the broad descriptive margin and identifies a particularly strong 7+ PASS profile, but it does not establish that additional visits caused those outcomes.

\subsection{Implications for mathematics-support evaluation}

The findings suggest four practical principles for evaluating support-centre data. First, initial engagement and repeated attendance should be analysed separately, because the largest association may occur at entry into the support system rather than at each subsequent visit. Second, institutional incentives must be mapped onto the exposure definition: in this setting, one to three visits can have a different behavioural interpretation from attendance beyond three. Third, high-frequency tails should be retained when the sample permits, because pooling them can hide substantively interesting profiles such as the 7+ group, although sparse overlap must remain visible in the interpretation. Fourth, distributional analyses should report both observed numeric grades and any completed/imputed outcome when administrative endings contribute materially to the lower tail.

These principles improve description but do not solve selection. The study answers how outcomes differ across recorded patterns of CMAT use; it does not answer what would happen if otherwise identical students were assigned one, five or seven visits.

\section{Limitations}

The principal limitation is unmeasured selection. Students are not randomly assigned to CMAT attendance or to a visit count, and the available administrative data do not provide a comprehensive pre-enrolment measure of mathematical preparation for the full cohort. Differences between zero, low-frequency and high-frequency attendance may therefore reflect characteristics that precede support use. Adjustment for an entrance-exam measure, if coverage and scale comparability are adequate, would reduce one source of confounding but would not establish causality.

Attendance and outcomes are observed within the same academic period, so timing may also run in both directions. Students may attend because difficulties emerge, may return because support is useful, or may stop attending because they withdraw or no longer need help. The records count entries but do not measure visit duration, content, quality or the staff member involved. Visit frequency is therefore a coarse exposure.

PPA complicates the meaning of the first three visits. The analytic records do not identify, student by student and period by period, who was subject to the requirement, whether a particular CMAT visit was motivated by PPA, whether the student could satisfy PPA through another activity, or whether the student would have attended CMAT anyway. The three-visit boundary cannot therefore be used here as a regression discontinuity, instrument or natural experiment. Frequencies above three are less directly explained by the CMAT PPA option but remain selected.

The upper-frequency groups are small. Exact six contains 50 students overall and 43 numeric complete cases; 7+ contains 101 students overall and 93 numeric complete cases. Instructor--period overlap is weaker in this part of the distribution. The Holm-significant PASS difference between one and 7+ is therefore noteworthy, but it should be interpreted together with the absence of a significant six-versus-7+ contrast and the broader uncertainty around sparse high-frequency groups.

The mixture analysis has additional limitations. Gaussian components are flexible density approximations, not identified latent classes. In particular, the complete-case 7+ lower component is effectively a one-observation component at the variance floor and should not be interpreted as a stable subgroup. The imputed analysis produces a more coherent lower component, but its structure depends on numerical representation of BV, RT and BA. The within-instructor-period imputation preserves their adverse status without assigning a fixed arbitrary score, yet the latent numeric values are not observed and imputation uncertainty is not propagated through multiple imputation.

Administrative outcomes themselves require caution. Exact withdrawal dates and reasons are unavailable, so students who leave earlier may have had less opportunity to accumulate visits. The data cannot establish that BV was used to protect a grade-point average, that students learned about administrative options through CMAT, or that attendance changed the withdrawal decision. Formal institutional consequences of BV, RT and BA should be verified against contemporaneous university policy before stronger claims are made.

Finally, the full eight-group fixed-effect logistic model yielded interpretable odds-ratio point estimates but a rank-deficient instructor--period-clustered covariance matrix. Logistic uncertainty estimates are therefore not used. Multiplicity-aware PASS inference is based on the fixed-effect linear-probability model, whose clustered covariance is estimable in the full design.

\section{Conclusion}

The full attendance profile shows more structure than a simple user/non-user comparison, but not a monotone dose response. The largest and most robust difference is between zero attendance and any recorded CMAT use. Within positive attendance, the first one to three visits are difficult to interpret as purely voluntary because three visits could satisfy a PPA participation activity, and the data show no abrupt outcome change at that threshold.

Beyond the PPA-compatible range, the descriptive profile becomes more heterogeneous. Mean standardised performance rises from four to five visits and again from exact six to 7+, although neither adjacent contrast is statistically resolved. The 7+ group is nevertheless the clearest high-frequency departure: it has the highest descriptive mean and pass rate, and its adjusted pass probability remains significantly higher than the one-visit group after Holm correction. This makes sustained high-frequency CMAT use an empirically distinct profile even though the observational design cannot determine why those students return so often or whether the visits themselves produce the difference.

The distributional analysis adds an important qualification. With imputed administrative outcomes, most user groups are well described by a higher-performance mode and a lower mode enriched in BV and other adverse outcomes; the higher-mode mean is relatively stable, while changes in the lower-component weight coincide with the visible four-to-five and six-to-7+ shifts. With observed numeric grades alone, however, evidence for two components is much less consistent, and the 7+ lower component is essentially one extreme failure. The paper therefore reports both representations rather than treating the imputed mixture as a literal two-type model.

Taken together, the evidence is compatible with the institutional expectation that CMAT participation is associated with more favourable academic trajectories, particularly among the highest-frequency users, but it does not establish a causal effect of attendance. The most defensible interpretation is that zero attendance, PPA-compatible low-frequency attendance and sustained high-frequency use correspond to meaningfully different observed outcome profiles, while selection, institutional incentives and administrative course endings remain integral to their interpretation.
