Our findings extend previous evaluations of mathematics support by showing how the observed relationship between attendance and performance changes when visits are matched to the academic period of the course and grades are compared within classes. The mean classroom-relative grade increased across the attendance groups in the primary specification, but the strongest and most consistent statistical difference was between students who attended CMAT and students who did not. Differences among students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times were much less clearly supported.

This pattern is consistent with viewing mathematics support attendance as one form of academic help-seeking rather than as a simple measure of tutoring intensity. \citet{fong2023} describe help-seeking as a self-regulated and motivated strategy whose relationship with achievement depends on both the form of help sought and student characteristics. A recorded visit to CMAT shows that a student used formal academic support, but the number of visits does not reveal the quality of the interaction or the amount of learning that took place. Repeated attendance may reflect greater need, persistence, confidence in the service, scheduling opportunities, recommendations from others, or combinations of these factors.

The comparison with \citet{jacob2019} is particularly relevant. Their longitudinal TEAMAT study found that students who attended mathematics support once should be distinguished from those who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. Our results also support separating one recorded visit from no attendance. However, although the mean $Z$ scores in the primary specification increase with visit frequency, the pairwise differences among students who attended different numbers of times are not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. The relationship between attendance frequency and performance should therefore be interpreted more cautiously than the broader difference between attendance and non-attendance.

The design choices used here improve comparability but do not remove self-selection. Matching attendance records to the MU academic period avoids attributing visits from other periods to the course result being analysed, and within-class standardisation reduces the influence of differences in grading distributions across classes. Neither step makes students who attend CMAT comparable with non-attenders on unobserved characteristics. Students experiencing greater mathematical difficulty may be more likely to seek support, while students who are more motivated, organised, or willing to use institutional resources may also be more likely to attend. The available administrative data cannot separate these mechanisms.

The sensitivity analysis leads to the same caution. Across the three outcome constructions, students with no recorded visits had lower mean classroom-relative performance than each group of students who attended CMAT. By contrast, the ordering of the attendance groups changed when non-numeric outcomes were handled differently. The evidence is therefore more consistent for an attendance--non-attendance difference than for a stable graded relationship between visit frequency and academic performance.

These findings also have implications for the evaluation of mathematics support using administrative data. Attendance should be matched to the academic period of the course outcome being studied rather than accumulated across unrelated periods. Where grading practices or grade distributions differ across classes, those differences should be considered explicitly rather than assuming that all raw grades are directly comparable. Finally, analyses should distinguish students who do not attend from those who attend and, where sample size permits, report separate attendance-frequency groups rather than imposing a linear effect of number of visits. These practices do not solve self-selection bias, but they make the resulting observational evidence more transparent and comparable across institutions.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations constrain interpretation. First, the available data do not contain a comprehensive measure of prior mathematical attainment for the full analytical cohort, so the analysis cannot fully account for student-level self-selection into CMAT attendance. Second, CMAT records indicate the number of visits but not the duration or content of each visit, and administrative attendance records may contain missing or duplicate entries. Third, attendance and academic performance are observed within the same academic period, so students may seek support in response to difficulties that emerge during the course. Matching visits to the course period improves the relevance of the attendance measure but does not establish a causal ordering between emerging difficulty, attendance, and final performance. Fourth, the treatment of non-numeric adverse outcomes affects the ordering and magnitude of the mean scores among attendance groups, although the broader difference between students with and without recorded attendance is retained. Fifth, institutional requirements and other contextual factors may affect how often students attend, so differences between visit categories should not be interpreted as the effect of an additional visit. Finally, comparisons involving specific attendance frequencies draw on fewer classes than the broader comparison between students who did and did not attend, particularly for the three-visit group. Nevertheless, 176 of the 190 classes contain both attendees and non-attendees, and the adjusted estimates change little when classes with fewer than 20 students are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

Among students taking their first eligible MU attempt, attendance at CMAT during the same academic period was associated with higher classroom-relative academic performance. Mean performance increased across the five attendance groups in the primary specification, but the clearest statistical difference was between students with no recorded visits and students who attended at least once. Differences among students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times were not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons, and the ordering of those groups changed under alternative treatments of non-numeric outcomes. Matching attendance to the course period and comparing grades within classes therefore provides a clearer observational description of the relationship between mathematics support attendance and performance, but it does not identify a causal effect of mathematics support. The findings support careful distinction between attendance and non-attendance while cautioning against interpreting number of visits as a simple measure of treatment intensity.
