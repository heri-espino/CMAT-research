Students who attended CMAT during the MU academic period had higher mean standardised grades than students with no recorded visits. This difference was observed for each of the four attendance-frequency groups. By contrast, students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times did not differ significantly from one another after adjustment for multiple comparisons, and their ordering changed across the sensitivity analyses. Taken together, the results show a stable difference between attendance and non-attendance, but not a consistent increase in performance with more frequent attendance.

Attendance at mathematics support can be understood as one form of academic help-seeking. \citet{fong2023} describe help-seeking as a self-regulated and motivated strategy whose relationship with achievement depends on both the form of help sought and student characteristics. In the present data, a recorded CMAT visit shows that a student used formal academic support, but it does not indicate the amount or quality of support received. Repeated attendance may reflect continuing need, persistence, confidence in the service, scheduling opportunities, recommendations from others, or combinations of these factors.

The comparison with \citet{jacob2019} is particularly relevant. Their longitudinal TEAMAT study found that students who attended mathematics support once should be distinguished from those who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. Our results also distinguish one recorded visit from no attendance, but they provide less evidence that higher attendance frequencies correspond to progressively better performance. The difference between the two studies may reflect the institutional setting, the way attendance is grouped, the standardisation of grades within instructor--period groups, or unobserved differences between students who attend at different frequencies.

Matching attendance records to the MU academic period and standardising grades within instructor--period groups improve the comparability of the observed data, but they do not remove self-selection. Attendance was not randomly assigned and may reflect both student choice and institutional incentives. Students experiencing greater mathematical difficulty may be more likely to seek support, while students who are more motivated, organised, or willing to use institutional resources may also be more likely to attend. The available administrative data cannot separate these possibilities.

For mathematics support centres using routine attendance data for evaluation, three practical considerations follow from the analysis. Attendance should be matched to the academic period of the course outcome being studied rather than accumulated across unrelated periods. Differences in grading practices or grade distributions across teaching contexts should be considered rather than assuming that all raw grades are directly comparable. Finally, where sample size permits, students with no attendance should be distinguished from students with different frequencies of attendance before assuming a linear relationship between number of visits and performance. These steps do not remove self-selection bias, but they make observational comparisons easier to interpret.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations should be considered. First, the available data do not contain a comprehensive measure of prior mathematical attainment for the full study sample, so differences between students who attend CMAT and those who do not may reflect unobserved differences present before or during the course. Second, the CMAT records indicate the number of visits but not the duration or content of each visit. Third, attendance and academic performance are observed within the same academic period. Students may begin attending CMAT in response to difficulties that emerge during the course, and the data do not establish the timing of those difficulties relative to the support received. Fourth, how non-numeric course outcomes are represented in the standardised grade affects the ordering and magnitude of the mean scores among attendance groups, although the broader difference between students with and without recorded attendance remains. Fifth, institutional requirements and other contextual factors may influence attendance frequency, so differences between visit categories should not be interpreted as the effect of an additional visit. Finally, comparisons involving specific attendance frequencies draw on fewer instructor--period groups than the broader comparison between students who did and did not attend, particularly for the three-visit group. Nevertheless, 176 of the 190 instructor--period groups contain both students who attended CMAT and students with no recorded visits, and the adjusted estimates change little when instructor--period groups with fewer than 20 students are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

For students' first MU attempts included in the study, CMAT attendance during the same academic period was associated with higher standardised grades. Mean performance increased across the five attendance groups, but the clearest difference was between students with no recorded visits and students who attended at least once. Differences among students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times were not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons, and the ordering of those groups changed when non-numeric course outcomes were handled differently. Matching attendance to the course period and standardising grades within instructor--period groups provides a more interpretable observational comparison, but it does not identify a causal effect of mathematics support. Accordingly, the number of visits is not interpreted as a direct measure of the amount of support received.
