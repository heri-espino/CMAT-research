This study examined whether the commonly reported relationship between mathematics-support attendance and academic performance remains when attendance is matched to the academic period of the course and grades are compared within classes. The mean standardised grade increased across the attendance groups in the primary analysis, but the strongest and most consistent difference was between students who attended CMAT and students who did not. Differences among students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times were much less clearly supported.

This pattern is consistent with interpreting attendance at mathematics support as one form of academic help-seeking rather than as a direct measure of the amount of tutoring received. \citet{fong2023} describe help-seeking as a self-regulated and motivated strategy whose relationship with achievement depends on both the form of help sought and student characteristics. A recorded CMAT visit shows that a student used formal academic support, but it does not show how much support was received or how useful the interaction was. Repeated attendance may reflect greater need, persistence, confidence in the service, scheduling opportunities, recommendations from others, or combinations of these factors.

The comparison with \citet{jacob2019} is particularly relevant. Their longitudinal TEAMAT study found that students who attended mathematics support once should be distinguished from those who never attended and reported more favourable outcomes at higher levels of attendance. Our results similarly show a clear difference between one recorded visit and no attendance. However, although mean standardised grades increase with visit frequency in the primary analysis, differences among students who attended different numbers of times are not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Our findings therefore provide stronger evidence for a difference between attendance and non-attendance than for a steadily increasing relationship between attendance frequency and academic performance.

The study design improves the comparability of the observed groups but does not remove self-selection. Matching attendance records to the MU academic period avoids assigning visits from other periods to the course result being analysed, and standardising grades within class reduces the influence of differences in grade distributions across classes. Attendance, however, was not randomly assigned and may reflect both student choice and institutional incentives. Students experiencing greater mathematical difficulty may be more likely to seek support, while students who are more motivated, organised, or willing to use institutional resources may also be more likely to attend. The available administrative data cannot separate these possibilities.

The sensitivity analyses reinforce the same caution. Under each treatment of non-numeric outcomes, students with no recorded visits had lower mean standardised grades than each group of students who attended CMAT. By contrast, the ordering of the attendance-frequency groups changed across analyses. The evidence is therefore more consistent for a difference between students who attended and those who did not than for a stable increase in performance with each higher level of attendance.

The findings also suggest several points for evaluating mathematics support using administrative data. Attendance records should be matched to the academic period of the course outcome being studied rather than accumulated across unrelated periods. Where grading practices or grade distributions differ across classes, those differences should be considered explicitly rather than assuming that all raw grades are directly comparable. Analyses should also distinguish students who do not attend from those who attend and, where sample size permits, report separate attendance-frequency groups before assuming a linear relationship between number of visits and performance. These steps do not remove self-selection bias, but they make the resulting comparisons easier to interpret and to compare across institutions.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations should be considered. First, the available data do not contain a comprehensive measure of prior mathematical attainment for the full study sample, so differences between students who attend CMAT and those who do not may reflect unobserved differences present before or during the course. Second, the CMAT records indicate the number of visits but not the duration or content of each visit, and administrative attendance records may contain missing or duplicate entries. Third, attendance and academic performance are observed within the same academic period. Students may therefore begin attending CMAT in response to difficulties that emerge during the course, and the data do not establish the timing of those difficulties relative to the support received. Fourth, how non-numeric adverse outcomes are represented in the standardised grade affects the ordering and magnitude of the mean scores among attendance groups, although the broader difference between students with and without recorded attendance remains. Fifth, institutional requirements and other contextual factors may influence attendance frequency, so differences between visit categories should not be interpreted as the effect of an additional visit. Finally, comparisons involving specific attendance frequencies draw on fewer classes than the broader comparison between students who did and did not attend, particularly for the three-visit group. Nevertheless, 176 of the 190 classes contain both students who attended CMAT and students with no recorded visits, and the adjusted estimates change little when classes with fewer than 20 students are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

Among students taking their first MU attempt included in the study, attendance at CMAT during the same academic period was associated with higher within-class standardised grades. Mean performance increased across the five attendance groups in the primary analysis, but the clearest statistical difference was between students with no recorded visits and students who attended at least once. Differences among students who attended one, two, three, or at least four times were not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons, and the ordering of those groups changed when non-numeric course outcomes were handled differently. Matching attendance to the course period and comparing grades within classes therefore provides a clearer description of the relationship between mathematics support attendance and academic performance, but it does not identify a causal effect of mathematics support. In particular, the number of visits should not be interpreted as a direct measure of the amount of support received.
