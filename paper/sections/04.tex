\section{Discussion}

The principal empirical pattern is a separation between students with no recorded CMAT attendance and those who entered the support system at least once, rather than a regular progression across successive visit counts. In the main eight-group analysis, adjusted standardised performance was higher at every positive attendance frequency than at zero attendance, and all seven zero-versus-positive continuous contrasts survived Holm correction across the full 28-comparison family. Within the positive-frequency groups, however, no continuous pairwise contrast survived that correction. The PASS effect-size display tells a similar descriptive story: the fixed-effect logistic odds-ratio point estimates were above one for every positive group relative to zero, but the sequence from one through 7+ visits was neither monotone nor suggestive of a simple dose-response relationship.

This distinction between initial entry and repeated use is conceptually consistent with recent work that separates whether students engage with mathematics and statistics support from how often they return after engaging \citep{gokhool2026}. It is also consistent with the wider observational mathematics-support literature, in which users often have more favourable outcomes than non-users even though attendance intensity is difficult to interpret causally \citep{macanbhaird2009,rickard2018,jacob2019}. The present results add a useful qualification: a large user/non-user difference can coexist with small, irregular and multiplicity-sensitive differences among users at neighbouring attendance frequencies.

The institutional context is central to that interpretation. During at least part of the study period, three CMAT visits could satisfy a first-semester PPA participation activity. A third visit could therefore reflect voluntary help-seeking, completion of an institutional requirement, or both. The observed data do not show an abrupt break at that point: the adjusted two-versus-three continuous contrast was close to zero, the observed pass rates differed by only one percentage point, and the logistic odds-ratio point estimate was essentially one. This is not a causal estimate of the PPA incentive because assignment to the requirement and the reasons students completed it are not observed as exogenous variables. It does, however, argue against interpreting three visits as an observed performance threshold.

Institutional expectations also matter for evaluation practice. A university operates a mathematics support centre because it expects the service to improve students' opportunities to succeed, so a favourable association between CMAT use and outcomes is not institutionally neutral. That expectation is legitimate as programme context, but it should not determine exposure definitions, significance thresholds or the interpretation of ambiguous findings. The analysis therefore distinguishes observed evidence from institutional expectation: the zero-versus-positive association is strong across several outcome representations, while the data do not justify a claim that each additional visit produces an incremental improvement.

The outcome-composition results further show why support-centre evaluation should not be reduced to a single grade measure. CMAT users did not have a higher overall prevalence of administrative outcomes; instead, among students who did not pass, users were substantially less likely to finish with a completed numeric grade below 7.5 and correspondingly more likely to have an administrative outcome. The adjusted contrast was concentrated in BV rather than RT, while BA was rare among users. This pattern changes the meaning of the lower tail because a continuous outcome that assigns latent numerical values to BV, RT and BA necessarily translates differences in administrative composition into differences in the completed grade distribution.

One possible interpretation is that CMAT attendance marks broader academic engagement or institutional navigation. Students who seek support may also differ in how closely they monitor progress, whether they seek advice or how they use available administrative procedures when a course is going poorly. The records cannot test that mechanism directly. They contain neither students' reasons for withdrawing nor their knowledge of institutional rules, motivation, concern for grade-point average or advice received. The BV association should therefore be read as evidence about outcome composition rather than as proof that CMAT attendance causes strategic withdrawal or that users are intrinsically more engaged.

The complete-case and imputed distributional analyses clarify how strongly administrative outcomes influence apparent mixture structure. Among observed numeric grades, a two-Gaussian representation was supported consistently for zero, one and two visits, whereas a single skew-normal was adequate for several higher-frequency groups and the five-visit group was inconclusive. Resolving the upper tail did not reveal a clean latent transition. Exact six visits were adequately represented by a skew-normal in the complete-case analysis, while the 7+ two-Gaussian fit was driven by an extremely small lower component with its variance at the numerical floor, making a substantive two-population interpretation implausible.

The imputed outcome produced stronger mixture evidence because administrative outcomes populate the lower part of the completed distribution. Groups 0--5 showed substantially stronger support for the two-Gaussian fit than in the complete-case analysis, while exact six remained specification-sensitive and 7+ showed more coherent mixture evidence. The corresponding lower-component weights were highly non-monotone across attendance frequency. This combination of results is more informative than selecting a single preferred mixture model: distributional complexity is real in parts of the data, but the fitted components do not trace a stable dose-like mechanism and should not be treated as latent student classes.

The numeric-failure robustness analysis points in the same direction. When BV, RT and BA were excluded entirely and failure was defined only from observed numeric grades, zero-attendance students still had a much higher failure rate than CMAT users. Among users, however, the pooled-tail model did not show a significant frequency gradient. The broad attendance association is therefore not mechanically created by imputing administrative outcomes, even though imputation materially changes the shape of the lower tail.

A secondary covariance sensitivity also cautions against over-interpreting a null frequency pattern. Re-clustering the earlier positive-attendance model by instructor produced a more suggestive continuous-outcome omnibus result and one non-adjacent PASS contrast after Holm correction. Because this sensitivity uses fewer effective clusters and the earlier pooled 6+ tail, it is not directly interchangeable with the main eight-group analysis. Its role is to show that some broader low-versus-high separation remains plausible even though adjacent visit counts do not form a stable staircase.

\subsection{Implications for mathematics-support evaluation}

Four implications follow. First, evaluations should separate initial engagement from subsequent frequency whenever the data allow, because a large zero-versus-positive difference does not imply that each additional visit corresponds to a distinct academic outcome. Second, frequency categories should be chosen and reported with their support structure visible; resolving a sparse upper tail may be useful descriptively even when pooled categories remain preferable for some robustness analyses. Third, institutional incentives attached to particular visit counts should be incorporated explicitly into interpretation, because they can change why students accumulate visits even if they do not produce a visible outcome discontinuity. Fourth, administrative final states should not automatically be collapsed into a generic failure category when they represent substantively different academic trajectories.

These recommendations improve the target of observational evaluation but do not solve self-selection. The present results answer what differs across recorded patterns of CMAT use, not what would happen if otherwise identical students were assigned additional visits.

\section{Limitations}

The central limitation is unmeasured selection. The available administrative data do not provide a comprehensive pre-enrolment measure of mathematical preparation for the full cohort, so differences between attendance groups may partly reflect characteristics present before MU. Adjustment for a well-covered preparation measure would reduce one source of confounding but would still not measure motivation, institutional knowledge, advice received or time-varying academic difficulty, and it would not establish causality.

Attendance and outcomes are observed within the same academic period, so reverse timing is also possible: students may begin attending because difficulties emerge during the course. The records count entries to CMAT but do not measure visit duration, content, quality or the identity of the staff member who provided support. A count of visits is therefore a coarse exposure measure.

The PPA requirement introduces an additional source of heterogeneity in visit meaning. The analytic records do not contain a period-by-period indicator identifying which students were subject to the requirement, whether a particular visit was made to satisfy it or whether a student would have attended anyway. The three-visit threshold cannot therefore be treated as an exogenous discontinuity, instrumental variable or natural experiment in the present study.

Administrative outcomes require separate caution. Exact withdrawal dates are not available, so students who leave earlier may have had less opportunity to accumulate visits. The reasons for BV, RT and BA are also not recorded. Although the observed composition difference is concentrated in BV, the data cannot establish that students selected BV to protect an academic average, that they understood the procedure because of greater engagement or that CMAT attendance influenced the decision. Any statement about the formal consequences of these codes should be tied to verified institutional rules applicable during the study period.

The primary continuous outcome requires numerical representation of BV, RT and BA. The within-instructor-period imputation preserves their adverse status while avoiding a fixed arbitrary score, but the latent grades are not observed and imputation uncertainty is not propagated through multiple imputation. The numeric complete-case analysis avoids that assumption but conditions on receiving a numeric final grade. The contrast between the two analyses is therefore a sensitivity analysis rather than a method for recovering a unique latent performance value.

The upper-frequency groups are small, especially exact six visits ($N=50$ overall; 43 numeric complete cases) and 7+ visits ($N=101$ overall; 93 numeric complete cases). Their instructor--period overlap is weaker than in lower-frequency groups, and the complete-case 7+ mixture contains an effectively degenerate lower component. These groups are useful for showing the shape of the observed upper tail but should not be assigned the same inferential stability as the large zero- and one-visit groups.

Finally, the full eight-group fixed-effect logistic model produced interpretable odds-ratio point estimates but a rank-deficient instructor--period-clustered covariance matrix. We therefore do not use its standard errors or $p$-values. Multiplicity-aware PASS inference is instead based on the fixed-effect linear-probability model, whose clustered covariance is estimable in the full design. This separation between an odds-ratio effect-size display and linear-probability inference should be kept in mind when comparing the binary panels.

\section{Conclusion}

Across 6,627 first eligible MU attempts, the dominant observational pattern was a separation between zero recorded CMAT attendance and positive attendance. Students with at least one visit had higher adjusted standardised performance and a higher probability of passing, and the same broad pattern remained when administrative outcomes were removed and failure was defined only from observed numeric grades. In the main 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ continuous-outcome analysis, every zero-versus-positive pairwise contrast survived Holm adjustment, whereas no positive-versus-positive contrast did.

The data do not show a regular visit-by-visit staircase, nor do they show an abrupt outcome break at the institutionally incentivised third visit. The upper-frequency tail contains some favourable descriptive outcomes, particularly at 7+ visits, but it is sparse and its distributional models are unstable enough to preclude a simple dose interpretation. Administrative outcome composition also matters: among non-passing students, CMAT users were less likely to end with a numeric failure and more likely to have an administrative outcome, with the adjusted difference concentrated in voluntary withdrawal.

These findings are compatible with the institutional expectation that mathematics support should be associated with more favourable student trajectories, but they do not establish that CMAT attendance caused the observed differences. Attendance is selected, partly institutionally incentivised and intertwined with academic need and administrative pathways. The most defensible interpretation is therefore that entering the CMAT support system marks a strong observational difference in academic outcomes, whereas the number of subsequent visits carries weaker, irregular and context-dependent information.
