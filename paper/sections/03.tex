We then estimate ordinary least-squares models with classroom fixed effects, degree-programme indicators, and standard errors clustered by classroom. The principal model includes indicators for 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits, with zero visits as the reference category. We report all ten pre-specified pairwise group contrasts and apply a Holm adjustment across those comparisons. The coefficients estimate conditional within-classroom associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects of receiving tutoring. The historical 1--2 pooling and upper-tail binary contrasts are retained only as secondary sensitivities and provenance checks.

Propensity-score methods can improve balance on observed pre-exposure characteristics when an appropriate covariate set is available \citep{rosenbaum1983,austin2011}. We do not present observed-covariate weighting as a solution to the current identification problem because the available administrative extract lacks a sufficiently broad and uniform pre-enrolment measure of baseline mathematics preparation. The primary interpretation therefore remains associational throughout.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Observed CMAT use in first-attempt MU}

Among the 6,627 students in the primary cohort, 5,393 recorded no CMAT registration during the focal MU period, while 1,234 (18.6\%) recorded at least one. Exact positive-use counts were 517 students with one visit, 245 with two, 170 with three, and 302 with four or more. \Cref{fig:visits} shows the full observed distribution, which is strongly concentrated at zero and has a comparatively small positive-use tail.

\subsection{Classroom-relative performance by support-use group}

\Cref{tab:means} and \Cref{fig:means} show a clear separation between students with no recorded CMAT use and each positive-use category. Mean classroom-relative performance was $-0.061$ for non-users and $0.219$, $0.247$, $0.278$, and $0.355$ for students with 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more visits, respectively. The descriptive means are ordered upward across the exact categories, but the uncertainty around the smaller positive-use groups is substantial enough that pairwise inference is important before interpreting this pattern as a dose-response.

The Brown--Forsythe test rejected homogeneity of variance ($F=15.88$, $p<0.001$), supporting use of the heteroskedastic Welch procedure. Welch's omnibus test also rejected equality of the five group means, $F(4,594.83)=43.46$, $p<0.001$. The pairwise pattern is more informative than the omnibus rejection. Games--Howell comparisons separated zero visits from each positive-use group, with adjusted $p\leq0.000002$ in all four comparisons. By contrast, none of the six comparisons among positive-use groups was statistically distinguishable at the 5\% level: adjusted $p$-values ranged from $0.090$ for one versus four-or-more visits to $0.994$ for two versus three visits.

The substantive result is therefore not simply that the sample means rise with visit count. The stable inferential separation is between no recorded use and positive formal help-seeking, while the data provide much weaker evidence that the positive-use levels represent distinct performance regimes. This distinction is important because a monotone sequence of point estimates does not by itself establish a graded tutoring dose-response.

\subsection{Within-classroom associations}

Classroom fixed-effect models with degree-programme adjustment retain the same qualitative pattern. Relative to students with zero visits in the same classroom environment, the estimated contrasts are $0.305$ standard deviations for one visit, $0.359$ for two visits, $0.373$ for three visits, and $0.461$ for four or more visits (\Cref{fig:dose}). The corresponding 95\% confidence intervals are $[0.221,0.388]$, $[0.262,0.457]$, $[0.248,0.498]$, and $[0.350,0.572]$, respectively. All four contrasts remain statistically distinguishable from zero after the pre-specified Holm correction across ten pairwise comparisons.

The adjusted positive-use groups are again much less clearly separated from one another. None of the six positive-positive contrasts survives the Holm correction. The largest adjusted difference is between one visit and four-or-more visits ($0.156Z$ in favour of the latter); its unadjusted $p$-value is $0.025$, but the Holm-adjusted value is $0.153$. Thus, even after restricting the comparison to a common classroom context and adjusting for degree programme, the evidence is considerably stronger for a broad positive-use versus non-use distinction than for sharply separated levels of positive use.

\subsection{Sensitivity to outcome construction}

The broad pattern is not dependent on a single treatment of adverse non-numeric course outcomes. \Cref{fig:sensitivity} compares exact visit-group means under the primary adverse-outcome rule, a uniform adverse-imputation sensitivity, and a numeric complete-case specification. Under all three constructions, the zero-visit group has a negative mean classroom-relative score and each positive-use group has a positive mean. The ordering among positive-use categories is less stable: the primary construction is monotonically increasing, whereas the uniform-imputation and complete-case specifications show local reversals. In the numeric complete-case sample, for example, the means for 1, 2, 3, and 4+ visits are approximately $0.256$, $0.193$, $0.326$, and $0.200$, respectively. This sensitivity strengthens the interpretation that the robust feature is the separation of non-use from observed formal help-seeking, not a precise dose gradient among users.
