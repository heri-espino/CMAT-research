\section{Results}

\subsection{Attendance distribution and observed outcomes}

The study sample contained 6,627 first eligible MU attempts. Of these, 5,393 students had no recorded CMAT visit during the corresponding academic period and 1,234 (18.6\%) attended at least once. Under the main reporting grid, the positive-attendance groups contained 517 students with one visit, 245 with two, 170 with three, 96 with four, 55 with five, 50 with exactly six and 101 with seven or more visits.

The broad zero-versus-any-attendance benchmark remained pronounced after adjustment for instructor--period fixed effects and degree programme. One or more visits were associated with a $0.361$ standard-deviation higher continuous outcome (95\% CI $0.302$ to $0.421$, $p<0.001$) and a 15.3 percentage-point higher probability of passing (95\% CI 12.4 to 18.1 percentage points, $p<0.001$). The latter contrast does not depend on assigning latent numeric values to BV, RT or BA because those outcomes enter PASS directly as non-passing records.

The descriptive pattern is dominated by the distinction between zero attendance and positive attendance rather than by a monotone sequence across positive visit counts. The zero-visit group passed at 72.1\%, compared with 80.8--89.1\% across the positive-frequency groups. Numeric grades below 7.5 accounted for 10.8\% of the zero-visit group but only 1.0--4.5\% of the positive-frequency groups. The administrative states did not move together: BV remained visible among users, whereas RT and BA were much less common at positive attendance frequencies.

\subsection{Adjusted pairwise comparisons across 0, exact 1--6 and 7+ visits}

The eight-group continuous-outcome model rejected equality across attendance frequencies ($p<0.001$). The pairwise matrix makes clear that this omnibus result is driven primarily by the zero-attendance benchmark. Adjusted standardised performance was higher in every positive-attendance group than in the zero group, with row-minus-zero differences ranging from 0.305 SD for one visit to 0.549 SD for 7+ visits. After Holm correction across all 28 pairwise comparisons, every zero-versus-positive contrast remained significant; the weakest of these was the exact-six-versus-zero contrast, with Holm-adjusted $p=0.042$. By contrast, no positive-versus-positive continuous contrast survived Holm correction. Before adjustment, the most visible positive-frequency contrasts involved five or 7+ visits---for example, one versus 7+ had $p=0.0077$ and one versus five had $p=0.0297$---but their Holm-adjusted values were 0.162 and 0.593, respectively.

\Cref{fig:pairwise-effect-dashboard} combines continuous and binary effect-size displays in the same eight-group frame. The left panel reports adjusted standardised-grade differences. The right panel reports fixed-effect logistic odds-ratio point estimates for PASS; because the cluster-robust logistic covariance was rank-deficient in the full fixed-effect model, these odds ratios are used for effect-size interpretation rather than significance testing. Relative to zero attendance, the estimated pass odds were higher at every positive frequency, from an odds ratio of 2.20 for one visit to 6.05 for 7+ visits. The estimated odds ratio for two versus three visits was 1.01, providing no indication of an abrupt binary-outcome change exactly at the three-visit PPA threshold.

The PPA threshold itself does not coincide with a visible discontinuity in the continuous outcome. The adjusted two-versus-three difference was only $-0.013$ SD, with raw $p=0.843$; descriptively, the pass rates were 80.8\% and 81.8\%, and the logistic odds-ratio point estimate for two versus three visits was 1.01. These comparisons do not estimate the causal effect of the PPA incentive because reaching three visits is not exogenous, but they argue against interpreting the incentivised third visit as an observed performance threshold in these data.

\subsection{Composition of non-passing outcomes}

The binary PASS outcome conceals substantial differences in how a non-passing attempt ends. Among students with zero visits, 10.8\% received a numeric grade below 7.5, 9.8\% had BV, 6.1\% had RT and 1.2\% had BA. Across positive-frequency groups, completed numeric failure was markedly less common, whereas BV remained visible: for example, BV represented 14.3\%, 13.1\%, 14.1\%, 10.4\%, 9.1\%, 14.0\% and 6.9\% of the one-, two-, three-, four-, five-, six- and 7+-visit groups, respectively. RT and BA were rare at the higher positive frequencies.

Aggregating positive attendance, 82.4\% of CMAT users passed and 3.2\% received a numeric grade below 7.5, compared with 72.1\% and 10.8\% among students with no visits. Conditional on non-PASS, 923 of 1,504 zero-visit cases (61.4\%) ended administratively and 581 (38.6\%) ended with a numeric grade below 7.5; among 217 non-passing CMAT users, 178 (82.0\%) ended administratively and 39 (18.0\%) had numeric failure. After adjustment, any attendance was associated with a 13.2 percentage-point higher probability that a non-passing outcome was administrative rather than numeric (95\% CI 6.8 to 19.6 percentage points, $p<0.001$).

The administrative codes did not share a common pattern. The broad adjusted contrast was concentrated in BV: among cases restricted to BV or numeric failure, any CMAT attendance was associated with a 16.8 percentage-point higher probability of BV rather than numeric failure (95\% CI 9.5 to 24.1 percentage points, $p<0.001$). By contrast, the adjusted RT-versus-numeric-failure difference was 0.5 percentage points (95\% CI $-15.1$ to 16.2 percentage points, $p=0.945$), and BA was uncommon among users. These results describe outcome composition; they do not establish that attendance caused withdrawal or that students used BV for any particular strategic reason.

\subsection{Numeric-failure robustness and covariance sensitivity}

The broad zero-versus-positive pattern persisted when administrative outcomes were removed entirely and the endpoint was restricted to observed numeric final grades. Under the pooled-tail robustness grouping, the observed numeric-failure rate was 13.0\% at zero visits and 3.5\%, 5.3\%, 3.5\%, 1.2\%, 4.1\% and 3.7\% for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits, respectively. The zero-inclusive fixed-effect logistic model strongly rejected equal failure odds ($p<0.001$). Among users alone, however, the joint test did not reject equal adjusted log odds across positive frequencies ($p=0.480$). Thus the lower prevalence of completed numeric failure among users is not an artefact of numerically representing BV, RT or BA, while the repeated-attendance pattern remains irregular.

Re-clustering the earlier positive-attendance specification by instructor, while retaining instructor--period fixed effects and standardisation, left the zero-versus-positive benchmark nearly unchanged: any attendance corresponded to 0.361 SD higher performance and 15.3 percentage points higher pass probability. The positive-frequency omnibus tests became more suggestive ($p=0.0477$ for the continuous outcome and $p=0.0551$ for PASS), and one non-adjacent 1-versus-6+ PASS contrast survived Holm adjustment under instructor clustering. No adjacent contrast did. This sensitivity weakens any claim that all positive-frequency groups are interchangeable, but it still does not produce a visit-by-visit staircase.

\subsection{Distribution-shape analysis}

The complete-case distributional analysis asks whether departures from a single Gaussian are better represented by a two-Gaussian mixture or by a single strongly skewed distribution. For zero, one and two visits, BIC, the skew-normal-null bootstrap and repeated held-out log predictive density gave concordant support for the two-Gaussian representation. For three and four visits, a single skew-normal was adequate. The five-visit group was inconclusive because of its small complete-case sample ($n=49$) and unstable predictive evidence.

The newly resolved upper tail required separate interpretation. For exact six visits ($n=43$ complete cases), the skew-normal had lower BIC than the two-Gaussian model ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=-2.36$), the skew-normal-null bootstrap did not reject that explanation ($p=0.11$), and mean held-out prediction slightly favoured the skew-normal. For 7+ visits ($n=93$), BIC ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=+7.34$) and the bootstrap ($p=0.02$) favoured the two-Gaussian fit, but the estimated lower component had only 1.1\% weight, mean $Z=-2.86$ and an estimated standard deviation at the numerical floor. The mean held-out log predictive density favoured the GMM, yet it won only 38\% of fold evaluations. The 7+ complete-case mixture is therefore treated as an unstable tail fit dominated by an extreme observation rather than as evidence for a stable latent subgroup.

The imputed outcome showed stronger mixture structure overall, but the exact-six split introduces an important qualification. Groups 0--5 all showed bootstrap support for the two-Gaussian model relative to a fitted skew-normal. For exact six visits, BIC slightly favoured the skew-normal ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=-2.17$) and the skew-normal-null bootstrap was non-significant ($p=0.12$), whereas cross-validation favoured the GMM on average; this group is therefore specification-sensitive. For 7+ visits, the evidence was more coherent ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=+4.56$, skew-normal-null bootstrap $p=0.02$, positive mean held-out GMM-minus-skew log-density difference).

The estimated lower-component weights illustrate why the mixture should not be interpreted as a dose-response mechanism. In numeric complete cases, the weights for 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7+ visits were approximately 21.4\%, 15.7\%, 13.0\%, 44.4\%, 25.4\%, 4.1\%, 57.8\% and 1.1\%, respectively. On the imputed outcome they were 35.5\%, 25.2\%, 21.4\%, 21.2\%, 35.7\%, 9.1\%, 25.2\% and 14.3\%. The extreme complete-case weights at six and 7+ are accompanied by the shape-instability diagnostics above, while the imputed sequence is also clearly non-monotone.

The comparison between complete-case and imputed distributions shows that administrative-outcome representation materially sharpens the lower tail without creating the entire pattern. Mixture evidence is already present in observed numeric grades for zero, one and two visits, whereas the imputed outcome produces stronger lower-component structure across more groups. Posterior-responsibility summaries from the pooled-tail robustness analysis further show that the imputed lower component is enriched in administrative outcomes, especially BV, while the higher component is overwhelmingly composed of passing records. The fitted components are therefore best understood as flexible summaries of outcome-density structure, not as literal student types.

Taken together, the results show a robust descriptive separation between zero attendance and positive CMAT use, much weaker and less regular differences among positive attendance frequencies, no visible discontinuity at the institutionally incentivised third visit, and substantial sensitivity of lower-tail distributional shape to administrative outcomes and sparse upper-tail observations.
