\section{Results}

\subsection{Attendance and the zero-versus-any-attendance benchmark}

Among the 6,627 students in the study sample, 5,393 had no recorded CMAT visit during the MU academic period and 1,234 (18.6\%) attended at least once. Among users, 517 made one visit, 245 made two, 170 made three, 96 made four, 55 made five and 151 made six or more visits under the pre-specified primary grouping. For the descriptive distribution displays below, the upper tail is additionally resolved into 50 students with exactly six visits and 101 with seven or more visits, while the inferential primary grouping remains 6+.

Students with any recorded attendance had more favourable final outcomes than students with no recorded visits. In the adjusted benchmark model, one or more visits were associated with a $0.361$ standard-deviation higher continuous outcome (95\% CI $0.302$ to $0.421$, $p<0.001$) and a 15.3 percentage-point higher probability of passing (95\% CI 12.4 to 18.1 percentage points, $p<0.001$). The pass-probability result is independent of the numerical values assigned to BV, RT and BA because all three are coded directly as non-passing outcomes.

\Cref{fig:observed-pre-imputation} shows the observed outcome structure before any numerical representation of administrative records, using the zero-inclusive sequence 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7+ visits. For each attendance group, the left panel reports voluntary withdrawal (BV), temporary withdrawal (RT) and academic withdrawal (BA) as percentages of the full group, while the right panel shows an unsmoothed histogram of observed numeric final grades on the original 0--10 scale, using 101 bins centred on 0.0, 0.1, \ldots, 9.9 and 10.0. Each histogram bin is normalised by the size of the full attendance group rather than by the numeric-grade subset, and the colours separate grades below and above the institutional pass mark of 7.5. The annotations report the total shares of the full group represented by numeric grades below 7.5 and by numeric passing grades. The five displayed outcome shares therefore partition each attendance group without assigning a numerical value to BV, RT or BA.

After adjustment for instructor--period fixed effects and degree programme, the joint null of equal outcomes across the six positive-attendance groups was not rejected for either standardised performance ($p=0.317$) or pass probability ($p=0.152$). None of the 15 pairwise comparisons among the six groups survived Holm adjustment for either outcome. For illustration, the one-versus-6+ contrast in the continuous outcome had an unadjusted $p$-value of $0.022$ but a Holm-adjusted value of $0.328$; the corresponding pass-probability contrast had an unadjusted $p$-value of $0.011$ and a Holm-adjusted value of $0.171$.

\Cref{fig:z-heatmap,fig:pass-heatmap} display the full adjusted pairwise effect matrices. The figures emphasise effect magnitude and direction rather than statistical significance. The estimates are compatible with some descriptive separation between lower and higher attendance frequencies, but the uncertainty and multiplicity-adjusted results do not support a simple staircase in which each additional visit defines a distinct outcome group. Supplementary zero-inclusive versions are provided in \Cref{app:zero-inclusive-benchmark}; because the zero-versus-positive contrast is much larger than most within-user contrasts, those versions are useful as a benchmark but would visually compress the frequency differences that are the focus of the main heatmaps.

The exploratory specification that separated exact six visits from a 7+ tail did not materially alter the multiplicity-adjusted conclusion. The omnibus $p$-values were $0.213$ for standardised performance and $0.102$ for pass probability, and no positive-frequency pairwise contrast survived Holm adjustment. Before multiplicity correction, however, the local evidence was concentrated in comparisons with the 7+ group: for standardised performance, the raw $p$-values for 1 versus 7+, 2 versus 7+ and 3 versus 7+ were $0.0048$, $0.0233$ and $0.0241$, respectively; for pass probability the corresponding values were $0.0025$, $0.0194$ and $0.0076$. After Holm correction these became $0.102$, $0.467$ and $0.467$ for performance and $0.052$, $0.368$ and $0.153$ for passing. The raw matrices therefore identify a possible broad separation between the highest-frequency tail and some lower-frequency users, but not a family-wise significant set of pairwise differences.

The three-visit institutional participation incentive provides an important reference point for this sensitivity analysis. If the incentive created a sharp outcome discontinuity at the threshold, the adjacent two-versus-three contrast would be a natural place to see it; instead, the raw $p$-values were $0.849$ for standardised performance and $0.352$ for pass probability. This is not a causal test of the PPA requirement, because reaching three visits is not exogenous and the historical implementation of the requirement is not modelled, but it does show that the observed adjusted outcomes do not exhibit an abrupt empirical break exactly at the incentivised third visit. Descriptively, exact six visits had mean $Z=0.288$ and an 82.0\% pass rate, whereas 7+ visits had mean $Z=0.457$ and an 89.1\% pass rate. The combination of sparse overlap and tail instability supports retaining 6+ as the primary inferential category while showing the 7+ tail separately in descriptive and sensitivity displays.

\subsection{Composition of final academic outcomes}

The binary pass outcome concealed substantial differences in the type of non-passing result. \Cref{fig:observed-pre-imputation} shows the exact PASS, numeric-below-7.5, BV, RT and BA composition before imputation, keeping the administrative records categorical and the numeric grades on their observed 0--10 scale. \Cref{fig:distribution-composition} complements that description by showing where those same observed outcome states contribute after the primary imputation and instructor--period standardisation used to construct the continuous performance outcome.

Aggregating the positive-attendance groups, 72.1\% of students with zero visits passed, 10.8\% received a numeric grade below 7.5, 15.9\% had BV or RT and 1.2\% had BA. Among students with at least one visit, 82.4\% passed, 3.2\% received a numeric grade below 7.5, 14.3\% had BV or RT and 0.2\% had BA. Students who attended CMAT therefore did not have a higher overall rate of administrative outcomes; the more marked difference was the lower prevalence of completed numeric failure.

Conditional on non-PASS, this change in composition was substantial. Among 1,504 non-passing students with zero visits, 923 (61.4\%) had an administrative outcome and 581 (38.6\%) had a numeric grade below 7.5. Among 217 non-passing CMAT users, 178 (82.0\%) had an administrative outcome and 39 (18.0\%) had a numeric grade below 7.5. After adjustment, any attendance was associated with a 13.2 percentage-point higher probability that a non-passing outcome was administrative rather than numeric (95\% CI 6.8 to 19.6 percentage points, $p<0.001$).

The exact administrative codes did not show a common pattern. BV represented approximately 9.8\% of all zero-visit outcomes and 12.9\% of all positive-attendance outcomes, whereas RT declined from approximately 6.1\% to 1.4\% and BA from approximately 1.2\% to 0.2\%. Among non-passing cases restricted to BV or numeric failure, any CMAT attendance was associated with a 16.8 percentage-point higher adjusted probability of BV rather than numeric failure (95\% CI 9.5 to 24.1 percentage points, $p<0.001$). By contrast, the corresponding adjusted difference for RT versus numeric failure was 0.5 percentage points (95\% CI $-15.1$ to 16.2 percentage points, $p=0.945$).

Within the positive attendance-frequency groups, the composition of non-PASS did not show a clear frequency gradient. The omnibus $p$-values were $0.495$ for any administrative outcome versus numeric failure, $0.599$ for BV/RT versus numeric failure and $0.523$ for BV versus numeric failure. No frequency-group pairwise comparison in these conditional analyses survived Holm adjustment. As with the performance outcomes, the principal observed separation was between zero attendance and any attendance rather than among the positive-frequency groups.

\subsection{Distributional and complete-case sensitivity}

The distributional analysis helps distinguish a broad shift in numeric performance from changes concentrated in the lower tail. Under the primary imputed outcome, the 10th and 25th percentiles were less negative in some higher-frequency groups while the 90th percentile changed comparatively little. The descriptive increase in mean $Z$ at higher attendance frequencies therefore did not appear to be driven principally by a small number of exceptionally high-performing students.

When the analysis was restricted to students with numeric final grades, however, medians and lower quartiles were substantially flatter across positive attendance frequencies. The complete-case omnibus test did not reject equal adjusted standardised performance across the positive-frequency groups ($p=0.397$). The difference between the imputed and numeric-only distributions indicates that the prevalence and composition of administrative outcomes contribute materially to the lower-tail pattern in the completed continuous outcome.

\subsection{Numeric failure among completed numeric grades}

The same broad zero-versus-positive distinction remained visible when administrative outcomes were removed entirely and the endpoint was restricted to completed numeric grades. The model-standardised probability of a numeric grade below 7.5 was 13.3\% for students with zero visits, compared with 3.3\%, 4.6\%, 3.6\%, 1.3\%, 3.7\% and 3.8\% for the 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ groups, respectively. The zero-inclusive fixed-effect logistic model rejected equal failure odds across the seven groups ($p<0.001$).

Among CMAT users alone, however, the corresponding adjusted failure probabilities were 2.5\%, 4.8\%, 5.3\%, 2.0\%, 6.6\% and 4.6\%, and the joint test did not reject equal adjusted log odds across positive attendance frequencies ($p=0.480$). No user-only pairwise odds-ratio comparison survived Holm adjustment. Thus the lower prevalence of completed numeric failure among users is not an artefact of assigning numerical values to administrative outcomes, but the numeric-only analysis provides no evidence of a regular failure gradient across repeated attendance frequencies.

Taken together, the binary PASS analysis and the numeric-failure analysis establish that the broad zero-versus-positive attendance separation does not depend on the imputed continuous outcome. PASS retains the full cohort without assigning latent grades to administrative outcomes, whereas the numeric-failure model excludes administrative outcomes entirely. The remaining distribution-shape analyses therefore address a narrower question: whether the apparent lower component is also present in observed numeric grades and how strongly the representation of administrative outcomes accentuates it.

\subsection{Instructor-level clustering sensitivity}

Re-clustering the covariance by instructor, while retaining instructor--period fixed effects and grade standardisation, left the zero-versus-positive benchmark essentially unchanged: any attendance was associated with a 0.361-standard-deviation higher continuous outcome (95\% CI 0.305 to 0.417) and a 15.3 percentage-point higher pass probability (95\% CI 12.8 to 17.8 percentage points). Among users there were 51 instructor clusters. Under this covariance specification, the continuous-outcome omnibus test was $p=0.0477$ and the pass-probability omnibus test was $p=0.0551$.

No continuous pairwise contrast survived Holm adjustment. For PASS, one non-adjacent contrast did: the 6+ group had an adjusted pass probability 12.0 percentage points higher than the one-visit group (95\% CI 4.3 to 19.8 percentage points; Holm-adjusted $p=0.0358$). No adjacent pass-probability contrast survived multiplicity adjustment. The instructor-level sensitivity therefore makes a broader low-versus-high frequency separation more plausible than under the primary covariance estimator, but it still does not support a visit-by-visit staircase.

\subsection{Distribution-shape analysis}

The numeric complete-case distributions were strongly non-Gaussian according to the parametric-bootstrap comparison of one versus two Gaussian components. With 199 bootstrap replicates, the bootstrap $p$-value was 0.005 in every attendance group except the five-visit group, for which it was 0.015. Model-selection criteria nevertheless disagreed about whether this departure from a single Gaussian should be represented as distinct mixture components. Among the six positive-frequency groups, BIC selected two components for 1, 2 and 5 visits and three components for 3, 4 and 6+ visits, whereas ICL selected one component for 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6+ visits and two components only for the five-visit group.

The two-component complete-case fits were also highly irregular across frequency. The estimated weight of the lower-mean component was 15.7\%, 13.0\%, 44.4\%, 25.4\%, 4.1\% and 14.8\% for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits, respectively. Ashman's $D$ was below 2 in five of the six positive-frequency groups; the exception was the small five-visit group ($n=49$), for which the lower component contained only about 4\% of observations. Repeating the fits on the imputed continuous outcome changed the corresponding lower-component weights to 25.2\%, 21.4\%, 21.2\%, 35.7\%, 9.1\% and 19.1\%, with the three-visit group showing the largest complete-case-to-imputed shift.

\Cref{fig:gmm-ridgeline} overlays the two weighted Gaussian components on the observed complete-case density, while \Cref{fig:lower-component-weight} compares the lower-component weight under complete-case and imputed specifications. A further specification check compared one Gaussian, one skew-normal and a two-Gaussian mixture on the same observations. For the imputed continuous outcome, the two-Gaussian mixture had lower BIC than the skew-normal in every frequency group; the BIC advantages were 1066.4, 77.3, 32.1, 28.1, 3.4, 12.4 and 11.4 for 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits. For the numeric complete-case outcome, the two-Gaussian model remained preferred for 0, 1 and 2 visits, with BIC advantages of 1223.3, 20.7 and 12.8, whereas a single skew-normal was preferred by BIC for 3, 4, 5 and 6+ visits.

The direct skew-normal-null bootstrap and repeated held-out prediction sharpened this distinction. For the numeric complete-case outcome, zero, one and two visits showed concordant evidence for the two-Gaussian model: the observed BIC advantage was more extreme than the bootstrap distribution generated from a fitted skew-normal, and the mixture also had higher mean held-out log predictive density. By contrast, the three-, four- and 6+ visit groups did not reject the skew-normal explanation and the skew-normal predicted held-out observations at least as well as the mixture. The five-visit group was not robustly classifiable: BIC and the skew-normal-null bootstrap did not require a mixture, while the cross-validation average favoured the mixture but did so inconsistently across folds in a sample of only 49 complete cases. We therefore treat this group as inconclusive rather than as evidence for a stable second numeric-grade component.

For the imputed continuous outcome, all seven attendance groups showed the opposite pattern: the observed two-Gaussian BIC advantage lay beyond the skew-normal-null bootstrap reference distribution, and the mixture had higher mean held-out log predictive density in every group. The agreement of in-sample penalised fit, simulation under a strongly skewed one-component null and out-of-sample prediction makes simple asymmetry an inadequate explanation of the imputed density structure.

Splitting the pooled 6+ category and re-estimating the full EM family for exact six and 7+ visits showed why the upper tail should remain a sensitivity rather than become the primary grouping. Among numeric complete cases, exact six visits were better described by a single skew-normal than by the two-Gaussian model on BIC ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=-2.36$), the skew-normal-null bootstrap did not reject that explanation ($p=0.11$), and mean held-out log predictive density also slightly favoured the skew-normal. For 7+ visits, BIC ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=+7.34$) and the bootstrap ($p=0.02$) favoured the two-Gaussian model, but the fitted lower component had only a 1.1\% weight with an estimated standard deviation at the numerical floor, indicating that the apparent component is effectively capturing an extreme observation rather than a stable lower-performance subgroup. The predictive diagnostic was correspondingly unstable despite a positive mean GMM-minus-skew log-density difference. On the imputed outcome, exact six visits remained diagnostically mixed, whereas the 7+ group showed more coherent support for the mixture ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=+4.56$, skew-normal-null bootstrap $p=0.02$, positive mean held-out log predictive difference). The imputed lower-component weights were 25.2\% for exact six and 14.3\% for 7+, again providing no monotone dose pattern. Supplementary \Cref{fig:tail-gmm-ridgeline,fig:tail-lower-component-weight} display these tail-resolution fits.

These comparisons also limit the interpretation that the apparent multi-component structure is merely an artefact of imputation. The complete-case analysis contains no BV, RT or BA values, yet the evidence for a mixture remains strong for zero, one and two visits. At higher positive frequencies, however, a single skewed numeric-grade distribution is generally adequate, so the data do not support a universal claim of two distinct numeric-grade populations. The imputed outcome produces a clearer multi-component pattern across all groups, indicating that administrative-outcome representation materially sharpens the lower component without providing the sole source of distributional complexity.

Posterior-responsibility composition of the imputed two-component model showed a strong connection between the lower density component and administrative final states. Aggregating CMAT users, approximately 55.5\% of the expected lower-component mass was administrative, including 49.5\% BV, 5.3\% RT and 0.7\% BA; the lower component also contained 34.4\% PASS and 10.1\% numeric non-PASS. The higher component was approximately 97.0\% PASS, 1.1\% numeric non-PASS and 2.0\% administrative. The clearer mixture structure in the imputed outcome is therefore closely associated with the representation of administrative outcomes, especially BV. This does not identify literal student classes, and neither component weights nor locations form a monotone sequence with visit frequency.
