# 6. I think Paper 2's eventual gap is starting to become clearer

I would not try to claim:

> “Nobody has studied whether mathematics support improves grades.”

That would be plainly false.

A lot of papers have.

Instead, I think our contribution can become something much more defensible:

```math
\boxed{ \text{How should the association between voluntary mathematics-support use and academic performance be characterized when exposure and outcome are defined within the same course-period context?} }
```

We then contribute several things simultaneously.

First, we restrict the study to **one common introductory mathematics course**, rather than pooling heterogeneous modules.

Second, exposure is reconstructed **only during the period in which that student was actually taking the course**.

Third, the academic outcome is not raw grade:

```math
Z_{i} = \frac{Y_i-\bar Y_{\text{professor}\times\text{course}\times\text{period}}} {s_{\text{professor}\times\text{course}\times\text{period}}}.
```

Fourth, we explicitly account for unequal variances through Welch/Games–Howell and classroom-clustered inference.

And fifth—and maybe most interesting—the observed relationship is **not a simple dose-response**.

What we currently see looks roughly like:

```math
E(Z\mid V=0) < E(Z\mid V>0),
```

but among positive users:

```math
E(Z\mid1\!-\!2) \approx E(Z\mid3) \approx E(Z\mid4+),
```

and the exact 1–7 visit analysis does not show a convincing monotonic trend.

That is scientifically more interesting than another:

> “students who attend tutoring get better grades.”

It suggests a distinction between **engagement/non-engagement** and a simplistic notion of tutoring \`\`dose.''

That is exactly where Byerley et al., Rylands & Shearman, Walsh & Guerin, Mullen, Jacob and the other literature becomes extremely useful.
